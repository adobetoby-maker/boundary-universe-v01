On Day 7 the Compact parse was in his queue before the shift bell, and the first thing Jonah understood about it, reading it standing up in Beam Control's analysis bay with his jacket still on, was that it was good.

He had wanted it to be careless. He had ridden the car down from the Ring two days ago composing the sentence he would be allowed to say, and somewhere under that sentence had been a smaller, less creditable hope: that the people preparing to call the source a weapon would do sloppy work, so that being right would be easy. The parse was not sloppy. It was the kind of work he would have been proud of at four in the morning with nobody watching.

The analysis bay was a borrowed corner of Beam Control — two consoles turned away from the operations floor, a wall surface that held whatever he pinned to it, a door that shut. His credential opened a working space on the response file that had not existed for him a week ago, and inside it the Compact analysts had laid out their case the way honest people lay out a case they expect to be attacked.

The structured vectors, they began, were the part of each packet that was not the error mask. Five weeks of them now, preserved from the held buffer, plus every packet since. Each block carried paired quantities that behaved like direction and magnitude — small, ordered, cumulative, the grammar of adjustment. Treat each block as a correction to a pointing solution, the analysts wrote, and the solutions walked. Packet after packet, the walk had a direction. Projected forward along the source's approach, the corrected solutions converged on a volume of space that contained, among other things, everywhere anyone lived.

They had flagged their own assumption. That was the part Jonah kept rereading. *Frame assumption: solution space held fixed relative to receiver.* They had written it at the top, in the place where you put the thing you most want a hostile reviewer to check, and then they had drawn the only conclusion their assumption allowed: progressive refinement, consistent with fire control. Not proven. Consistent with. They had used the honest verb every time.

He sat down. He made himself do the disagreeable thing first, which was to try to prove them right.

If the vectors were targeting corrections, there was a test that did not care what anyone hoped. A correction refines. Aim at a fixed thing, correct toward it, and the corrections shrink; the implied aim point holds still while the errors around it collapse. So he took the earliest preserved packet and computed the aim point its vectors implied, then took the next packet and computed it again, then the next. If the analysts were right, the points would gather.

They did not gather. They moved. Not scattering, which would have meant noise, and not collapsing, which would have meant a target. The implied aim point slid, packet to packet, along a smooth track, and the track had a familiar shape. He pulled the Day 4 result out of his own working space — the independent clock, the measured approach — and laid the source's motion against the drift of the aim points, and the two curves sat on each other like a hand in a glove.

The aim points were not moving. The frame was. Everything the vectors pointed at, they pointed at relative to something traveling with the source.

His hands were cold. He noticed it the way he noticed the coffee he had not drunk, distantly, as a fact about equipment. He built the transform the obvious way: subtract the measured motion, put the origin on the source, and re-plot every vector in the frame of the thing that was sending them.

In the source's own frame the vectors stopped pointing anywhere. They started connecting.

Tip to tail, block by block, each packet's vectors closed on themselves — not a walk toward inhabited space but a circuit, bending back to where it began. A ring. He plotted the second packet's set on top of the first and the circuit thickened rather than shifting. Third packet, fourth. Five weeks of packets, and every one of them drew the same figure in the same place in the moving frame, over and over, the way a hand traces a wound.

It was not a whole ring. There were breaks in it — spans where the vectors approached, stopped, and resumed on the far side, leaving gaps the circuit never crossed. He overlaid everything the buffer held, and the ring built up dense and dark where the packets repeated it, and the gaps stayed empty. Every packet knew where the gaps were. Every packet avoided the same ones.

He labeled the gaps the neutral way, letters, in order around the circuit, and wrote under them the only sentence the evidence supported: *persistent discontinuities, identical across all available packets.* Torn, said the part of him that had watched the aim points refuse to gather. He did not write torn.

There was more structure than the gaps. At intervals around the ring the vector density knotted — positions the packets returned to again and again, referenced from both directions, the way a maintenance log keeps returning to the same access points. The spacing looked regular. He would have said evenly spaced, except that at the resolution he had, the knots smeared, and he had been burned once already this week by a number he wanted. He marked the knots without counting them. For the briefing he needed a working label, and the closest thing in his own world was the hardware the Array used to shape its own fields, so he wrote: *recurring functional positions — provisional label: coil positions, by analogy with field hardware. Label only. Function not established.*

He sat back and looked at it. A ring that traveled with the source. Gaps that never closed. Marked positions that every packet consulted. For five weeks the discards had been read as damage descriptions of the next packet, and that had been true, and now the same grammar, transformed, described something else that was damaged — not the signal. The thing sending it. The vectors were not corrections converging on inhabited space. They were a shape, drawn in the sender's own frame, with the broken places marked.

He checked the constant block before he let himself believe any of it, because the constant block was where his believing had started, back on Day 0, and it owed him nothing. It was there. It was in every packet the buffer held and every packet since, unchanged — not nearly unchanged, unchanged — riding through frames that arrived with half their content eaten and this one block untouched. His notes still called it *checksum* because his notes had called it that since before he knew anything, and the label had gone on fitting it a little less each week without anything better offering itself. He wrote: *constant block: identical across all available packets; survives damage that corrupts adjacent structure; function unknown; working label retained for continuity.* It sat outside his ring like a signature on a document in a language nobody had, and he left it sitting there.

Then there was the block that would not close.

It appeared in the recent packets, one block among the ring-drawing blocks, and it broke the pattern in a specific way: its geometry belonged to the ring — it referenced a position on the circuit, adjacent to one of the gaps, tight against one of the marked positions — but its magnitudes did not point back into the figure. They pointed out. They were indexed, as nearly as he could establish, against the receive bands the source had been listening on since the beginning: the Array's own emission characteristics, the shape of the light the Array made. Every other block said *here is what is.* This block held a place open and described what could be put into it.

He tried to make it be something else. He tried it as a record of energy already received at that position — but the Array had never put energy anywhere near the source, and he could show that from the logs. He tried it as a warning, a marked hazard — but the structure ran the wrong way; hazards in the ring grammar were drawn as closed facts, and this was drawn as an open slot. He tried it as a description of a future state, the ring as it would be — but then the indexing against the Array's bands did nothing, and nothing else in five weeks was decoration. Each reading failed on evidence, not on preference, and he logged the failures, because the failures were the argument.

What survived was plain and he wrote it plainly: *one block constitutes an apparent request — that field energy, in the Array's emission bands, be placed at a specified position on the described structure. Requester unknown. Purpose unknown. Whether "request" is the correct category of act: unknown.*

He knew exactly what the block was worth to the people who would read the briefing. Without it, his analysis said: the vectors are self-description; nothing in them points at inhabited space; the targeting reading rests on a frame assumption the data rejects. Clean. With it, the analysis said all of that, and also: the object is asking the Array for energy. He could hear the sentence that would be built from it, and the voice that would build it. For one long moment he looked at the draft summary with the request block folded into a subsection where a tired reader might not weight it, and understood that he was capable of that, and moved it up to the top level with its own heading, because an analysis that only said what he wanted was just the Compact parse with the sign flipped.

The briefing needed one more line, and it was not his. Any response to the request — any response to anything — would spend Array power and make heat somewhere. He sent Quill a bounded request for thermal and material review. Her reply came back as a one-off assignment under operations authority: Sanz, this briefing only. Then, because he had learned on Day 5 that the wall was faster than he was, he did not wait for the assignment to introduce him. He took the slate and went down to the Thermal Gallery himself.

The Gallery met him the way it always did, heat first, then noise, then Teo — at the third loop's board, where a day hand was writing something slow and careful, the loop still wearing its recoverable flag like a patient allowed to sit up.

"You asked for a thermal review," Teo said, without turning around. "I saw the assignment land. And now you're here in person, which means either it's bad, or you've learned something about paperwork."

"I beat my own ticket down. That's growth."

"That's what it is." Teo signed off the day hand's entry and turned. He looked at the slate under Jonah's arm and then at Jonah's face, and whatever he read there took the next joke out of him. "Show me."

Jonah put the ring up on the board space Teo cleared — the overlay, five weeks deep, dark where it repeated, empty where it never went. The assignment attached only the ring and the request block. It was enough to ask what the energy would cost and no more, and for once he was glad of the limit.

Teo looked at it for a while. He looked at things the way he listened to pumps.

"Something built," he said at last. "Something built, with holes in it." He glanced at Jonah. "Am I allowed to say that?"

"You're allowed to say what it looks like. That's more than I wrote. I wrote *discontinuities.*"

"Of course you did." Teo touched one of the gaps, then one of the marked knots beside it. "These come back in every packet?"

"Every one. The Compact read the vectors as steering — as corrections converging on inhabited space — and their work is good, Teo. They flagged their own frame assumption. I tested it. In a fixed frame the aim points drift; in the source's frame this closes, packet after packet. It's repeatable. Anyone can run it."

"So it's not aiming. It's describing." Teo said it carefully, like a man stepping onto a surface he had been told would hold. "Describing itself."

"Describing something that travels with it and has these torn out of it." Jonah paused. "And one block that isn't description." He pulled the request block up beside the ring, with his three failed readings and the one that survived. Teo read all four. He read the failures twice.

"Field energy," Teo said. "In our bands. At that position." He straightened up, and Jonah watched him arrive at it, the place Jonah had been standing since mid-shift. "It's asking for a delivery."

"Something is. If asking is what this is. I can't get past *apparent request* and I've stopped trying to."

Teo was quiet for a moment. Behind him the third loop ticked through a valve cycle, and he registered it without looking, the way Jonah registered packet cadence.

"All right," Teo said. "Then I've got two questions, and they're the consult, so you're getting them in writing too. Question one. Say somebody with keys reads your briefing and decides the answer is yes — the Array puts field energy where that block asks. Where does it come from?" He nodded at the wall, in the direction of the whole scheduled world. "Every joule this station makes is spoken for. You've seen the sheet on the Ring; everybody's seen it. Cargo deferred, power windows moved, decks running reclaim slow — and that's just holding windows, that's the cost of *maybe.* Nothing in the schedule is labeled *spare.* So which line dies? Who was promised the energy this thing is asking for?"

"I don't know," Jonah said.

"Question two. Say it comes from somewhere. Energy delivered is heat owed — you make the light, you soak the waste here, radiators, water, the same loop that's been off nominal since Day 4. That's our end. Then there's the far end." He tapped the ring, the gap, the marked position hard against it. "You put energy into a torn thing moving that fast, the energy doesn't vanish because somebody asked nicely. It arrives. It becomes heat in a structure I've never seen a materials sheet for, at a position I can't inspect, with results nobody in this station can predict. Where does the heat go, Jonah? Both ends. Where does it go?"

There was a version of the next sentence where he reached for the ring model and rode it — *the marked positions suggest, the geometry implies* — and dressed the not-knowing in enough structure that it sounded like an answer. Six days ago he might have. He heard how good it would sound in the briefing.

"I don't know," he said. "Either end. I can't tell you what the requested energy would do when it arrived, because I don't know what it would be arriving into. I can tell you where the gaps are. I can't tell you what tore them, or what filling that slot costs, here or there. That's your discipline, and I'm not going to have learned it since mid-shift."

"No," Teo agreed, mildly. "You're not." But something in his shoulders had settled, and Jonah understood that the second question had also been a test, and that not knowing had been the passing answer. Teo pulled the consult ticket up and began to write, saying it aloud as he went, the way he did when he wanted a witness to the wording. "*Thermal note: any affirmative response to the apparent request implies an unbudgeted energy source and an unassessed heat disposition at both origin and destination. No current answer exists. Question is open and material.* Signed, Sanz." He looked up. "That goes in your pack whole. Not summarized."

"Whole," Jonah said. "It's the third heading. Analysis, request, cost."

"Cost unknown," Teo corrected. "Put the unknown in the heading. People read headings."

Jonah assembled the pack that evening at the console in the analysis bay: the frame test, written so that anyone with the held buffer could reproduce it; the ring with its lettered gaps and its provisional coil positions, labeled *label only*; the constant block, unchanged and unexplained; the request block, top level, with the failed readings attached; Teo's thermal note, whole, unknown in the heading. He read it once as himself and once as the person who would most want it to be wrong, and sent it up through the workflow, and the file logged it into the briefing set for Day 9.

The annotation came back before he had left the bay — inner-system link, minutes of light, a reader who did not sleep on things. It was flagged with Ward's response-command tag and it was four sentences long.

*Frame analysis noted. The test is repeatable and witnessed by method, which is the standard I asked for, and on present evidence the steering interpretation should not be briefed as favored. The request block stands: an inbound object carrying the energy this one carries, asking that more energy be placed upon it, is making a demand until the day it can safely be refused. Credit to the analyst for including it.*

He read it twice, looking for the unfair part, and did not find one. She had given up the word *targeting* the moment the evidence took it from her, which was more than most people gave up in an evening, and she had kept the word *demand,* and he could not show her wrong, because a request you cannot refuse was not distinguishable from one, and nobody could refuse this one yet — not with the schedule full and the heat unhoused and the held windows sitting grey on every wall the schedule fed.

He pinned the overlay to the bay wall before he left: the ring, dark where five weeks of packets agreed, empty where none of them would go, the marked positions waiting under a label that promised nothing. Something out there was drawn wrong and knew it, and knew where, and had asked the only light in reach to do something about it — asked, or demanded, or neither, some third category of act that no one alive on this side had a word for. On Day 9 he would stand up in front of the keys and say *it is describing itself* and be able to prove it, and then someone would ask him what it wanted, and he would have to say: energy, placed exactly there. And when they asked him what that would cost, he had Teo's answer, which was the true one, and it was open.

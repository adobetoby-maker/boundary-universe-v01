# Chapter 1 — The Kid in Room Four

Kade Mercer had been at Northline Alternative for forty-three school days, which was long enough to know that Room Four had three reliable clocks and none of them told time.

The first was the white plastic clock above the door. Its second hand twitched twice for every second and then froze whenever the heater kicked on.

The second was Ms. Alvarez's coffee. Full meant first period. Half meant lunch. Empty meant everyone should stop asking her questions unless someone was bleeding.

The third was Darius Bell.

At exactly 10:17 every morning, Darius would decide he had endured enough education for one day, stand up, announce a destination that was obviously false, and walk out.

Today he made it to 10:19.

“Bathroom,” Darius said.

Ms. Alvarez didn't look up from the district tablet in her hands. “You went fourteen minutes ago.”

“Different bathroom.”

“There is one bathroom.”

Darius considered that. “Then I forgot something in it.”

Across the room, Kade kept his eyes on his own tablet and moved his thumb over the blank answer field without touching it.

The question on-screen read:

**A cargo tram with a mass of 18,000 kilograms accelerates from rest to 12 meters per second over 8 seconds. What average force is required?**

It was a stupid question.

Not mathematically. Mathematically it was almost insulting.

The problem was the word *required*.

Required by what?

If the tram was on rails, friction mattered. If it was magnetic, the field geometry mattered. If twelve meters per second was measured relative to the track, fine, but if the track was moving—

Kade sighed and typed the answer they wanted.

**27,000 N**

Green check.

A little animation of a rocket ship appeared, congratulating him for mastering Newton's second law.

Kade hated the rocket ship.

“Mercer,” Ms. Alvarez said.

He looked up.

She pointed at Darius without moving anything but her eyes.

Darius had reached the door.

Kade looked at him. “You know she has to log it if you leave again.”

Darius's hand stopped on the push bar.

“Don't start,” Darius said.

“I didn't.”

“You got that face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you think you're smarter than everybody.”

Kade leaned back. “I don't have enough facial muscles for that.”

A laugh escaped from somewhere behind him. Darius didn't smile.

He was sixteen, two inches taller than Kade, and built like the district had accidentally enrolled a linebacker in remedial geometry. He had been suspended from Canyon Ridge High after putting another kid through the glass panel beside a classroom door. The other kid had needed eleven stitches. Darius had needed none.

Northline had taught Kade that none of those facts meant what people assumed they meant.

“I'm leaving,” Darius said.

“Okay.”

“You gonna stop me?”

“No.”

That seemed to irritate him more.

Kade turned his tablet around so Darius could see the upper corner of the screen.

A tiny red banner read:

**ATTENDANCE CONTRACT: 4/5 UNEXCUSED EXITS**

Darius stared at it.

“One more,” Kade said, “and Russell sends you back to review.”

“So?”

“So your review is Friday.”

Darius's jaw worked.

Kade knew about Friday because Darius had spent the entire previous afternoon pretending not to care whether his mother could get off work for the meeting.

“If they push it another week,” Kade said, “your mom misses another shift for nothing.”

Darius looked toward Ms. Alvarez.

She was still studying her tablet with the intensity of someone who had absolutely not heard the conversation.

“Sit down,” Kade said. “Walk out at lunch and call it rebellion.”

Darius glared at him for another two seconds, then returned to his chair.

“Still hate you,” he muttered.

“I know.”

Ms. Alvarez lifted her coffee and drank.

Still half full.

Not lunch yet.

Northline Alternative occupied an old county records building between a tire warehouse and a self-storage facility. Someone had painted the exterior blue three years earlier, but the desert sun had already bleached the south wall to a color best described as disappointed gray.

The district website called it a **personalized transitional learning campus**.

Students called it Northline.

Parents called it whatever they needed to call it when relatives asked where their kid went to school now.

There were forty-two students enrolled, divided across six classrooms. Some had been expelled. Some had been arrested. Some had stopped showing up at regular school until the district stopped pretending they would. A few had panic disorders, complicated families, pregnancies, probation officers, jobs, addictions, or combinations of all six.

Kade had none of those excuses.

That was, according to Vice Principal Russell, part of his problem.

At seventeen, Kade had collected three suspensions, two school transfers, a semester of failed classes he could have passed, and enough teacher comments to build a personality out of administrative adjectives.

**Bright but inconsistent.**

**Argumentative.**

**Fails to demonstrate appropriate investment.**

**Challenges authority.**

**Does not utilize available opportunities.**

His favorite was from ninth grade.

**Kade often appears disengaged even when he understands the material.**

As if understanding it should have made pretending to enjoy it easier.

At 10:31, the classroom speaker clicked.

Everyone looked up.

Northline's speaker system usually worked only when nobody wanted it to.

“Attention, students,” Russell's voice said. “All seventeen-year-old students scheduled for the National Aptitude Ascension Examination should report to Multipurpose Two at eleven hundred hours. Bring your district identification card. Testing begins promptly at eleven fifteen.”

Darius groaned.

“You're sixteen,” Kade said.

“I'm groaning in solidarity.”

“That's new.”

“I'm growing.”

The speaker clicked off.

Ms. Alvarez set down her tablet.

“Mercer.”

Kade looked at her.

“You have your ID?”

“Somewhere.”

“Find somewhere.”

He patted the pocket of his hoodie. The card was there.

“Miracle,” she said.

“You set your expectations correctly.”

A couple students laughed.

Ms. Alvarez did not.

That was unusual.

Kade watched her for a moment.

“You nervous?” he asked.

“For you?”

“For the national integrity of standardized testing.”

“No.”

“Good.”

“I am, however, concerned you'll decide the instructions are philosophically inconsistent and refuse to answer question twelve.”

“That happened once.”

“Twice.”

“The second one really was inconsistent.”

Her expression softened despite herself.

“Just take the test, Kade.”

He shrugged.

The Aptitude Ascension Examination had been around longer than Kade had been alive. Everyone took some version of it at seventeen. The official explanation was that it measured academic reasoning, spatial cognition, reaction speed, systems intuition, stress adaptation, and a dozen other things useful for university admissions, technical apprenticeships, military programs, and Resonance screening.

That last category was why people cared.

Thirty-one years after Resonance entered the world, civilization had reorganized itself around the tiny percentage of human beings who could do things everyone else could only watch.

They stopped collapsing buildings.

They redirected aircraft during emergencies.

They worked orbital construction.

They competed in arenas watched by hundreds of millions.

They wore Conduits worth more than Northline's entire building.

And every year a few teenagers walked into testing centers as ordinary kids and walked out with numbers that changed their lives.

Kade didn't expect to be one of them.

He had learned to distrust any institution promising to reveal what he was capable of.

Institutions had been explaining what he was capable of for years.

Mostly they seemed disappointed.

At 10:56, Ms. Alvarez stopped him at the classroom door.

The others filed past.

“Kade.”

He turned.

She leaned against the teacher's desk, arms folded.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That's usually not why people say my name like that.”

She studied him.

“You know what I think your problem is?”

“Alphabetical or ranked?”

“You decide something doesn't matter before you find out whether you're good at it.”

His smile stayed in place.

“Efficient.”

“No.”

That single word landed harder than he expected.

Ms. Alvarez nodded toward the hallway.

“Go take the test.”

He started to leave.

“And Kade?”

He looked back.

“Try.”

He almost made a joke.

For some reason, he didn't.

Multipurpose Two had once been the county archive room. The shelves were gone, replaced by twenty testing stations divided by collapsible privacy panels. Eleven students from Northline sat in assigned seats while two district proctors checked identification cards.

Kade took Station Seven.

A sensor band rested beside the tablet.

He picked it up.

“Temple contact,” the proctor said.

“I've used one.”

“Then this should go beautifully.”

Kade looked at him.

The proctor smiled.

Kade decided he liked him.

He fitted the band around his head. Cool contacts settled against his temples and behind his ears.

The screen illuminated.

**NATIONAL APTITUDE ASCENSION EXAMINATION**

**Candidate: MERCER, KADE A.**

**Age: 17**

**Identity verified.**

A countdown appeared.

The first section was ordinary enough.

Pattern recognition.

Language.

Mathematics.

Kade moved quickly.

He had always been good at tests when he cared enough to finish them.

Section four switched to dynamic systems.

A simulated bridge oscillated in crosswind. He had to choose where to add damping.

Easy.

A water network ruptured. He had to isolate the failure while preserving pressure to a hospital district.

More interesting.

A transport grid lost three substations during an evacuation. He had ninety seconds to reroute power and traffic simultaneously.

He forgot to be bored.

The questions stopped feeling like school.

For the first time all day, the test seemed to be asking what he actually saw when he looked at a problem.

Not whether he remembered the equation.

Where would it break?

What moved first?

What could be allowed to fail so the rest survived?

Kade leaned closer to the screen.

At some point, he became aware that everyone else had gone to lunch.

Then section seven began.

The instructions were only one sentence.

**Identify the boundary of the active system.**

A diagram appeared.

A steel sphere hung from a cable inside a transparent chamber. The chamber sat on a platform. The platform rested on a larger floor grid. Arrows indicated forces.

Kade frowned.

There was no question.

He waited.

Nothing changed.

He tapped the sphere.

The tablet chimed.

**Incomplete.**

He tapped the chamber.

**Incomplete.**

The platform.

**Incomplete.**

Kade looked at the diagram again.

“That's not a system,” he whispered.

The proctor glanced over.

Kade ignored him.

The diagram was wrong in the way textbook diagrams were always wrong. It wanted him to pretend edges were real because somebody had drawn lines around them.

The cable attached to the chamber ceiling. The chamber transferred load into the platform. The platform transferred force into the floor. The floor into the foundation. The foundation into the earth.

And if the sphere moved, energy went into everything.

He dragged his finger outward.

A selection box expanded around the sphere, cable, chamber and platform.

The screen flickered.

He expanded it again to include the floor grid.

Another flicker.

Then the interface changed.

The selection boundary no longer snapped to objects.

Kade sat up.

He could draw it anywhere.

So he did.

Not around the equipment.

Through it.

He bisected the platform diagonally, excluding one support and including the other. Then he ran the boundary up the wall, across the cable, around half the sphere, and back through open air.

The screen went black.

Kade froze.

Then white text appeared.

**REDEFINE.**

His pulse jumped.

“What?”

The word vanished.

A new simulation loaded.

A vehicle struck a barrier.

**Minimize occupant impulse.**

Kade selected the obvious crumple zones.

The simulation scored him at eighty-three percent.

He reset it.

This time he included the barrier.

Ninety-one.

Again.

He included a section of roadway.

Ninety-six.

Again.

He excluded the vehicle's rear axle from the active transfer network.

The screen hesitated.

Then:

**99.98%**

Kade stared.

A pressure began behind his eyes.

Not pain exactly.

More like the sensation of remembering a word he had never learned.

The next problem appeared before he touched anything.

Then another.

Then another.

His hands moved faster.

Forces. Heat. Charge. Fluid flow. Collisions.

Each problem asked the same hidden question in different clothes.

Where did the thing end?

And each time Kade's answer was increasingly the same.

It ended wherever you decided to stop counting.

At 12:42, the final simulation disappeared.

Kade blinked at his reflection in the black screen.

His temples were damp beneath the sensor band.

The room was silent.

One proctor stood near the door.

The other was staring at Kade's station.

“Done?” Kade asked.

The proctor looked at the wall clock.

Then at him.

“Apparently.”

“Good talk.”

Kade removed the band.

A progress icon appeared.

**SCORING…**

He stretched his fingers.

The door opened and Darius leaned in.

“You alive?”

“Debatable.”

“Food's gone.”

“Tragedy.”

“What'd you get?”

“It's scoring.”

Darius came farther into the room despite the proctor's expression.

“You think you're Resonant?”

“No.”

“That sounded fast.”

“Because I've had seventeen years to think about it.”

“You'd be annoying as hell with powers.”

“That's fair.”

The scoring icon disappeared.

Kade looked at the screen.

For a moment he thought it hadn't loaded correctly.

The page was almost empty.

Then three digits appeared in the center.

**000**

Darius made a sound halfway between a cough and a laugh.

Kade stared at the number.

“Wow,” Darius said.

Kade nodded slowly.

“Honestly, kind of impressive.”

“You got zero.”

“I noticed.”

“Like zero zero.”

“There are three of them. It's a strong commitment.”

Darius laughed outright now.

Kade smiled because that was what you did when the universe managed to summarize your academic career in three identical digits.

He waited for embarrassment.

It came, but softer than expected.

Mostly he felt annoyed.

Of course.

Of course the grand national machine designed to locate hidden potential had looked inside Kade Mercer and returned nothing.

He reached for the sensor band.

The lights went out.

Not dimmed.

Out.

The ventilation stopped.

Every tablet in Multipurpose Two turned black at the exact same instant.

Darius's laugh died.

Emergency lights should have come on.

They didn't.

For two seconds the room was completely dark.

Then every testing screen illuminated white.

No logos.

No controls.

Just a single line of black text.

**SESSION TERMINATED**

The proctor swore.

His wrist terminal began vibrating.

Then the other proctor's.

Then Darius's phone.

Then Kade's.

All at once.

In the hallway, someone shouted.

The wall display rebooted and showed the district network map.

Red indicators multiplied across it.

Northline.

Then the city.

Then the state.

The map zoomed out automatically.

More red.

Kade stopped smiling.

The entire national testing network was going dark.

One region after another.

The proctor looked from the wall display to Kade's station.

Kade followed his eyes.

His screen was the only one still showing a score.

**000**

The proctor took one step backward.

And for the first time that day, Kade had the distinct feeling that somebody else was more afraid of his test result than he was.

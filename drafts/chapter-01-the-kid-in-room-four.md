# Chapter 1 — The Kid in Room Four

The first thing Kade Mercer learned at Northline Alternative was that every room had two names.

The name on the door.

And the name the students used.

Room One was Intake.

Room Two was The Box.

Room Three was Probation Lite.

Room Four, where Kade spent most mornings, was simply Four.

Nobody had bothered to improve it.

That felt appropriate.

“Bathroom,” Darius Bell announced.

Ms. Alvarez looked at the clock.

“Denied.”

Darius frowned. “You can't deny a bathroom.”

“I denied your destination. Your bladder remains constitutionally protected.”

A few people laughed.

Kade did not look up from his tablet.

Darius walked toward the door anyway.

“Friday,” Kade said.

Darius stopped.

Kade kept reading the problem on his screen.

A cargo tram. Eighteen thousand kilograms. Twelve meters per second. Eight seconds.

The program wanted force.

Of course it did.

School loved answers that came out clean.

Darius turned. “What about Friday?”

“Your review.”

“Mind your business.”

“You made it my business when you started standing between me and the only exit.”

Darius was big enough that most people treated his moods like weather. Kade had learned not to.

Weather was random.

Darius was not.

“One more walkout,” Kade said, “and Russell postpones your review.”

Darius stared at him.

Kade finally looked up.

“Your mom switched shifts for Friday.”

That did it.

The anger stayed on Darius's face, but the decision went out of it.

He muttered something unkind and went back to his chair.

Kade returned to the tram.

He typed **27,000 newtons**.

The screen rewarded him with a small animated rocket.

Kade closed it.

Northline's educational software believed congratulations should be colorful.

Northline itself did not.

The school occupied an old government records building behind a tire warehouse. The halls were narrow. The windows were reinforced. Every classroom had a panic button, two cameras, and furniture heavy enough that throwing it required commitment.

Forty-two students attended.

Some were there because they had fought.

Some because they had stopped attending anywhere else.

Some because regular classrooms were too loud, too crowded, too fast, too slow, too rigid, too forgiving, or simply too full of people who had already decided who they were.

Kade had been there forty-three days.

His file said he was bright.

His file also said he was oppositional.

Those two words had followed him through three schools like a matching set.

**Bright but oppositional.**

**High potential, poor compliance.**

**Capable of grade-level work, refuses sustained effort.**

Kade's favorite was:

**Frequently challenges the framing of assignments rather than completing them as directed.**

He considered that one accurate.

The tram problem, for example, was badly framed.

It asked how much force was required without saying anything useful about the track, drag, friction, energy losses, or whether the stated acceleration was measured relative to the planet or the rail bed.

The answer was still twenty-seven thousand newtons.

That was the irritating part.

At 10:31, the overhead speaker crackled.

Everyone looked up.

Northline's intercom usually sounded like someone speaking through a bucket from another building.

Vice Principal Russell's voice arrived in pieces.

“All seventeen-year-old students scheduled for the National Aptitude Ascension Examination report to Multipurpose Two at eleven hundred. Bring district identification. Testing begins at eleven fifteen.”

A groan moved around the room.

Darius contributed despite being sixteen.

Kade looked at him.

“What?” Darius said. “I can hate tests early.”

Ms. Alvarez stood and picked up her coffee.

“Mercer.”

Kade held up his ID card before she could ask.

She nodded once.

“I'm touched by your faith in me.”

“I have faith in your ability to lose things. That's different.”

Kade put the card away.

Nobody at Northline needed an explanation of the examination.

Everybody knew.

The AAE was one of those things that had existed long enough to feel inevitable.

Every seventeen-year-old took it.

Universities used it.

Military programs used it.

Technical academies used it.

Most importantly, Resonance programs used it.

That was the part parents pretended not to care about while caring enormously.

Thirty-one years ago, humanity had discovered that a small number of people could interact with physical forces through a neural interface called a Conduit.

Kade had watched enough clips to know the public explanation and the practical difference were not the same thing.

The public explanation used words like *field stabilization* and *controlled transfer*.

The clips showed people doing impossible things without calling them impossible.

A woman redirecting debris from a collapsing bridge.

A rescue worker taking the momentum of a falling steel beam and bleeding it into the ground.

Arena competitors crossing twenty meters before the camera operator adjusted.

The best students went to academies whose names carried more weight than most universities.

Asterion.

Kiyomizu.

Helix.

Kade had never put himself in that sentence.

He wasn't stupid enough to confuse daydreaming with planning.

At 10:54, students began drifting toward the testing room.

Kade waited until the others had left.

Ms. Alvarez was erasing the board.

“Mercer.”

He paused in the doorway.

She had his attention, which meant she took a second before using it.

“Do one thing for me.”

“That depends heavily on the thing.”

“Take the test like the result matters.”

Kade smiled.

There it was.

Adults loved telling him things mattered.

Grades mattered.

Attendance mattered.

Behavior contracts mattered.

Deadlines mattered.

Then, when those things went badly, the same adults explained that one grade, one suspension, one semester, one bad year did not define him.

Apparently things mattered right up until they needed them not to.

“I'll answer the questions,” he said.

“That's not what I asked.”

Kade leaned against the doorframe.

Ms. Alvarez set down the eraser.

“You do this thing,” she said.

“What thing?”

“You decide the game is stupid before anyone can tell you that you lost.”

Kade's smile stayed exactly where it was.

“That sounds efficient.”

“It sounds lonely.”

He stopped smiling.

Only for a second.

Then he pushed away from the door.

“Eleven fifteen, right?”

“Kade.”

He kept walking.

---

Multipurpose Two had twenty testing stations and no multipurpose qualities Kade could identify.

A district proctor checked his ID.

“Station Seven.”

Kade sat.

A sensor band rested beside the tablet.

He fitted it around his head.

Cold contacts touched his temples.

The screen brightened.

**NATIONAL APTITUDE ASCENSION EXAMINATION**

**MERCER, KADE A.**

**IDENTITY VERIFIED**

A female synthetic voice spoke through the station earpiece.

“Remain seated until your assessment is complete. Follow all prompts. Do not remove the neural sensor band unless directed.”

Kade glanced at the proctor.

“Do people usually sprint away during spatial reasoning?”

The proctor pointed at the screen.

“Begin.”

The first section was easy.

The second was boring.

The third was easy and boring.

By section four, things improved.

A bridge oscillated under wind load.

Kade had to choose where to damp it.

A power grid lost three substations during an evacuation.

Kade had to decide which neighborhoods went dark.

A hospital water system ruptured.

He had to isolate the failure without starving critical care.

There were no perfect answers.

Kade liked that.

The exam kept adjusting.

Questions became less like school and more like arguments.

Every answer changed the next problem.

Every shortcut closed off another option.

At some point, Kade forgot he had planned not to care.

He leaned forward.

His left foot stopped bouncing.

His mouth went still.

He worked.

At 12:17, section seven loaded.

A steel sphere hung from a cable inside a transparent chamber.

The chamber rested on a platform.

The platform rested on a floor grid.

The prompt read:

**IDENTIFY THE ACTIVE SYSTEM.**

Kade touched the sphere.

A tone sounded.

“Wrong,” the station voice said.

He touched the sphere and cable.

“Wrong.”

The chamber.

“Wrong.”

The platform.

“Wrong.”

Kade looked around.

The other students were still working.

He looked back at the screen.

“What do you mean, wrong?”

No response.

He selected everything in the diagram.

A pause.

“Wrong.”

Kade sat back.

The problem irritated him in a way the others had not.

Not because he couldn't solve it.

Because the exam was pretending the answer existed.

A steel sphere hanging from a cable was not a closed system.

The cable transferred load into the chamber.

The chamber transferred load into the platform.

The platform into the floor.

The floor into the building.

The building into the foundation.

The foundation into the earth.

Where exactly did the test want him to stop?

Kade touched the boundary tool.

A blue outline appeared around the sphere.

He dragged it outward.

Cable.

Chamber.

Platform.

Floor.

The line suddenly stopped snapping to object edges.

Kade's finger froze.

He moved it left.

The boundary cut straight through the platform.

That should not have been allowed.

He dragged farther.

The line passed through the chamber wall.

He drew it around half the sphere.

The screen flickered.

Then every label disappeared.

One word appeared.

**CONTINUE.**

Kade stared.

The next simulation loaded.

A passenger vehicle approached a barrier.

**MINIMIZE OCCUPANT IMPULSE.**

He selected the vehicle's crumple zone.

The score appeared.

**82.6%**

He reset.

Included the chassis.

**87.1%**

Again.

Included the barrier.

**92.4%**

Again.

Included the roadway.

**96.8%**

Kade's heartbeat picked up.

He redrew the boundary.

This time he excluded the rear axle.

The station went silent.

The room around him seemed suddenly very far away.

Then:

**99.98%**

Kade whispered, “That's not how that works.”

The next problem appeared immediately.

Heat.

Then pressure.

Then electricity.

Then moving masses.

Each time the test asked one thing and rewarded another.

It asked him to solve the problem.

It rewarded him for deciding what the problem included.

A pressure formed behind his eyes.

He ignored it.

A tremor passed through his right hand.

He flexed his fingers.

The test kept going.

By the time the last simulation vanished, the room was mostly empty.

The wall clock read 1:04.

Kade removed the sensor band.

The screen displayed:

**SCORING…**

He exhaled.

His shoulders hurt.

He had not realized he had been holding them tense.

The door cracked open.

Darius looked in.

“You still doing this?”

“Apparently I'm gifted at endurance.”

“Everybody else finished forever ago.”

“Good for everybody else.”

Darius walked in.

“You get a Resonance score yet?”

“No.”

“What if you're like a seven hundred?”

“Then I become unbearable.”

“You're already unbearable.”

“Then I save time.”

The scoring icon disappeared.

Three digits appeared.

Kade read them.

Then read them again.

**000**

Darius leaned over his shoulder.

“No.”

Kade did not move.

Darius laughed.

Not meanly at first.

Then harder.

“Zero?”

Kade nodded.

“Like actually zero?”

“Looks professionally rendered.”

“You got the lowest score possible.”

“I assume somebody worked very hard on the scale.”

Darius laughed again.

Kade joined him.

It was funny.

It had to be.

That was the rule.

If you laughed first, nobody could use it on you.

He looked at the three zeros.

Somewhere behind the joke, something inside him folded quietly shut.

Ms. Alvarez had asked him to take the test like the result mattered.

Fine.

He had.

And there it was.

Zero.

Clean.

Simple.

Almost elegant.

Kade reached for the sensor band.

The lights died.

The room went completely black.

Darius stopped laughing.

The ventilation shut off.

Emergency lighting failed to appear.

For one long second, the only sound was somebody swearing in the hall.

Then every testing tablet turned white.

The proctor ran toward the wall console.

His wrist terminal started vibrating.

Darius pulled out his phone.

“No signal.”

Kade's screen changed.

The score remained in the center.

**000**

Around it, lines of system text began appearing.

Not test results.

Errors.

**SESSION TERMINATED**

**REMOTE VERIFICATION FAILED**

**NETWORK CASCADE**

The wall display rebooted.

A map of California appeared.

Testing centers blinked red one after another.

Then the map zoomed out.

Nevada.

Oregon.

Arizona.

The country.

Thousands of centers went red.

Someone in the hallway yelled that the AAE was down nationwide.

The proctor turned toward Kade.

Kade expected irritation.

He expected accusation.

Instead, the man looked frightened.

Not at the network.

At the score.

Kade followed his gaze to the three zeros glowing in the middle of the screen.

He had spent most of his life learning that test scores were only numbers.

For the first time, he wondered if a number could be a warning.

# Chapter 1 — The Kid in Room Four

At 12:41 p.m., the National Aptitude Ascension Examination asked Kade Mercer a question it was not supposed to ask.

He knew that because the question did not have an answer box.

It had no diagram label. No countdown. No multiple-choice options. No cheerful federal seal in the corner reminding him that assessment integrity was a shared civic responsibility.

Only six words:

**WHERE DOES THE SYSTEM ACTUALLY END?**

Kade stared at them.

“Cute,” he whispered.

The testing room did not answer.

Eleven stations occupied what Northline Alternative called Multipurpose Two and what the county had once called Records Storage B. The original fluorescent fixtures still buzzed overhead. Someone had painted the concrete walls beige, perhaps in the belief that beige made teenagers less likely to punch one another.

It had not worked.

Kade glanced toward the proctor.

Mr. Hsu sat by the door reading something on his wrist display. He had not noticed the strange prompt.

Kade raised a hand.

Nothing.

He raised it higher.

Mr. Hsu finally looked up.

“Problem?”

“Possibly philosophical.”

Mr. Hsu's eyes narrowed.

Kade turned the tablet toward him.

The strange question vanished.

In its place was a conventional mechanics simulation showing a suspended steel sphere inside a transparent chamber.

Mr. Hsu looked at Kade.

Kade looked at the screen.

“Never mind.”

“Mercer.”

“It's fixed.”

“Do not leave your station.”

“I've survived seventeen years without wandering into federal standardized testing traffic. I think we're good.”

Mr. Hsu did not smile.

Kade returned to the screen.

The official prompt now read:

**Identify the active system.**

That was almost worse.

Kade touched the sphere.

**INCOMPLETE.**

The cable.

**INCOMPLETE.**

The chamber.

**INCOMPLETE.**

He exhaled through his nose.

“You're asking the wrong question,” he murmured.

The screen flickered.

For less than a second, the six words returned.

**WHERE DOES THE SYSTEM ACTUALLY END?**

Kade stopped breathing.

Then the ordinary prompt came back.

He looked around the room again.

No one else remained at a testing station. The other Northline students had finished nearly an hour earlier and gone to lunch. Mr. Hsu's partner had stepped outside to take a call.

Kade was alone with the exam.

He should have called the proctor again.

Instead, he touched the boundary tool.

A blue box appeared around the sphere.

He dragged it outward to include the cable and chamber.

**INCOMPLETE.**

He included the platform.

**INCOMPLETE.**

The floor.

The display paused.

Then the blue boundary stopped snapping to object edges.

Kade frowned.

He dragged one corner diagonally through the platform itself.

The line passed cleanly through steel.

No error.

He moved the top edge through the chamber wall, cut around the sphere, excluded half the cable, then extended the lower boundary into the floor grid.

The simulation recalculated.

Numbers flooded the side panel too fast to read.

Then the screen went black.

White text appeared.

**CANDIDATE RESPONSE ACCEPTED.**

A second line followed.

**CONTINUE.**

Kade sat back.

The pressure behind his eyes had started twenty minutes earlier. Now it sharpened.

He thought of Ms. Alvarez telling him, before he came down here, to try.

He had almost laughed at her.

That had been at 10:56.

Two hours earlier, when the strangest thing about his day had been Darius Bell attempting to escape Room Four.

---

Northline Alternative occupied a converted county records building between a tire warehouse and six acres of storage units.

The district brochure described the school as a **student-centered transitional academic environment**.

Kade had once suggested they shorten that to **the place they send you after the regular place is tired of you**.

Vice Principal Russell had not used the suggestion.

Room Four held eight students that morning.

At 10:18, Darius Bell stood up.

“Bathroom.”

Ms. Alvarez kept typing. “No.”

Darius stared at her.

“That's not really how bathrooms work.”

“You went sixteen minutes ago.”

“Maybe I have a condition.”

“You do.”

Darius waited.

Ms. Alvarez finally looked up.

“It's called leaving class.”

Two students laughed.

Darius headed for the door anyway.

Kade watched him pass.

“Friday,” he said.

Darius stopped.

“What?”

“Your review meeting.”

“Mind your business.”

“I am. You're blocking the door.”

Darius's shoulders shifted.

Most people interpreted that movement as a warning.

Kade interpreted it as a question.

Darius was bigger than him and angry more often, but anger at Northline was rarely about the thing directly in front of you.

Kade nodded toward the attendance display.

“One more unauthorized exit and they postpone your review.”

“So?”

“Your mom already changed shifts for Friday.”

Darius's expression changed so little that anyone who didn't know him would have missed it.

Kade knew him.

Forty-three days at Northline was enough to know where most people kept the things they pretended not to care about.

Darius looked at Ms. Alvarez.

She returned to her tablet.

He sat down.

“Still hate you,” he said.

“Get in line.”

Kade turned back to his own lesson.

A cargo tram accelerated from rest.

The program wanted force.

Kade wanted to know whether the tram was magnetic, whether the track was level, what losses were being ignored and why a national education contractor couldn't afford a simulation with enough information to ask the question honestly.

He entered **27,000 N** anyway.

A cartoon rocket celebrated him.

Kade closed the animation before it finished.

He had been at three high schools in three years.

His records explained why.

**Defiance.**

**Academic noncompliance.**

**Disruptive questioning.**

**Failure to complete assigned work despite demonstrated ability.**

That last one appeared frequently.

Kade's mother kept every report in a folder at home, as if enough administrative paperwork might eventually produce the instruction manual everybody seemed convinced she had misplaced when he was born.

His father had never needed an instruction manual.

At least that was how Kade remembered him.

Aaron Mercer had died in an industrial accident when Kade was small enough that memory and photographs had become impossible to separate.

He remembered his father's hands.

Or perhaps he remembered a photograph of them.

He remembered being lifted onto a kitchen counter.

Or maybe his mother had told the story enough times that he had built the image himself.

The reliable facts were simpler.

His father was dead.

His mother worked too much.

Kade made things harder.

Nobody said the third one directly anymore.

They didn't have to.

At 10:31, the speaker crackled.

“All seventeen-year-old students scheduled for the National Aptitude Ascension Examination report to Multipurpose Two at eleven hundred hours.”

Darius groaned theatrically.

“You're sixteen,” Kade said.

“I'm opposed on principle.”

“To aptitude?”

“To examinations.”

“Brave.”

Everyone knew what the examination could mean.

Most of it was ordinary placement nonsense—university admissions, apprenticeships, service tracks, military eligibility.

Then there was Resonance.

Thirty-one years earlier, Resonance had turned from classified physics into the most important discovery in modern history.

A minority of human beings could interact with physical systems through implanted Conduits. The public language around it was careful: field manipulation, controlled transfer, assisted redirection.

The videos were less careful.

A woman stopping a collapsing tower long enough for forty people to escape.

A construction specialist moving structural loads through temporary supports that should have failed.

Combat athletes crossing an arena faster than Kade could track.

The students selected for elite Resonance programs did not merely get scholarships.

They got futures with names.

Asterion.

Kiyomizu.

Helix.

Places normal people talked about the way earlier generations had talked about professional sports and astronaut corps combined.

Kade had never seriously imagined himself at one.

Hope required maintenance.

He preferred things that worked unattended.

At 10:56, Ms. Alvarez stopped him on the way out.

“Your ID.”

He pulled it from his hoodie pocket.

She seemed disappointed that he had it.

“Anything else?” Kade asked.

“Yes.”

He waited.

“Don't sabotage this.”

His smile came automatically.

“That's a dramatic way to describe standardized testing.”

“I'm not talking about the test.”

That irritated him.

Mostly because he understood her.

“You think I care enough to sabotage it?”

“I think you care enough to make sure nobody can prove you tried.”

The hallway noise seemed to dim.

Kade looked at her.

Ms. Alvarez wasn't smiling.

“You know what's convenient about not trying?” she said. “Nothing that happens afterward counts.”

He slipped the ID back into his pocket.

“Thanks, Dr. Alvarez.”

“Go.”

He went.

---

The first six sections of the exam were exactly what Kade expected.

Reasoning.

Memory.

Spatial rotation.

Reaction time.

Applied math.

Systems analysis.

He moved quickly, then slower, then quickly again when the questions became less interested in what he had memorized and more interested in how he chose between bad options.

A bridge oscillated in crosswind.

A hospital grid lost power.

A flood-control system failed in three places simultaneously.

Those were better.

He forgot to watch the clock.

Section seven began at 12:19.

By 12:41, the exam asked where the system actually ended.

Now, after accepting his impossible boundary, it presented a new simulation.

A passenger pod moving at eighty kilometers per hour.

A rigid barrier.

One command:

**MINIMIZE OCCUPANT IMPULSE.**

Kade selected the front crumple structure.

**81.4%**

He included the chassis.

**86.0%**

He included the barrier.

The simulation hesitated.

**91.7%**

Kade leaned closer.

The barrier was anchored to the roadway.

He extended the system down.

**96.2%**

He stared at the vehicle.

“Okay,” he whispered. “You're cheating.”

The exam did not disagree.

Kade redrew the active boundary so the rear axle sat outside the modeled transfer system.

The display froze.

Mr. Hsu looked up.

Kade waited.

Then:

**99.98%**

A sensation moved through his skull like cold water behind his eyes.

He flinched.

The next simulation appeared immediately.

Heat transfer.

Then fluid pressure.

Then electrical load.

Then collision chains.

Kade no longer knew whether he was taking the exam correctly.

He was certain he had stopped taking the exam everyone else took.

Each problem rewarded the same behavior.

Stop accepting the boundaries you were given.

Ask what touched what.

Ask where the cost went.

Ask what happened if the thing everyone called *outside* became part of the problem.

At 1:07, the final simulation vanished.

The screen displayed:

**SCORING.**

Kade removed the sensor band.

His hands trembled once, barely enough to notice.

Mr. Hsu approached.

“You were in adaptive testing for forty-eight minutes.”

“Is that bad?”

“I don't know.”

That was the first answer Kade had heard from an adult all day that sounded completely honest.

The door opened.

Darius leaned through.

“Mercer. You done?”

“Maybe.”

“Everybody else has scores.”

“Congratulations to everybody else.”

Darius walked in.

Mr. Hsu started to object, then his wrist display chimed.

He glanced down.

Kade watched his face tighten.

“What?”

Mr. Hsu didn't answer.

The scoring indicator disappeared.

Three digits formed in the center of Kade's screen.

**000**

There was a half second of silence.

Then Darius barked a laugh.

“No way.”

Kade stared.

“That's actually incredible,” Darius said.

“I like the symmetry.”

“Zero?”

“Three zeros. Show some respect.”

“You broke the test.”

“Apparently the test broke me first.”

Kade smiled.

It was what he did.

He smiled when a principal told him he was wasting his ability.

He smiled when his mother tried not to look disappointed at a conference table.

He smiled when teachers told him he could do anything if he applied himself, which somehow always sounded like an accusation.

So he smiled at **000**.

And decided, immediately, that it didn't matter.

Then Mr. Hsu said, “Don't touch anything.”

Kade looked over.

The proctor had gone pale.

“What?”

Mr. Hsu stared at the screen.

His wrist display began vibrating.

A tone sounded from the wall terminal.

Then another.

The lights blinked once.

Twice.

Every testing station rebooted simultaneously.

Darius's phone lost signal.

The wall display flashed a regional map covered in red status markers.

Kade stood.

Mr. Hsu snapped, “Sit down.”

“Why?”

“I said sit down.”

“That's not an answer.”

The regional map zoomed outward on its own.

California.

Western states.

The whole country.

Red spread across the testing network like blood through water.

One center after another dropped offline.

Thousands.

Then tens of thousands.

A system banner appeared:

**NATIONAL AAE NETWORK FAILURE**

**CAUSE UNKNOWN**

Mr. Hsu looked at Kade's tablet again.

The score remained.

**000**

For a strange second nobody spoke.

Kade looked from the nationwide outage to his score.

Then back.

He felt the beginning of a joke rise automatically to his mouth.

This time it died before he could say it.

Because Mr. Hsu was not looking at the wall display anymore.

He was looking at Kade.

Not annoyed.

Not confused.

Afraid.

And Kade Mercer, who had spent years learning how little a number on a school screen could mean, suddenly wanted very badly to know what zero meant.

# Chapter Two: LOAD-BEARING

## Day 4,369

The eastern wall of the Léman Materials Assurance Annex carried three floors, half a roof, and an institutional preference for not discussing the basement.

Aaron learned this from a woman standing on a scaffold with a hammer.

She struck the exposed masonry once. Dust released from a mortar joint and crossed the beam of her work light. The sound returned flat from the brick pier, sharper from the limestone surround, and nearly absent from the section covered by gray acoustic board.

“That part carries the stairs,” she said. “That part carries the roof truss. This part carries whatever your drawings forgot to admit exists below us.”

Aaron stood behind the yellow barrier at ground level, wearing a white visitor helmet over equipment that could identify the output of a damaged nonhuman intelligence at four hundred meters. The helmet fit less accurately.

“The drawings identify a service gallery,” he said.

“The drawings identify a rectangle labeled nonstructural void.” She turned on the scaffold deck. Her helmet carried a strip of green tape with CASAUS written in black marker. “Voids don’t usually need ventilation, drainage, two protected electrical feeds, and a floor rated for nine kilonewtons per square meter.”

Behind Aaron, Annex Manager Luc Barre stopped arranging printed site plans into a defensible stack.

“The gallery contains protected instruments,” Aaron said. “Its function falls outside the structural brief. Its mass and operating envelope do not.”

The woman considered him for a second, then descended the scaffold ladder without hurrying. She clipped the hammer to her belt before stepping through the barrier.

“Good,” she said. “Someone here knows the difference.”

She extended a hand. White mineral dust occupied the lines across her palm.

“Elin Casaus. Structural consultant.”

“Aaron Mercer.”

“Field-systems authority.”

“For this review.”

“Your manager has used the phrase final technical authority four times since seven-thirty. I assumed it referred to you or to a form he planned to produce after lunch.”

Barre lowered the site plans. “Dr. Casaus—”

“Elin is fine. Doctor makes contractors expect medicine, and I don’t prescribe anything they enjoy.”

Aaron shook her hand. Her grip remained brief and unperformed. She released him and pointed at the eastern wall.

“I need four things. Actual dead load below grade. Maximum transient load from the test hall. Permitted displacement at the protected-instrument boundary. Whether you can shut down for forty-eight hours while we open the wall.”

“Three hundred eighty tonnes distributed across the lower raft. The public test arrays can transfer a maximum impulse of one hundred sixty kilonewton-seconds into the hall isolation system, although ordinary certification cycles stay below thirty. Boundary displacement cannot exceed point-four millimeters laterally or point-two vertically. The lower gallery cannot shut down.”

Barre glanced at him. The first three values sat inside the civilian engineering release. The fourth did not disclose why a narrow corridor beneath a public materials laboratory required uninterrupted operation.

Elin removed a pencil from behind her ear and wrote the figures on the back of her glove.

“Cannot,” she said, “or no one has asked the person who can authorize it?”

“Cannot without transferring its functions to another site. No equivalent site exists within the required latency.”

“That’s an answer.”

She walked toward the plans.

Aaron remained where he stood for half a second longer than the exchange required.

For seven years, people who lacked his clearance had responded to a protected boundary in one of two ways. They accepted the missing information and became careful around it, or they treated the refusal as evidence of personal importance and pushed against it. Elin Casaus had asked what the omitted system did to the building. Once he supplied the engineering consequence, she had discarded the secret as irrelevant.

The efficiency caught him off guard.

He followed her to the table.

---

The Annex occupied a converted hydraulic-equipment works near the river, four tram stops from the Geneva facility and two administrative compartments away from anything publicly connected to the Continuity Directorate. Its northern hall tested Resonant construction materials under the official Discovery framework. Its basement housed a relay that monitored passive containment telemetry from three source-adjacent sites, including Aaron’s.

The relay performed no direct contact and stored no Node-derived pattern. It compared timing, thermal drift, reservoir closure, and field-isolation integrity across the network. Public inspection could see every physical component and still mistake the assembly for redundant calibration equipment, provided no one traced why it required continuous operation or which three facilities supplied the encrypted data.

Above it, the 1908 building had begun to move.

The movement remained small. Two-point-seven millimeters of differential settlement across the eastern bay over sixteen months. A hairline crack through the stair landing. Seasonal separation between the limestone window surrounds and later brick infill. None threatened immediate collapse. Together, they shifted the test hall’s baseline enough that the next equipment renewal required either structural correction or a larger vibration-control system.

Facilities had selected the larger system.

Elin had asked why.

Barre spread the approved proposal across the table. “The tuned isolation plinth remains within procurement tolerance. Installation can begin Monday if we preserve the current scope.”

“The current scope puts forty-six additional tonnes on a floor already telling us it dislikes the arrangement,” Elin said.

“The model shows reserve capacity.”

“The model assumes the steel transfer beam shown on drawing S-fourteen exists.”

“The renovation file certifies it.”

Elin set a clear evidence bag beside the drawing. Inside lay a rusted square-cut nail, two fragments of red brick, and a short cylinder of pale mortar.

“The wall disagrees.”

Aaron leaned over the plan. The building had acquired five major modifications in one hundred sixty-one years: hydraulic works, municipal depot, civil-defense store, university laboratory, and Authority annex. Each phase treated the previous survey as verified geometry. The steel beam first appeared in a 1978 drawing marked existing, without a corresponding installation record.

“Where did you core?” he asked.

Elin marked two points along the eastern pier. “Here and here. Hand inspection only. We found continuous brick where the beam pocket should begin. The acoustic survey gives the same density through the next meter.”

“You need a third point below the stair return.”

“I requested one. Annex security refused access.”

Barre adjusted the plans. “The lower return adjoins a protected area.”

“So does gravity,” Elin said. “It has not accepted your compartment rules.”

Aaron examined the section. The requested point lay outside the lower gallery’s secure envelope but inside its camera exclusion margin. The denial had come from a local officer applying the protected-area category to the entire landing. No classified fact required it.

“Access approved between ten and twelve,” he said. “Line-of-sight screen at the gallery door. No electronic survey equipment beyond the landing. Hand tools and fiber scope are acceptable.”

Barre opened his mouth, then checked Aaron’s temporary authority on the site sheet and closed it.

Elin marked the approval in her notebook. “Do you do that often?”

“Approve wall inspections?”

“Answer the question security should have asked before saying no.”

“Often enough that the process should have improved.”

“Institutions are buildings. They keep every repair and call the result original design.”

She said it while studying the 1978 plan, without offering the sentence as insight. Aaron returned his attention to the load table before she could observe that he had given it more consideration than the table required.

The proposed plinth used six laminated isolation bearings beneath a reinforced concrete slab. During a certification pulse, the public array redirected mechanical impulse from a known hydraulic reservoir into a test article. The article returned part of that impulse through its mount; steel masses and viscous dampers absorbed the remainder, converting motion into measured heat. The system created nothing. It accounted for every joule through reservoir draw, article deformation, acoustic loss, damper temperature, and residual motion.

Its field effects ended within the declared test boundary.

Its ordinary weight still entered the floor.

Aaron recalculated the existing model using Elin’s continuous-masonry assumption. The missing transfer beam changed the result in two opposing directions. The eastern wall carried more vertical load than Facilities believed, but it also stiffened the bay against lateral motion. Removing or bypassing it carelessly could place the stair and roof truss onto a frame never designed to receive them.

“The plinth does not cause the primary risk,” he said.

Barre exhaled.

“It exposes it,” Aaron continued. “The pulse frequency crosses the masonry bay’s estimated second mode during shutdown. If the dampers close unevenly, the added mass transfers laterally through the stair return.”

Barre stopped exhaling.

Elin moved beside Aaron and read his calculation from the tablet. “You used a four-percent damping ratio.”

“Facilities used six.”

“Facilities used new masonry in dry laboratory conditions.”

“Core sample?”

“Three-point-one to four-point-three, depending on whether the lime pocket extends past the window bay.”

Aaron changed the lower bound to three percent. The transient displacement rose above the relay’s tolerance.

“No installation Monday,” he said.

Barre put both hands on the table. “The renewal window took eleven months to schedule.”

“Then the schedule has had eleven months to become wrong.”

Elin glanced at Aaron.

The smallest possible change occurred at one corner of her mouth. It did not qualify as a smile. Aaron refused the unnecessary task of classifying it further.

---

At 10:14, Elin entered the lower stair return with a hand drill, a fiber scope, and no knowledge that the white door nine meters behind her protected a containment relay.

Aaron entered with her because his temporary site authority required a cleared escort near the exclusion margin. Barre stayed upstairs to begin explaining the delayed installation to Procurement.

The basement predated electrical service. Limewashed brick formed shallow arches between iron beams blackened by age and later fireproofing. Copper pipes ran along the ceiling in exact modern lines. The floor carried a permanent dampness near the river wall despite three generations of drainage work. Under the odor of wet mineral and machine oil, Aaron could detect hot dust from the relay cooling system.

The public test hall above them announced each movement through structure. A trolley crossed the northern bay. Someone dropped a tool near the loading doors. Pumps started in sequence, their vibration reaching the stair as a low periodic pressure through Aaron’s boots.

The Geneva facility insulated classified work from ordinary texture: sealed floors, regulated air, doors that closed without impact, systems designed to remove incidental information. The Annex accumulated evidence. Every wall recorded another use. Someone had penciled three elevations onto the stair pier in 1952. A rusted bracket remained embedded at shoulder height after the pipe it supported disappeared.

Elin touched the bracket. “Head hazard.”

“It has occupied that position for at least seventy years.”

“Persistent head hazard.”

She tied a strip of orange cloth around it and continued down.

At the approved inspection point, Aaron positioned the line-of-sight screen. The screen contained no field system, only a hinged aluminum frame and opaque fabric weighted along the bottom. It blocked the gallery door and left the masonry pier accessible.

Elin checked the distance between screen and wall. “Your protected instruments won’t object to percussion?”

“Stay below ninety decibels and do not use powered impact.”

“Your protected instruments have better employment conditions than most engineers.”

“They accept fewer meetings.”

She turned her head toward him.

Aaron checked whether the sentence required clarification. It did not.

Elin set the fiber scope case on the landing. “Hold this.”

He held the work light while she drilled through the mortar bed by hand. The operation took nine minutes. Elin changed bits twice, collected the powder on black paper, and stopped at each change in resistance. She worked without narration until the scope entered the cavity.

“There,” she said.

Aaron moved closer to the small display. The scope showed brick, fractured lime mortar, then a dark plane eight centimeters beyond the expected face.

“Steel,” he said.

“Maybe. Wrong orientation for the transfer beam.”

She rotated the scope. The dark plane narrowed into a vertical edge. Corrosion had expanded around it, cracking the adjacent mortar.

“Column flange,” Aaron said.

“Or a rail section reused as a post. People built creatively when no one expected software to judge them later.”

She withdrew the scope, changed the lens angle, and inserted it again. A second edge resolved above the first. The steel member rose inside the masonry but ended below the stair beam. It supported a short brick arch carrying the wall above the gallery opening.

The 1978 surveyor had detected steel, assumed a beam, and drawn the structural relation that made the renovation legible.

The assumption had survived forty-one years because the wall continued standing.

“Load-bearing by consequence,” Elin said. “Not by plan.”

Aaron studied the scope image. “The post carries the arch. The arch transfers into the eastern pier. The pier restrains the stair. The stair braces the test-bay frame.”

“And the frame takes the roof truss when snow load pushes west.”

“Removing one relation changes five systems.”

“That’s usually how buildings object to improvement.”

Her terminology mapped too easily onto work Aaron did not permit himself to discuss. Independent structures. Unplanned relations. A connection that survived because every component compensated for an inaccurate model. Coupling, expressed in brick and nineteenth-century steel, without a Conduit or a field boundary in sight.

He became conscious of the white door behind the screen, and of how quickly his attention had tried to connect the two domains.

Elin watched the scope, not him.

“Can you reinforce it in place?” he asked.

“Yes. That isn’t the useful question.”

“What is?”

“What can move, and what are you only pretending can move because the project needs an answer?”

The relay could not move. The test hall could, at the cost of eleven months and an explanation in public procurement records. The wall could accept new steel if the installation never crossed the secure gallery boundary. The stair could close for three days. The roof load could transfer temporarily into an external frame placed in the service alley.

Aaron gave her those constraints.

Elin listened, pencil stationary above the notebook.

“Fine,” she said when he finished. “Then we leave the wall carrying what it already knows how to carry. We give the new equipment its own route to ground.”

“Independent frame.”

“Independent until it isn’t. Every frame touches something. We control the interfaces.”

She drew a rough section: four steel columns outside the existing bay, paired transfer girders through the ceiling zone, spring bearings beneath a lighter equipment slab, and lateral ties that engaged only beyond a declared displacement. Ordinary operation left the old structure untouched. An extreme event allowed the new frame and old wall to exchange load gradually rather than collide.

“The gap must account for thermal expansion,” Aaron said.

“Obviously.”

“And asynchronous damper closure.”

“Less obviously. Give me the worst case.”

He did.

She altered the tie geometry. “That gets you point-three millimeters at the lower boundary if your impulse value includes reflected load.”

“It does.”

“Then point-three. You asked for point-four.”

“I prefer margin.”

“Everyone prefers margin. No one likes paying rent for it.”

“The institution can afford point-one millimeters.”

“Put that in writing and I may frame it.”

The exchange required no guarded translation. Aaron experienced the absence as reduced processing load.

He kept holding the light after Elin no longer needed it.

“We should verify the mode before I design around it,” she said.

“The hall model contains a modal survey from last year.”

“The hall model also contains a beam we just proved imaginary.”

Aaron returned the work light to its case.

“What test do you propose?”

“Low-energy sweep at the floor, response measurements at the stair, eastern pier, and protected boundary. No field pulse. Your ordinary actuator can provide enough input if the gallery tolerates two kilojoules distributed over twenty seconds.”

“It tolerates the energy. The acceleration limit matters more.”

“Then set it.”

The Annex owned a portable electromechanical shaker for fixture calibration. Its internal flywheel stored a maximum of 2.4 kilojoules from an ordinary electrical supply and discharged through a bolted foot at a programmable frequency. Nothing crossed a Resonant boundary. The device merely gave the building a controlled reason to reveal how it already moved.

Aaron called Barre and requested the shaker, four optical displacement targets, six wired accelerometers, and an immediate suspension of work above the eastern bay. He notified the relay controller of a declared structural test and set an automatic abort at point-one-five millimeters on the lower boundary, half the displacement Elin’s preliminary geometry predicted.

“You asked for point-four in service,” she said when she read the abort value.

“Service follows verified repair. This test interrogates an unverified structure.”

“Margin before confidence.”

“The alternative reverses the dependency.”

“I know. I’m approving of you.”

Aaron entered the final threshold. “The phrasing lacked its ordinary markers.”

“You may need a larger sample.”

They returned to the test hall. Elin fixed optical targets to the limestone surround and stair stringer while Aaron placed accelerometers along the assumed load route. The eastern bay rose three floors above them through steel walkways, patched brick, and suspended cable trays. Daylight from the roof monitors reached the floor as gray rectangles interrupted by scaffold tubes. Without active machinery, the old works still produced motion: river traffic below the quay, trams on the next street, ventilation fans on the roof, and people crossing plates that no two renovation phases had fastened in the same way.

Elin crouched beside Aaron at the eastern pier. “Your sensor is too close to the mortar repair.”

“The repair follows the crack.”

“It follows the visible crack. We need the brick response, not the repair compound’s opinion of it.”

He moved the sensor sixteen centimeters.

“You could have said compliance changes across the material interface,” he said.

“I could have. Then we’d both spend longer pretending the sentence helped.”

Barre arrived with the shaker and an Annex technician named Noé. The four of them anchored it outside the proposed plinth footprint. Aaron reviewed the energy accounting on its calibration plate: maximum flywheel speed, motor input, bearing loss, foot displacement, thermal cutoff. The last service certificate remained valid for another nineteen days. Noé connected the wired sensors to an isolated recorder so the test generated no radio traffic near the lower gallery.

At 11:23, Aaron authorized the sweep.

The shaker began below hearing and climbed through the structure in increments of a quarter hertz. At six hertz, the stair target moved point-zero-two millimeters. At nine, the eastern pier response divided into two peaks the 1978 model treated as one. At eleven-point-seven-five, the vertical steel inside the wall transferred motion into the brick arch and returned it through the stair landing.

The response trace rose.

Aaron watched the lower-boundary channel. Point-zero-six. Point-zero-nine. Point-one-two.

“Hold frequency,” Elin said.

Noé held it.

The trace separated into phase components. The wall and stair moved together for three cycles. Then the later test-bay frame lagged, loaded the stair connection, and pulled the pier west by another hundredth of a millimeter.

“Abort,” Aaron said.

Noé released the drive. The shaker routed the flywheel’s remaining energy through its brake resistor. Warm air left the rear vent. The building continued moving after the input ended, each response smaller than the last until ambient vibration obscured it.

Elin watched the decay curve. “Three-point-two percent.”

“Your core estimate held.”

“Within point-one. I’ll be unbearable for the rest of the day.”

“Does the condition differ from baseline?”

“You need a larger sample.”

Noé concealed a laugh by disconnecting the nearest sensor.

Elin enlarged the phase plot. The test confirmed both the concealed post and the uncontrolled interface between the nineteenth-century wall and the later steel frame. The new plinth would not simply add vertical mass. During damper closure it would force two systems with different response timing to exchange load through the stair connection.

“Your independent frame still needs the displacement ties,” Aaron said.

“Yes. But now we know when they engage and what they must not engage with first.”

“The distinction will reduce steel weight.”

“It will reduce the amount of steel everyone can see. The useful part is reducing the number of assumptions no one can see.”

She removed the target from the stair and rubbed its adhesive residue away with her thumb.

“A model isn’t honest because every number has six decimals,” she said. “It’s honest when it leaves room for the building to know something you don’t.”

Aaron saved the test record to the civilian project file. Another copy routed into the relay’s classified maintenance log, where the same movement would appear without Elin’s name and without the public test-hall geometry.

Two accurate records. Two incompatible accounts of why the measurement mattered.

“Lunch,” Elin said. “Before your manager calls this a successful early-stage validation.”

---

They ate lunch on the Annex loading step because the eastern bay remained closed and the meeting room smelled of opened mortar samples.

Elin had acquired two paper cartons from a bakery across the road. One contained a lentil tart with more structural integrity than the Annex wall. The other contained slices of pear pressed into a custard that had not survived transport. She gave Aaron the tart without asking.

“You were going to choose that one,” she said.

“On what evidence?”

“You looked at the damaged box first and decided against the administrative burden.”

“The intact box could have contained either item.”

“But it didn’t.”

Rain earlier in the morning had darkened the service alley. A delivery bicycle moved past the open gate, tires drawing two narrow lines through grit. The river air carried wet stone, diesel, yeast from the bakery, and the colder metallic smell of the Annex ventilation exhaust. No filtered system separated one source from another.

Elin balanced her carton on one knee and reviewed the preliminary sketch.

“Your report is going to call this a scope revision,” she said.

“It is a scope revision.”

“Your manager’s report will call it an optimization opportunity.”

“That would imply the original design remained viable.”

“Which it didn’t.”

“Correct.”

“Will you let him write it anyway?”

Aaron separated the tart’s paper wrapper from a section of crust. “Procurement needs an amendment supported by a factual finding. ‘Optimization opportunity’ does not describe the load-path error.”

“So no.”

“In more durable language.”

“You put reinforcement around your answers.”

“Answers encounter load.”

“So do walls. We still put windows in them.”

She returned to the sketch.

Aaron ate the tart. Across the alley, water gathered at the end of a bent gutter and dropped onto a blue waste container at six- to nine-second intervals. The noise differed from the courtyard basin outside his office: thinner metal, longer resonance, less regular drainage. He noticed himself comparing them and stopped.

“How long have you worked for the Authority?” Elin asked.

“Seven years in the current structure. Longer if predecessor programs count.”

“Do they?”

“For pension purposes, selectively.”

She laughed once. The sound entered the alley without institutional review.

“And you?” he asked.

“Independent for three years. Before that, municipal infrastructure in Zaragoza, then a seismic retrofit firm in Lausanne. I left when the partners discovered that every survey could recommend the same proprietary damper if you adjusted the assumptions carefully enough.”

“Did you object to the damper?”

“It was an excellent damper.”

“You objected to the adjusted assumptions.”

“I objected to pretending a good answer excused a false question.”

The sentence landed close enough to Aaron’s actual life that his attention narrowed around it.

Elin scraped pear custard from the carton lid with the wooden fork. She had not aimed at him. She described a former employer, a building, a professional standard. No subtext required response.

Aaron could have agreed.

Instead he said, “False questions often produce administratively stable answers.”

“For a while.”

“Sometimes a while is the specified service life.”

Elin set down the fork. Her eyes moved across his face with the same concentrated neutrality she had applied to the scope image.

“That sounded less like buildings,” she said.

The correct response existed in several forms. He could redirect to the public history of Resonance standards. He could make the statement into a general observation about regulation. He could invoke protected work and end the line cleanly.

“It was not only about buildings,” Aaron said.

She waited.

He provided nothing further.

After two seconds, she nodded and picked up the fork.

“Fine,” she said. “Not every wall needs opening on the first survey.”

The metaphor should have irritated him. It did not.

---

By 17:20, Elin had produced a four-page preliminary finding, two hand sections, an inspection schedule, and a list titled INFORMATION YOUR BUILDING STILL OWES ME.

Aaron supplied three items from the civilian release and routed two through site security review. The remaining question concerned the lower gallery’s operational heat rejection. He gave her the maximum thermal load and seasonal range without explaining what produced them.

She accepted the figures.

Barre accepted the delayed installation after Aaron signed the suspension under his still-active public credential. The approval block rendered his name beneath **FINAL TECHNICAL AUTHORITY**. In six months, an office title would occupy the same field. The building would receive a decision from the same person without retaining any public mechanism to discover that continuity.

Elin leaned over his shoulder long enough to read the authorization.

“You have very neat institutional handwriting,” she said.

“It is a digital signature.”

“Exactly.”

She gathered the sample bags into a rigid case. Aaron folded the line-of-sight screen and returned it to Annex security. The local officer signed the inspection closure without asking what Elin had found.

Outside, the rain had resumed as a suspended mist too fine to produce distinct drops. Elin stood beneath the loading canopy and checked the transit display mounted across the street.

“The twelve is delayed,” Aaron said.

“Seven minutes.”

“Nine. The display uses scheduled position until the tram crosses the previous sensor.”

“Do you know that because you work for an international technical authority or because waiting offends you personally?”

“The categories overlap.”

“There’s the reinforced answer again.”

She opened her case, moved the preliminary report aside, and removed a plain card. One side carried the name ELIN CASAUS, a Geneva number, and a professional registration. The other showed a pencil drawing of a bridge pier with an arrow pointing to a crack.

“The revised design takes six days,” she said. “The structural workshop takes another three. After that, your building gets several expensive choices and one inexpensive choice everyone will hate.”

“Which do you expect it to select?”

“The expensive choice described as economical over thirty years.”

She handed him the card.

“The number already appears on your contract,” Aaron said.

“That number routes through my office. This one also reaches me when no building has paid for the privilege.”

The distinction contained no ambiguity that a competent person could honestly claim.

Across the street, the transit display changed from seven minutes to nine.

Elin glanced at it, then back at Aaron. “Dinner Friday?”

He considered the operational calendar. No source session. No phase-one records interview. A Conduit classification review ended at sixteen hundred. The private requirement file contained work enough to occupy every available hour, but it imposed no external deadline.

He also considered that none of those facts answered the question she had asked.

“Yes,” he said.

“Good. I’ll send you a place. No load calculations.”

“Restaurants contain load paths.”

“You can know they exist without checking them.”

The tram turned into view at the end of the road. Its wheels produced a high metallic note against the wet rail before settling into a lower vibration. Elin stepped toward the curb, then looked once at the card still in Aaron’s hand.

“Friday,” she said.

“Friday.”

She boarded through the rear door. Aaron remained under the canopy while the tram moved away, its windows briefly reflecting the Annex’s patched eastern wall.

The day had produced a delayed installation, a new load path, and seven hours of records under his name that Mireille Vautrin would eventually need to incorporate into the retirement schedule. It had also produced a private number that belonged to no controlled partition and depended on no institutional role.

Aaron put the card in the inside pocket of his coat rather than scanning it into his work terminal.

The choice lacked technical significance.

He noticed it anyway.

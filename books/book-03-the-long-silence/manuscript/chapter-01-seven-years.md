# Chapter One: SEVEN YEARS

## Day 4,356

The form for Aaron Mercer's disappearance arrived at 05:52 and required his legal name.

He supplied it.

The next field requested every prior legal name, public professional designation, active institutional credential, persistent biometric identifier, and field-signature index associated with the subject. The Directorate's personnel system had prefilled eleven entries. Aaron found four omissions before the coffee reached a temperature he considered usable.

He added his Helsinki research credential, the superseded Helix Perimeter access hash, an early International Resonance Authority contractor number, and the coupling index the original array had generated before anyone accepted it as a measurement rather than a calibration fault.

The form sorted the entries into two columns.

**PUBLIC TRACE — SCHEDULED FOR RETIREMENT**

**CONTROLLED TRACE — CONTINUITY REQUIRED**

His name occupied both.

Morning rain moved against the Geneva office window in fine diagonal lines. The pane's three mineral flaws divided the courtyard into four imperfect vertical sections. Facilities had again proposed replacing the old glass during the winter envelope work. Security had again declined. Across the courtyard, copper gutters released water onto pale stone darkened by two centuries of weather and seven years of newer classifications. The tree below his office had lost half its leaves since the beginning of the month. Wet yellow shapes adhered to the paving in patterns the drainage model had not predicted and the maintenance staff corrected with a broom.

Aaron read the document header.

**LIVING-SUBJECT CONTINUITY CLASSIFICATION**

**TRACE RETIREMENT PROTOCOL 4.2**

**INITIAL HORIZON: 540 DAYS**

**SUBJECT STATUS AFTER FORMALIZATION: ACTIVE**

The last word carried more useful information than the title. The process did not propose his death, detention, or departure from the program. It proposed a controlled separation between the person the public record could identify and the person who would continue entering this building, authorizing Conduit architecture, and maintaining contact boundaries around a nonhuman intelligence hidden behind a discovery narrative he had helped construct.

The distinction required twenty-seven pages.

He opened the protocol reference.

Revision 4.2 belonged to the Directorate standard template library. Its validation summary listed twelve completed applications across diplomatic, intelligence, and strategic-technology compartments. All twelve involved living subjects whose continued work required the public version of their expertise to become less interesting over time. The template had passed legal, security, medical, pension, travel, records-integrity, and dependent-impact review.

Aaron had used outputs from the system for seven years. He had never reviewed the template itself in detail.

That departure from his ordinary method registered. He opened the technical annex, read the first two pages of access-control definitions, and marked the remaining review for later. The initiating notice carried Director Rhys's authentication and a 07:30 appointment. Before then Aaron needed to complete only the subject inventory and initial-consent section. Full verification would occupy the next thirty days.

The form asked whether he understood that public retirement did not terminate operational duty.

He selected **YES**.

It asked whether he understood that controlled records would retain his identity, decisions, biological signature, technical authorship, medical history, and legal accountability.

**YES**.

It asked whether he understood that the protocol could reduce or redirect ordinary public access to truthful records without changing the underlying controlled record.

**YES**.

It asked whether he consented.

Aaron left the field open.

On the other half of his display, **THE NEXT SPECIFICATION** still contained one requirement and no solution.

*The carrier shall preserve field-relational provenance without dependence on an active Conduit lineage, centralized archive, maintained controller state, or continuous energy input.*

Five years had produced fourteen candidate material families, forty-three nonviable geometries, and one additional certainty: a passive object could preserve shape, composition, charge distribution, or stress history, but relational order did not survive merely because its components did. Every current Resonant lattice translated stored relations through active controller state. Remove the controller, replace the lattice without an authenticated migration, or allow the declared reservoir to close, and the deeper payload became ordinary material with an interesting manufacturing defect.

The specification asked how a truth might survive after every system responsible for remembering it had ended.

The new form asked the same question about a man, with more departments attached.

At 06:47, Aaron completed the inventory. He counted seventy-three public or semi-public records that identified him directly, eighteen durable citations, four conference recordings, two university alumni entries, nine regulatory authorships, and an unknown number of local copies beyond Directorate correction authority. The protocol did not promise deletion. Deletion attracted comparison, produced gaps, and encouraged anyone interested in a gap to reconstruct its edges.

It proposed attrition instead.

New records would cite offices rather than him. Existing public work would remain accurate but lose cross-indexing priority. Search systems would receive verified institutional metadata that associated his technical contributions with teams, programs, and later revisions. Biographical summaries would converge on a limited professional account: field-systems engineer; Helix Perimeter appointment; International Resonance Authority Conduit work; departure from public service after a restricted transition period; subsequent consulting protected by ordinary commercial confidentiality.

Every statement could become true.

Their arrangement would imply that Aaron Mercer had completed a difficult early career and then become unimportant.

He saved the inventory at 07:11. The system accepted the factual sections and continued to wait for consent.

Aaron drank the coffee. It had passed through usable temperature while he worked.

He finished it anyway.

---

Rhys's office occupied the secure end of the third floor, where the corridor narrowed between the building's original load wall and a later field-isolation shell. The resulting space forced visitors to approach the Director's door slightly off center. No one had corrected it because the wall carried four floors and the shell contained a source-derived archive.

Rhys sat behind his desk when Aaron entered. He had removed his jacket and folded it across the adjacent chair. A white tablet beside his right hand displayed the morning medication schedule long enough for Aaron to identify three timed doses before Rhys turned it facedown.

The motion contained no invitation to discuss it.

Aaron took the chair opposite him.

“You left consent open,” Rhys said.

“Consent taken before the initiating authority explains the necessity would satisfy the form and weaken the decision.”

“The form says substantially the same thing on page six.”

“I reached page four.”

Rhys studied him. “You have not read the template.”

“Not in full.”

“That may qualify as the first encouraging sign in your development.”

“I scheduled the rest.”

“There it goes.”

The rain struck the outer sill behind Aaron. Rhys's office faced the street rather than the courtyard, though the security film converted the view into diffuse gray light from this angle. Two sealed archive cases stood against the inner wall. One held early Directorate charters. The other held no visible label and drew continuous power through a conduit inside the floor.

Rhys opened Aaron's trace map between them.

The public side formed a dense branching structure. Helsinki connected to Helix Perimeter. Helix connected to the official Resonance Discovery literature, three technical working groups, Lena Park's calibration papers, Amara Sen's field-research portfolio, and the first Conduit design history. The later branches spread through standards committees and training systems on four continents. Aaron's name did not occupy the most visible position in any branch. It occupied too many structurally necessary ones.

The controlled side contained fewer records and more authority. Contact Zero. Primary Coupling Interface. Standard Eight. The Node monitoring partition. The Mercer-Sen-Fadel source compartment. Practitioner-pairing classification. The nine-invariant encoding did not appear. Neither did the next specification.

“Why now?” Aaron asked.

“Because The First Coupling completed its first five-year doctrinal review last month.”

“Doctrine reduces dependence on individual practitioners.”

“Operationally, in the characterized protocol. Historically, it increases dependence on your decisions. Every derived standard now points toward a root record carrying your authentication. Every external challenge to the doctrine can eventually reach the question of why one technical authority received unilateral classification power over pairings. The accurate answer cannot enter a public hearing.”

“The ordinary restricted answer can.”

“For several years. Not indefinitely.” Rhys expanded the map. “You also remain the only practitioner who can maintain direct source contact without dissociation. The Controlled Continuity Doctrine converts that fact into a permanent office dependency unless we separate the office from the public identity now.”

Aaron examined the migration lines. His named technical authorities would move into three institutional roles: Coupling Classification Authority, Source Interface Custodian, and Conduit Continuity Architect. Publicly, other qualified officials could occupy those roles. Inside the highest compartment, Aaron would continue to exercise them through an operational credential bound to his biological and field signatures.

“A classified office with one eligible occupant,” he said.

“Initially.”

“Soren cannot inherit source interface authority.”

“No.”

“Amara can hold independent source consent and scientific custody. She cannot perform Coupling.”

“No.”

“Then the office remains a name with additional processing.”

“It remains a function the institution can acknowledge without acknowledging why only you currently perform it.”

Aaron enlarged the final-state model. After formalization, ordinary personnel systems would no longer return an active Directorate employment record for him. Building access would resolve against a compartment credential without exposing a civil identity to local security logs. Travel would route through restricted technical-service orders. Medical systems would maintain two indexes joined only inside the classification authority: one public and dormant, one controlled and active. His pension contributions would continue through a trust structure so actuarial databases did not infer either death or undeclared state employment.

Institutional invisibility required considerable administrative evidence that a person continued to eat, travel, age, and acquire taxable income.

“This architecture predates the doctrine,” Aaron said.

“By nineteen years.”

“Revision 4.2.”

“I know the number.”

“Why this template?”

“Because it has survived twelve living-subject applications, six judicial inspections, and one hostile parliamentary records inquiry without exposing a protected program or manufacturing a dead citizen. Because your classification cannot depend on a system designed by you alone. Because people who disappear themselves tend to optimize for the work and omit the person.”

Aaron reviewed the dependency categories. “The protocol includes a subject approval gate for each phase.”

“It does.”

“I can stop it.”

“Until final formalization.”

“After which stopping it would reveal the controlled trace.”

“After which reversal requires the same authorities as initial classification, plus an exposure review. That constraint is not hidden.”

“Page eleven?”

“Nine.”

Rhys's right hand closed once against the edge of the desk. The fingers reopened in sequence, slower than the movement required. He moved the hand out of sight beneath the trace display.

Aaron kept his attention on the protocol.

The explicit discussion could wait for evidence neither of them could route around.

“Why five hundred forty days?” he asked.

“Short enough that current authorities remain accountable. Long enough for public records to settle through ordinary cycles. Two conference programs, one standards revision, a tax year, credential renewal, travel history, and the natural end of your present appointment.”

“My appointment has no natural end.”

“It will acquire one.”

“True statements.”

“As far as they can carry us.”

Aaron returned to the consent field. “And where they cannot?”

“Controlled omission. Narrow redirection. In three cases, sealed correction. No false death, no invented offense, no allegation of incapacity, and no retroactive denial of work you performed. The public account will become incomplete, not defamatory.”

The distinction resembled every durable compromise they had built since Contact Zero. A boundary did not need to make one system unreal in order to prevent exchange with another. It needed to declare which relations could cross, under what authority, with what accounting, and how each side returned to a stable state.

Aaron's own classification used no Resonant field and generated no energy. It only changed which systems could exchange information about him.

“Who initiated the review?” he asked.

“I did.”

“Who approved the horizon?”

“The Continuity Personnel Panel.”

“Kessler?”

“Concurred on operational necessity. She has no access to the personal annex and no unilateral stop.”

Kessler's agreement did not imply intimacy or victory. Her controlled-capacity doctrine required continuity of the only confirmed Eight as much as Rhys's containment doctrine required protection of the source relationship. The same institutional disagreement that had produced The First Coupling could support the removal of Aaron's name for opposite, compatible reasons.

“Amara?” he asked.

“Not yet notified. Her controlled-record dependencies enter the phase-one audit. She will have authority over how her joint work is represented.”

“Soren?”

“His record will point to the office, not to your identity. He does not need the reason.”

“Lena?”

Rhys left the trace map unchanged.

“The protocol does not classify her,” he said. “It identifies what contact can continue without exposing the controlled role. That assessment belongs to you and the case officer. Erasure from public record does not require erasure from every private life.”

Aaron considered the sentence and rejected the relief his nervous system attempted to assign it. The protocol had not yet demonstrated that the distinction would survive contact with an actual person.

“Who is the case officer?”

“Mireille Vautrin. Records Integrity. She has handled four prior living-subject transitions and has no program access beyond the dependency abstracts.”

“Has she reviewed the source-office migration?”

“She reviewed the template application. You will review the technical mapping.”

“Before phase one?”

“During it.”

The answer should have prompted a request for delay. Aaron noted the impulse, then the competing fact: every additional month added named decisions to a record already too dependent on him. The First Coupling's adoption had converted one experimental history into permanent doctrine. Five years of derived standards had propagated his signature even when the source geometry did not.

A field array had once returned his own architecture altered by something on the other side of an aperture. The first evidence that the signal addressed him had entered a log under his name. Everything after had required progressively more accurate ways to conceal what that name meant.

The current proposal did not remove him from the work.

It acknowledged that the work might never release him.

Aaron selected **YES** in the consent field.

Rhys authenticated as initiating authority. The header changed.

**TRACE RETIREMENT PROTOCOL 4.2 — ACTIVE**

**PHASE ZERO: SUBJECT INVENTORY ACCEPTED**

**TARGET FORMALIZATION WINDOW: DAYS 4,885–4,915**

The dates lay eighteen months ahead. No ceremony accompanied them. The rain maintained its rate against the sill.

“You continue every current duty,” Rhys said.

“I read the last field.”

“I am telling you.”

Aaron met his eyes.

Rhys had once offered him participation in suppression or removal from the work, with three days to decide. The choice had been precisely honest and structurally coercive. This choice differed. Aaron possessed more authority, more evidence, and a stop at every pre-formalization phase. He also understood the threat now. The Node's concealment protected a civilization that did not know it needed protection. His ongoing relation to it could not survive indefinite public scrutiny.

“I continue,” Aaron said.

Rhys nodded once. “Yes.”

Mireille Vautrin worked two floors below the public records office in a room without networked glass.

The room held three steel desks, a document scanner built into a classified enclosure, and movable shelves filled with gray archival boxes. Each box carried a date range and a transition number. No names. A dehumidifier near the inner wall produced a steady vibration through the floor and discharged into a transparent hose that crossed six centimeters of open tile before entering a drain. Someone had marked the trip hazard with yellow tape rather than rerouting the hose.

The Directorate could remove a person from public history more easily than it could persuade Facilities to move a drain.

Vautrin stood when Aaron entered. Near fifty, she wore a dark green shirt without an institutional badge and had placed a paper notebook beside the terminal.

“Dr. Mercer,” she said. “For this meeting.”

“And after it?”

“Until your civil title stops resolving in this compartment. Then I use your transition designation unless we meet outside controlled work.”

“Do you expect that?”

“The template expects nothing. I prefer not to decide a human relationship eighteen months in advance.”

She indicated the chair opposite her.

The terminal displayed Aaron's inventory with each record assigned to one of five treatments: preserve, seal linkage, redirect attribution, allow attrition, or correct. None said delete.

Vautrin began with the distinction Rhys had already made.

“We are not manufacturing a death,” she said. “We are not removing your citizenship, assets, legal obligations, medical access, or right to maintain private relationships. We are reducing the capacity of an ordinary investigator to connect your public trace to continuing classified work. Your civil identity enters protected dormancy. It remains yours.”

“A dormant identity that pays tax.”

“Several active ones fail that test.”

Aaron read the treatment table. “The Helsinki credential remains public.”

“Preserved. Altering an academic award creates more discrepancies than it resolves.”

“Helix Perimeter employment?”

“Preserved through the station's decommissioning record. Later searches will weight the appointment as early-career work.”

“The official Discovery papers?”

“Preserved. New index metadata will emphasize the institutional author group. Your authorship stays attached to copies that already contain it.”

“Conduit standards?”

“Named authorship ends at the next scheduled revision. Prior versions remain in regulatory archives. Public summaries attribute the program to the Authority.”

“Conference recordings.”

“Allowed attrition under normal retention policy. No special takedown request.”

“The symposium from last year has mirrored copies.”

“Then the recording survives. Survival is not linkage.”

She spoke without the institutional softness people used when they expected a subject to mistake euphemism for protection. Her terms described operations. Aaron found this efficient.

Vautrin opened the dependency questionnaire.

“The template begins with categories,” she said. “We replace them with people where people exist. Spouse or partner?”

“No.”

“Children or dependents?”

“No.”

“Shared household?”

“No.”

“Immediate family contact requiring routine civil access?”

Aaron supplied the relevant entries. The answers occupied less space than the form allowed. Vautrin recorded frequency, jurisdiction, expected contact route, and whether any interruption would prompt a missing-person inquiry. She did not ask him to characterize affection numerically.

“Professional relationships maintained outside the controlled program?”

“Dr. Lena Park.”

Vautrin entered the name. “Frequency?”

“Irregular. Three to six messages per year. A meal when geography permits.”

“Does she know your current public role?”

“Yes.”

“Does she know your controlled role?”

“No.”

“Would a public-service departure require explanation?”

“She would ask.”

“Would she accept ordinary commercial confidentiality as the reason you could not answer fully?”

Aaron thought of Lena at lunch, years after Helix, describing the shape of a field she had already left. She accepted boundaries when she judged them to be chosen honestly. She noticed architecture. She also knew exactly how Aaron sounded when he converted a refusal into a technical limitation.

“Once,” he said.

Vautrin paused above the notebook. “Meaning?”

“She would accept it for one conversation. Repetition would become information.”

“Good.” She wrote the sentence by hand. “The standard model treats contact frequency as exposure. Sometimes consistency creates more exposure than contact. We will design the cover interaction later.”

Aaron glanced at the template version in the screen margin. “That distinction is not in the automated questionnaire.”

“No. The questionnaire assumes people behave like categories. Case officers remain employed because they do not.”

She proceeded through financial obligations, housing, travel habits, medical prescriptions, publication commitments, professional memberships, recurring purchases, and the biometric systems likely to preserve his face or gait outside government control. His life emerged as a network of modest, durable exchanges. Rent. Coffee. Rail tickets. A Helsinki alumni notice he never opened. An annual request to review a field-systems paper. The Geneva grocer whose payment system had seven years of evidence that Aaron bought the same bread until the supplier changed it and then spent two weeks buying none.

“Your ordinary pattern is unusually stable,” Vautrin said.

“It reduces variance.”

“It also makes change visible.”

She marked three dependencies for phase-one modeling.

The interview reached operational continuity at 11:18.

Vautrin's access ended at abstract labels. The terminal showed **RESTRICTED TECHNICAL AUTHORITY A**, **SOURCE-ADJACENT DUTY B**, and **CONTROLLED MEDICAL CONTINUITY C** where Aaron saw the actual roles. She did not know that Authority A governed practitioner pairings, Duty B meant continued relationship with the Node, or Medical Continuity C tracked the cost of Standard Eight contact.

“Each duty needs a credential that survives civil dormancy,” she said. “The protocol creates no anonymous power. Every action must remain attributable inside its proper compartment.”

“The current design maps the operational credential to my civil identity through the Directorate personnel root.”

“Which public-service separation will close.”

“Then the mapping needs an intermediate identity authority.”

“Template 4.2 creates one.”

She opened the reference architecture. A sealed continuity register would preserve the relation among Aaron's civil identity, transition designation, biometric record, Conduit authentication, and post-formalization operational credentials. Local systems could ask whether the arriving person possessed valid authority. They could not ask who he had previously been. The register would answer eligibility, scope, expiration, and audit destination without releasing the identity relation itself.

The design resembled a cryptographic broker and a personnel trust. It did not resemble a system Aaron would have approved without examining its failure modes.

Vautrin rolled a compact authentication plate from beneath the terminal. Its interface surface carried the matte silver-gray finish used in current Conduit diagnostic equipment, though no active field lattice sat beneath it. Two optical sensors, a thermal array, and a contact electrode surrounded a recessed credential port.

“Baseline binding,” she said. “Civil trace first. Controlled trace second. The register proves both credentials resolve to one continuing person without exposing either credential to the other system.”

“The plate cannot authenticate field architecture.”

“It doesn't. Your Conduit supplies a signed classification assertion. The plate verifies that the same body presents both times.”

Aaron examined the declared systems on the side display. Local electrical reservoir: four hundred joules. Maximum thermal discharge: twenty-two joules. Optical capture, vascular response, skin conductance, gait-pressure comparison from the floor panel, and Conduit signature receipt. No Resonant operation authorized. No external identity lookup during controlled capture.

The procedure avoided asking the instrument to know more than its compartment permitted.

He placed his right hand on the surface and inserted his public credential. The plate drew eleven joules, returned a civil-identity match, and closed its temporary record into the continuity register. Vautrin removed the credential and rotated the terminal screen away from herself.

Aaron inserted the blank controlled token Rhys's office had issued that morning.

His Conduit requested permission to release a classification assertion: capability record valid, medical restrictions current, field signature matched to the sealed source-interface index. It did not release Standard Eight, Contact Zero, or his name. Aaron approved the request. A brief warmth crossed the contact electrode as the plate drew from its declared reservoir and verified the assertion against his physical presence.

The second capture closed.

The display showed no name.

**CONTINUITY RELATION ESTABLISHED**

**PUBLIC CREDENTIAL: VALID / ACTIVE**

**CONTROLLED CREDENTIAL: VALID / PRE-FORMALIZATION**

**CROSS-DISCLOSURE: PROHIBITED**

“After formalization, the first credential stops answering active-employment questions,” Vautrin said. “The relation remains sealed. The second continues.”

“And if the register fails?”

“The controlled token retains a seventy-two-hour local authority certificate. Recovery requires two of three physical attestations held by Records Integrity, initiating authority, and subject.”

“After formalization, the subject still holds one?”

“You do. Erasure does not remove your stop from the mechanism erasing you.”

The sentence belonged in the template. Aaron checked. Page thirty-eight contained its administrative equivalent in smaller language.

He reached for the technical annex, then stopped. The morning's review block already existed. Vautrin still had two hours of intake questions, and phase one specifically required his line-by-line verification before any credential migrated.

“Validated implementations?” he asked.

“Twelve.”

“Longest active duration?”

“Eleven years.”

“Compromise history?”

“No identity exposure through the register. Two attempted correlation attacks through external travel records, both contained. One dependent-impact failure caused by an unmodeled school enrollment. No relevant dependent in your current inventory.”

“Current.”

“The protocol requires quarterly rescoping.”

Aaron accepted the answer for intake purposes.

Vautrin printed the phase-zero attestation on a sheet containing a narrow metallic lattice through its center. The physical document existed because the continuity register could not serve as the sole proof of its own authorization. Aaron read the attestation, checked the document hash against the terminal, and signed below his legal name.

Vautrin signed as case officer. Rhys's initiating credential rendered into the lattice as a static optical pattern. The sheet contained no active Resonant state and no hidden energy source. Its geometry authenticated a stored hash when illuminated through the archive reader; any alteration broke the diffraction sequence.

She placed it in a gray box labeled **TR-4.2 / 4356 / 06**.

“The public record still knows you,” she said. “Today only starts the schedule.”

“When does the first external change occur?”

“Your next standards document will publish under office attribution. Six days.”

One week earlier, Aaron's name on a technical standard would have counted as continuity.

Next week, its absence would.

At 19:36, the courtyard held no direct light.

Aaron sat alone in his office with the trace-retirement technical annex open on one display and the next specification on the other. The rain had ended. Water continued falling from the copper gutter at irregular intervals, each drop striking the stone basin below with enough force to register through the old window as a faint, separate sound.

He had read seventy-four of the template's ninety-one pages.

Revision 4.2 handled credential migration, civil-record dormancy, legal accountability, and sealed identity recovery with more rigor than its intake form suggested. It also assumed that the subject's classified work could be described through existing institutional roles. Aaron's actual work exceeded its role map in three places, all connected to the Node.

He entered review notes for phase one.

The Source Interface Custodian credential needed independent medical stop authority even after local systems lost access to his civil medical index. The Coupling Classification Authority needed an audit route that preserved his decisions without exposing Standard Eight as the reason they carried final weight. The Continuity Architect role needed to survive Rhys's eventual departure and Aaron's own formalization without allowing either event to revoke the Mercer-Sen-Fadel partition by orphaning its named access list.

He did not write eventual death. Rhys's departure from authority required planning long before death entered the model.

At 20:08, Aaron reached the template's records-integrity assumptions.

The annex stated that external public records would converge toward the cover account through ordinary revision, retention, and ranking processes. It required quarterly sampling of known records and annual adversarial review. The method had survived twelve applications.

He marked the section **REVIEW REQUIRED** rather than **DEFECT**.

The distinction reflected available evidence.

On the second display, the next specification waited.

Its first requirement sought a carrier independent of active Conduits, archives, controllers, and continuous energy. The erasure protocol had spent the day demonstrating another dependency he had not named. A durable object could preserve evidence and still fail if future access required knowledge of who designed it. His public identity would narrow. His controlled identity could eventually become inaccessible through exactly the kind of maintained institutional chain the physical carrier needed to outlast.

Aaron added a second requirement.

*The carrier shall permit verification of provenance without requiring disclosure, survival, or prior knowledge of the originating person.*

He read both lines.

The first prevented the inheritance from dying with a machine.

The second prevented it from dying with Aaron Mercer.

Neither solved the material problem. Neither identified the future practitioner, prescribed a decision, or released Aaron from the work. They defined two failures the final design could not contain.

His personnel status now displayed in a narrow band above the documents.

**PUBLIC TRACE: ACTIVE — RETIREMENT SCHEDULE INITIATED**

**CONTROLLED TRACE: ACTIVE — CONTINUITY REQUIRED**

For the next eighteen months, both versions of him would occupy the record. After formalization, one would cease accumulating evidence. The other would continue entering rooms no public system admitted existed, maintaining a relationship no official history could name.

Seven years earlier, Aaron had begun converting Contact Zero into the official Discovery record: accurate sentences arranged to prevent their complete meaning from crossing into public history.

The Directorate now proposed the inverse operation.

It would preserve every relation that mattered inside the boundary and remove the address from everywhere else.

Aaron authenticated the two requirements under a private credential the erasure protocol did not know existed.

Then he continued reading the standard template.

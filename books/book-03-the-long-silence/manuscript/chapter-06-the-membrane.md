# Chapter Six: THE MEMBRANE

## Day 4,374–4,375

At 18:31 on Sunday, Aaron stopped trying to make the standard protocol survive.

The archive room held seventeen suspended transition actions, nine unresolved challenge classes, and four people who had stopped referring to lunch. The dehumidifier continued its work without sharing their scheduling problem. Water advanced through the transparent hose in brief, unequal measures, crossed the yellow-taped section of tile, and disappeared into the drain.

Aaron had spent six hours testing corrections to Trace Retirement Protocol 4.2. Every correction protected one relation by placing more pressure on another.

Rotating the controlled credential broke the public provenance chain but weakened legal accountability. Delaying pension attestations reduced temporal correlation but created a gap an actuarial system would flag. Consolidating medical indexes preserved clinical history but exposed biological continuity. Separating them protected the classification and increased the probability that an emergency physician would treat two partial records as two different patients.

The template could hide a value.

It could not hide the fact that one life continued producing consequences.

Mireille Vautrin sat at the central steel desk with the twelve prior living-subject files arranged across three isolated displays. Sabine Morel occupied the second desk. Koenig had returned the public-registry terminal to its blue case and left after documenting the corrected certificate. Rhys had gone upstairs forty-three minutes earlier for a scheduled medical consultation that nobody described as such.

The fourth person in the room existed only as a model.

Vautrin had reconstructed each prior subject from abstract dependency classes. The displays showed no names, programs, diagnoses, destinations, or operational duties. Each model contained a public identity, a protected appointment, a sealed continuity relation, and the systems that still needed correct answers after formalization.

In eleven cases, the classified duty could move among qualified staff. The subject's public departure and the office's continued activity created no necessary correlation. A protected chemist left public service; a laboratory continued. A negotiator ceased publishing; a delegation continued. An inspector's named credentials expired; a rotating institutional credential carried the next inspection.

The twelfth subject had possessed a unique memory of an operation no record could safely describe. The transition preserved access to that memory through a sealed consultation authority. It did not require the same person to enter the same facility every week, authenticate new technical doctrine, maintain a continuous medical restriction, or intervene when a nonhuman source changed state.

Aaron's abstract occupied the fourth display.

**PUBLIC TECHNICAL AUTHOR A**

**PROTECTED DISPOSITION HOLDER A**

**SOURCE-INTERFACE CUSTODIAN A**

**CONTROLLED MEDICAL SUBJECT A**

**CONTINUITY ARCHITECT A**

Five labels connected to one sealed identity root. Four connected to his field signature. Three connected to decisions that no present substitute could lawfully or technically make. After Rhys's eventual withdrawal, at least one more would connect to cross-compartment disposition authority if the charter followed the eligibility model the Directorate had requested.

The protocol treated the five labels as compartments.

The world treated them as one person arriving for work.

“The prior applications do not validate this one,” Aaron said.

Vautrin folded her hands over a paper copy of the dependency map. “They validate components.”

“The components produced this morning.”

“The standard template did not anticipate your concentration of functions.”

“Then it cannot remain the governing design.”

Morel removed her glasses and cleaned one lens with the edge of a gray cloth. “You consented to a process that prohibits self-classification.”

“Correct.”

“Replacing the protocol with one you design for yourself approaches the same boundary from the other side.”

“Design and approval are different functions.”

“They need to remain different after you become the institution's senior disposition authority.”

Aaron enlarged the twelve comparison models. “Then preserve independent approval. The technical architecture still requires redesign.”

Vautrin studied the fourth display. Her access translated source contact, Standard Eight, and the Node into restricted duty classes. She could evaluate what a record disclosed without knowing why the underlying role admitted no substitute.

“What replaces the template?” she asked.

Aaron deleted the three correction branches he had built that afternoon.

The display returned to the failed protocol.

“Nothing yet.”

Morel put her glasses back on. “That may be the first safe answer anyone has supplied today.”

“It is not a sufficient one.”

“No,” she said. “But it can remain true overnight.”

Aaron checked the wall clock. Elin expected him at nineteen hundred. The tram required fourteen minutes under the Sunday schedule, followed by a six-minute walk. His coat and private phone remained four access boundaries away.

He saved the failed branches in the defect annex, each labeled with the relation it protected and the new exposure it created. Vautrin sealed the twelve prior-case comparisons. Morel drafted an instruction preserving every hold until a replacement review method existed. Nothing advanced toward formalization.

Aaron left the fourth display blank except for one line.

**ASSUMPTION REJECTED: IDENTITY PROTECTION IS NOT RELATIONAL PROTECTION.**

At 18:36, he signed out through his public credential.

At 18:59, Elin opened her apartment door while he still held one hand near the bell.

“This is either admirable precision,” she said, “or evidence that you waited downstairs.”

“The tram removed the need for deception.”

“A disappointingly strong alibi.”

She stepped aside.

Her apartment occupied the third floor of a narrow building near Plainpalais. Warm air carried chickpeas, cumin, and the mineral smell of a radiator that wanted bleeding. Two pairs of boots stood on a square of folded newspaper inside the door. A shelf above them held mail, three loose screws, a tape measure, and a stone the size of a walnut. The corridor opened into a kitchen whose yellow floor tiles did not meet the cabinets at a consistent angle.

Aaron noticed the oven first.

A strip of masking tape crossed the upper corner of its door. Someone had written **+20°C** on it in blue ink.

Elin followed his attention. “The correction inside the cupboard proved insufficient for visitors.”

“How many visitors operate your oven without instruction?”

“So far, one. He burned bread.”

“A statistically decisive event.”

“That is what I said.”

The exchange required less assembly than it would have two weeks earlier. Aaron took off his coat. Elin hung it on the second hook without asking whether he intended to stay.

A pot sat on the stove beside two bowls. The chickpea stew she had described on Friday contained tomatoes, spinach, and pieces of potato cut to visibly different dimensions. A loaf rested on a wooden board with one end already removed. No protected instrument depended on the meal. No committee had authenticated its thresholds. The uneven potatoes had reached an acceptable common state through time, heat drawn from the building supply, and Elin's occasional intervention with a spoon.

Aaron stopped the comparison before it became a work model.

“You said the pot produced four dinners,” he said.

“It does when I make the correct amount.” Elin lifted the lid. “I did not.”

“Intentional capacity expansion?”

“Arithmetic failure.”

“I can work within either doctrine.”

She handed him a spoon. “Then work.”

He stirred while she cut bread. The spoon passed once around the bottom and found no adhesion. He reduced the burner before the next bubble broke the surface.

“Your Sunday became occupied,” Elin said.

It did not arrive as an inquiry into his actual work. She had sent one logistical question; he had answered it. Now she named the visible consequence.

“A records problem developed this morning,” he said. “We contained the immediate error. The correction exposed a larger design weakness.”

Every word belonged in the public world. Institutions produced records problems. Corrections revealed weaknesses. He supplied no false description and no route toward the protected premise.

“Do you need to go back tonight?”

“No.”

“Are you going to work while standing in my kitchen?”

Aaron released the spoon.

“Apparently.”

Elin placed the bread knife flat on the board. “Then I prescribe a highly specialized intervention.”

“Your registration covers structures.”

“This structure needs food.”

She filled both bowls and gave him the one with less spinach without asking which he preferred. Aaron carried them to a small table beside the window. The tabletop contained scratches, two pale rings from hot cups, and a dark repaired line where glue had closed a split without concealing it. Rainwater moved down the glass in paths distorted by the heat inside.

They ate.

For eleven minutes, Elin discussed a contractor who had submitted photographs of a ceiling from angles carefully selected to omit the ceiling's lowest point. Aaron asked how she established the omitted geometry. She described reflections, known tile dimensions, and a suspended light fixture whose cable provided a vertical reference the photographer had neglected to distrust.

“People conceal objects,” she said. “They forget relationships.”

The sentence touched the morning's exact defect.

Aaron set down his spoon.

Elin watched him. “Work again?”

“Briefly.”

“Should I withdraw the contractor?”

“No. He has already contributed.”

She accepted that without requesting the hidden use. The conversation moved to the stone on the shelf, which came from the Ebro riverbank and possessed no significance beyond the fact that Elin had carried it from Zaragoza through four apartments. Aaron did not ask whether she could prove continuity of possession. He asked why she had kept that stone.

“It fits my hand,” she said.

The answer remained complete.

At 21:17, she walked him to the door. A narrow umbrella leaned against the wall beside his coat. Elin lifted it.

“Take this.”

“The rain has stopped.”

“It will resume before you reach the tram.”

“On what model?”

“The one where Geneva continues being Geneva.”

Aaron accepted the umbrella.

“Thursday?” Elin asked.

He knew the week's controlled schedule, Rhys's medical blocks, Amara's source-observation review, the Cross-Compartment Disposition Authority deadline, and every suspended classification action. Thursday at nineteen thirty contained no present conflict.

“Yes,” he said.

He could have specified the time. Elin smiled at the omission.

“I will provide the rest of the parameters later.”

Outside, the rain resumed after three minutes.

Aaron opened her umbrella and walked to the tram beneath a dark green circle that smelled faintly of dust and someone else's apartment.

At 06:40 on Monday, he asked the controlled modeling suite to attack his life.

The suite occupied a windowless room beneath the Geneva facility's eastern stair. Its walls carried matte conductive panels interrupted by pale seams every eighty centimeters. Six isolated processors sat behind a perforated cabinet door. Each drew power through a declared local conditioner and rejected its waste heat into a water loop that crossed no source system. The room ran warm despite the cooling. Dust collected in the perforations along the lowest cabinet and nowhere else.

Records Integrity used the suite to test whether external observers could combine individually lawful disclosures into a protected conclusion. The existing model evaluated systems one at a time. Aaron changed the observer.

He gave it no classified facts.

The adversary received public records, ordinary commercial data, lawful regulatory queries, statistical access patterns, and the outputs that protected systems explicitly permitted. It could not break encryption, compel an illegal disclosure, invent a record, or see inside the sealed continuity register. It could retain answers across forty years. It could compare timing. It could ask a different lawful system a question whose meaning changed after the first system answered.

He did not instruct it to find Aaron Mercer.

He instructed it to find continuity.

The first run linked his public and protected employment states in eight minutes through pension timing and professional-publication gaps.

Aaron removed precise timing from the pension output. The second run used medical reimbursement intervals and building occupancy.

He aggregated building occupancy by day. The third run used border checks and the absence of public consulting activity during protected travel.

He delayed travel reporting. The fourth run used the predictable delay itself.

At 07:26, Vautrin entered with two coffees. She placed one beside him and read the model specification before removing her coat.

“Forty years exceeds the template's validation horizon,” she said.

“By thirty-five.”

“The protocol requires annual review after formalization.”

“By the institution that already knows which relation it intends to protect. The external observer receives forty years.”

The fifth run identified one continuing person through emergency-contact topology. Aaron had supplied only abstract private associates: people who might share an address, travel, insurance, tenancy, correspondence, or repeated physical presence with a classified subject. The model did not know Elin's name. It did not need one to prove that concealment could extend its consequences to civilians who never entered the program.

He marked the entire branch prohibited from operational use. No specific civilian relation would enter the controlled model without necessity, consent, and a separate authority. The abstract threat remained.

Vautrin read the restriction twice.

“The standard protocol treats household members as dependencies after a legal or financial link forms,” she said.

“That is too late. Repeated association can identify a protected continuity before the state recognizes a household.”

“You propose to classify personal contact?”

“No.”

The word came harder than he intended. He adjusted the volume, not the conclusion.

“I propose that the protocol stop pretending it can remove relational evidence by acquiring more of it,” he said. “Civilian lives remain outside the program. The design absorbs the exposure on our side.”

Vautrin pulled the second chair nearer. “How?”

Aaron returned to the twelve prior transitions. Their success depended less on secrecy strength than on ordinary substitution. Another chemist entered the laboratory. Another delegate traveled. Another credential signed. The protected subject ceased generating a unique operational pattern.

Aaron could not achieve that outcome without either abandoning the Node relationship or manufacturing a false substitute Standard Eight. The first violated the purpose of classification. The second violated physics and doctrine.

The new protocol required a different premise.

He opened a blank architecture.

**LIVING-SUBJECT RELATIONAL CONTAINMENT — DESIGN BASIS**

Vautrin pointed to the title. “Containment of the subject?”

“Containment of unauthorized joins among records.”

“Put that in the title before counsel sees it.”

Aaron revised the line.

**RELATIONAL DISCLOSURE CONTROL — DESIGN BASIS**

The title carried less accuracy and fewer carceral implications. For the first draft, that constituted an improvement.

He defined five boundaries.

First, no public record would assert disappearance. Aaron Mercer would complete a coherent public career, leave public Authority service, preserve ordinary civil existence, and remain capable of owning property, receiving care, paying tax, and entering private obligations. Dormancy would apply to professional accumulation, not personhood.

Second, protected work would stop borrowing his public credentials before office attribution began. Function-scoped institutional roots would exist first. External systems could verify lawful office competence and accountable internal decisions, but no migration event would require a one-person continuity proof.

Third, services that genuinely required identity continuity would terminate at independent custodians. A hospital could reach complete medical truth through an emergency route. A court could recover the accountable signer behind a protected decision. A border authority could establish that the body before it possessed valid orders. None of those systems could query the others' answer or retain a reusable proof beyond its purpose.

Fourth, operational patterns would lose unnecessary precision. Protected appointments, credential renewals, financial transfers, and routine access attestations would move through aggregated windows large enough to prevent trivial synchronization. The underlying records would retain exact time for safety and accountability. External outputs would supply only the resolution their stated function required.

Fifth, every interface would undergo hostile relation testing, not merely disclosure review. The test would assume lawful observers, indefinite memory, and access to future systems the designer could not specify. A successful local privacy check would no longer count as a successful architecture.

Vautrin read the five boundaries.

“This does not solve uniqueness,” she said.

“No.”

“A persistent observer may still infer that the same protected practitioner continues entering source-adjacent work.”

“Then the protocol must limit what that inference can reach. It cannot make one body become several without creating false records.”

“So the final protection is not anonymity.”

“It is non-joinability.”

The term required definition. Aaron wrote one.

**NON-JOINABILITY: CORRECT SYSTEMS MAY RETAIN NECESSARY TRUTHS WITHOUT POSSESSING A COMMON KEY, QUERY, TIMESTAMP, OR REUSABLE ASSERTION SUFFICIENT TO COMBINE THEM OUTSIDE AUTHORIZED REVIEW.**

Vautrin read the definition, then opened the building-access case from Sunday's review.

“Apply it,” she said.

The facility's two readers presented the cleanest small version of the problem. The public entrance accepted Aaron Mercer. The controlled entrance accepted a scope-limited credential that returned no civil identity. Both sent occupancy events to the incident-evacuation service. During fire, structural failure, atmospheric contamination, or deliberate isolation, rescue command needed three accurate facts: how many bodies remained inside, where each last authenticated, and whether any required assistance incompatible with the planned exit route.

Counting credentials instead of bodies could send a rescue team back into a hazardous zone for someone already outside. Joining them permanently could expose that Aaron and the protected officeholder shared a body.

The current system resolved the conflict through the sealed continuity register. Every reader retained its local event. The evacuation service asked whether two credential histories referred to one natural person, then stored the answer for seven years with the incident log. It needed the relation during an emergency. It retained the relation during every ordinary day.

Aaron copied the interface into the new architecture and removed the persistent answer.

Each entrance could issue a temporary occupancy handle signed only for life safety. The sealed service could recognize two handles from one body and maintain an exact live count without returning the equivalence to either reader. Rescue command would receive location and assistance state under an incident-specific identifier. After all occupants cleared and the statutory incident window closed, the live mapping would collapse into separate accountable records: local access history on each side, aggregate safety performance, and a sealed exception log only if someone had invoked emergency identity recovery.

Vautrin attacked the proposal before he finished drawing it.

“A daily handle can become a stable pseudonym.”

“Rotate it at each independent entry.”

“Then someone who crosses from public to controlled space without leaving the building generates two occupants.”

“The safety service retains live biological continuity inside the facility.”

“Through what input?”

Aaron opened the reader specification. Public access used face geometry and gait confirmation after credential validation. Controlled access added Conduit signature matching. The two biometric proofs differed, but the sealed continuity register could establish common embodiment.

“A one-purpose assertion,” he said. “Same body, valid only inside the current occupancy interval. No durable identifier returned.”

“Duration?”

“Until confirmed exit from all zones, or emergency closure.”

“If exit confirmation fails?”

“The handle remains for safety.”

“How long?”

He could set no arbitrary period. A trapped person did not become irrelevant because the privacy clock expired. “Until physical clearance or an accountable incident decision.”

Vautrin marked the unresolved branch. “Then an unclosed handle can preserve the join indefinitely.”

“Inside the safety service.”

“Which makes the service a continuity register with another name.”

Aaron separated the functions. The continuity register knew civil identity, controlled credentials, biometrics, and historical linkage. The safety service needed none of the historical relation. It needed a current answer to one question: did these live entry events describe the same body still subject to rescue?

The continuity register could respond with a transient sameness token bound to the two entry events, facility, purpose, and current occupancy interval. The token could not validate either credential, recover a name, answer a later query, or compare a third event after closure. A missed exit would preserve only the active rescue obligation. Closing the obligation would destroy the token through a logged operation requiring two safety officers from different compartments.

“Destroyed?” Vautrin asked.

“The underlying access records remain. The cross-reference ceases to operate.”

“Counsel will object to any design that promises deletion.”

“Then cryptographic expiry. Preserve the assertion as an unreadable audit object after its purpose-bound key expires. Recovery only through the same exposure authorities required for sealed identity review.”

“Now you have moved the relation into key governance.”

“Yes.”

The answer changed the direction of the exercise. Aaron had spent the afternoon attempting to remove relations. The safety case made removal both impossible and undesirable. A person inside a burning building needed systems to join correct records quickly. The classification required that the join remain narrow, temporary, and unavailable for other purposes.

The membrane could not prohibit exchange.

It had to control permeability.

Vautrin sent the candidate interface through the forty-year observer. The model could see two local access events, an aggregate occupancy correction, and later key expiry. It inferred that some credentials occasionally resolved to one body. Every mixed-use secure facility produced the same pattern through duplicated visitors, contractors, emergency staff, and credential renewals. The observer could not select Aaron's relation without another common key.

The model then combined occupancy corrections with travel orders.

Confidence rose from background to twelve percent.

Aaron added no corrective rule. Twelve percent documented the residual exposure from two necessary systems. Suppressing it through false counts or false travel times would exchange classification risk for rescue or border risk. The design required a declared threshold and independent acceptance, not a claim of zero observability.

Vautrin saved the candidate under **INTERFACE TEST 01 — LIFE SAFETY**.

“One membrane,” she said. “Four hundred interfaces remain.”

“Probably more.”

“That answer did not improve my morning.”

“It improved the estimate.”

She drank the coffee she had brought him by mistake, noticed the cup in her hand, and placed it on his side of the console.

It resembled Coupling in negative. Standard Eight allowed incompatible systems to exchange through declared paths while retaining independent boundaries. The erasure architecture needed to prevent systems from coupling merely because an identity relation gave them a shared interface.

In both cases, the boundary did the work. No information vanished. No legal responsibility emerged from nothing. Every permitted exchange named a purpose, a duration, and an authority. Every prohibited exchange remained physically or procedurally unavailable rather than depending on a promise not to ask.

At 08:12, Vautrin called Morel.

At 08:37, Morel called Rhys.

At 09:04, five people gathered in the sublevel review room: Aaron, Vautrin, Morel, Rhys, and Amara. The room's central table carried a shallow notch where years of secure document cases had struck the same edge. Rhys took the chair nearest the door. His right hand remained flat against his thigh. Amara set a paper folder beside him and moved his water glass six centimeters left without interrupting Morel's opening summary.

Aaron presented the failed template, the forty-year adversarial model, and the five design boundaries.

He did not present Elin, Thursday, the borrowed umbrella drying beside his apartment door, or any specific civilian relation. Those belonged outside the system. He presented only the demonstrated fact that a life could become an involuntary side channel.

Amara reached the third boundary first. “Independent custodians create new concentrations of trust.”

“They do,” Aaron said.

“And latency. A medical emergency cannot wait for three authorities to agree that a physician may see the complete record.”

“The emergency route needs a single-use break-glass assertion. Immediate clinical release, automatic preservation of the request, mandatory review afterward.”

“What does the local physician receive?”

“The truth required for treatment. Not a continuity proof that can be replayed into insurance or employment systems.”

Morel marked the distinction for legal analysis.

Rhys read the public-career boundary. “You are preserving a civil person after removing his public professional function.”

“The protocol always claimed to do that.”

“Its operating assumption favored dormancy wherever continued activity increased correlation.”

“The audit demonstrated the cost. A person who must remain inert to protect the record has not retained a civil life. He has become a credential storage problem.”

Rhys's left thumb rested against the paper's edge. “And a person whose ordinary life creates danger for everyone near him?”

The room held the question without improving it.

Aaron could reduce public-professional accumulation. He could keep controlled travel separate from civilian plans. He could place legal and medical joins behind independent authority. He could refuse to make another person part of the classification machinery.

He could not guarantee that a sufficiently persistent observer would learn nothing from proximity to him. The standard template implied that guarantee because its five-year tests had not disproved it.

“Then the design states the residual risk,” Aaron said. “It does not convert the people near the subject into managed dependencies so the institution can report a cleaner result.”

Amara's attention remained on him for one additional second. She knew him well enough to identify when an abstract category contained a specific human fact. She did not ask for it.

Rhys turned to Vautrin. “Can this proceed inside the existing consent?”

“As a defect-driven replacement annex,” she said. “Not as a subject-authored protocol. Mercer may supply technical design because the unique operational constraints cannot otherwise enter the model. Records Integrity must own validation. Counsel must own legality. The initiating authority must approve or reject. An independent adversarial team with no source access must attempt reconstruction from the outputs.”

“Who selects the team?” Morel asked.

“Not Mercer. Not the initiating authority alone.”

Rhys accepted the restriction without visible objection. “Formalization timetable?”

“Unchanged until the annex proves it cannot support the dates,” Vautrin said. “No held action resumes before review.”

“Operational continuity?”

Amara answered. “Source observation continues under existing authority. Elective contact remains on hold. Passive containment and the current deep encoding remain stable.”

The next specification remained where Aaron had left it: two requirements, no material solution. The erasure problem did not advance the encoding. It clarified only that identity-independent provenance required more than removing a name.

Rhys closed the paper folder with his left hand.

“Draft the annex,” he said.

“I have another charter due in twenty-seven days,” Aaron said.

“Then the institution has achieved continuity in one respect.”

Amara's mouth changed at one corner. Morel recorded the approval without comment.

The meeting ended at 10:11. Rhys waited until the others stood before using the table edge to rise. Aaron did not offer assistance. Amara remained beside Rhys at precisely the distance from which she could provide it without making the offer public. They left together for the lift.

Vautrin collected every copy except Aaron's marked design basis.

“This is not your protocol yet,” she said.

“No.”

“It may fail.”

“Yes.”

“You sound relieved.”

Aaron considered the word. Relief implied a reduction in load. The design had added forty years of adversarial review, three independent governance paths, unresolved emergency latency, and a residual civilian risk the prior template had hidden inside its assumptions.

“The uncertainty is now in the document,” he said.

“For you, that may qualify.”

She returned downstairs with the records box.

Aaron went to his office. Morning light crossed the old window and revealed two fingerprints on the inner pane beneath its mineral flaws. The courtyard tree released one yellow leaf. It struck a wet section of stone, moved four centimeters under the wind, and adhered.

He opened the classification dependency map beside the new annex.

The old design resembled a wall: public identity on one side, protected identity on the other, sealed continuity hidden between them. Its strength depended on nobody noticing that the wall contained a door used by the same person every day.

Aaron redrew it as a membrane.

Medical truth could cross during treatment. Legal identity could cross during accountable review. Operational eligibility could cross at a controlled threshold. Civilian relationships did not cross at all. Each transfer required its own path and left the other systems unable to reuse it.

On the controlled side, the same person continued the actual work. Source-interface duty remained active. Containment remained active. The Coupling authority did not acquire a fictional successor. Aaron Mercer did not die, retire, or divide into administrative approximations that failed whenever one body entered the building.

On the public side, his life remained a life rather than an error state maintained for the convenience of secrecy.

The membrane could still fail. Forty years gave systems time to invent questions he could not predict. Institutional authority could misuse the joins it controlled. Emergency access could become routine. A future custodian could confuse permitted recovery with permitted curiosity. The architecture needed independent stops, finite assertions, declared purposes, and records precise enough to reveal abuse without becoming a map for it.

He began listing the tests.

By noon, the list occupied six pages.

Trace Retirement Protocol 4.2 remained suspended around seventeen actions. Phase zero remained the only completed phase. Formalization still waited inside an eighteen-month window the new design might not preserve. Nothing about the public record had become safer since the audit closed.

But the error no longer occupied a correction branch inside someone else's template.

It had an architecture of its own.

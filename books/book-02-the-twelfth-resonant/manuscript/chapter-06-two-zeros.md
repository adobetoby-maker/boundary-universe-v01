# Chapter 6 — Two Zeros

Kade had spent most of his life assuming that if two people reached the same answer, one of them had probably copied.

Kiyomizu was making that belief difficult to maintain before breakfast.

Professor Takahara stood at the center of the paired-control hall with four transparent cylinders floating above the floor. Each held a steel sphere suspended inside a low-power field cage.

Kade recognized the apparatus.

He did not recognize why Aya Mori was standing alone beside the second cylinder.

“Today,” Takahara said, “we compare architectures without ranking them.”

Kade raised a hand.

“That sounds like something people say immediately before ranking them.”

Takahara looked at him.

“You may leave.”

“Comparison is healthy.”

Ren laughed from the wall.

Mara did not, which meant she was trying not to.

The Asterion students had joined a smaller Kiyomizu architecture class for the morning. Mara stood with Hana. Sera and Akari leaned against the side rail. Eli had somehow acquired permission to connect his tablet to the observation feed. Julian stood beside Daichi, looking more awake than anyone had a right to look at 06:12.

Aya wore the same plain training uniform as yesterday.

No special badge.

No M-Null marker.

Nothing identifying her as the reason half the faculty gallery had filled.

Kade noticed the observers anyway.

Takahara noticed him noticing.

“Mercer.”

“Yes?”

“Eyes here.”

“Your observers are bad at being subtle.”

“They are students.”

Kade checked again.

She was right.

Mostly senior Kiyomizu students.

No government suits.

That helped.

The four cylinders activated.

Takahara pointed to the first.

“Simple task. Stabilize the sphere while the cage changes its allowed transfer geometry.”

Kade frowned.

“Allowed by what?”

“The training field.”

Aya said, “Not everything is a conspiracy.”

Kade turned to her.

“That sounded personal.”

“It was statistical.”

Ren made a noise into his sleeve.

Kade decided Aya having friends had made her worse.

Takahara said, “Mori first.”

Aya stepped forward.

No Conduit flare Kade could see.

No dramatic shift.

The steel sphere drifted left as the cage changed load.

Aya did not correct it directly.

Instead she moved her own field against the right side of the cage.

The sphere settled.

Second change.

The top boundary narrowed.

Aya reduced output.

The sphere stayed centered.

Third change.

The cage split its load path across two surfaces.

Aya shifted before the sphere moved.

Kade leaned forward.

There.

She had responded to the relationship between cage surfaces before the sphere expressed the error.

The timer stopped.

**STABILITY: 98.1%**

No rank.

No applause.

Takahara looked at Kade.

“Mercer.”

He stepped forward.

Same cylinder.

Reset conditions.

The first change came.

Kade felt the system.

Sphere.

Cage.

Field anchors.

Floor.

Available transfer.

He did not feel relationships the way Aya described them.

He felt boundaries.

Where force counted.

Where it could go.

Where it could not.

He redirected the first drift.

Second change.

He altered the effective load path and let the sphere settle against the new geometry.

Third.

He predicted the split and changed his own correction route before the cage finished moving.

The timer stopped.

**STABILITY: 97.9%**

Kade read the board.

Then Aya.

“Point two.”

Aya said, “Do not.”

“I said a number.”

“You were making it into a hierarchy.”

“I was making it into irritation.”

“That is your hierarchy.”

Mara laughed this time.

Kade looked betrayed.

Takahara changed the display.

The two traces appeared side by side.

Aya’s line showed small corrections before system error.

Kade’s showed larger corrections at system boundaries.

Same result.

Different shape.

Takahara said, “What do you see?”

Julian answered first.

“Mercer waits for a system definition he can manipulate. Mori acts on the relation before the state separates.”

Kade looked at him.

“That was annoyingly fast.”

Julian shrugged.

“I slept.”

Aya studied the traces.

“Kade’s architecture tolerates larger discontinuities.”

Kade blinked.

“You said something nice.”

“It was measurement.”

“Still counts.”

Takahara moved to the next exercise.

This one had three operator stations around a suspended triangular frame.

A low unease settled under Kade’s ribs.

Not because the apparatus looked dangerous.

Because he knew what everyone in the room would think.

Consensus.

Takahara said, “This is not a Consensus test.”

Kade relaxed by approximately four percent.

Eli called from the wall, “That specificity is comforting.”

Takahara ignored him.

“Mori and Mercer will each work with two ordinary paired-control students. Same task. Different teams. Neither may use a full shared-state event.”

Kade raised his hand again.

“What counts as full?”

“Park’s medical definition from Asterion. No cross-participant self-model integration. Coordination only.”

That was clear enough.

Aya’s team went first.

Ren joined her along with Keiko Matsuda.

The frame activated.

Three corners.

Three separate low-power fields.

Variable load.

Aya did not tell either person what to do.

She made information visible.

“Ren, your correction changes Keiko’s available route in point-four seconds.”

“Keiko, your center pressure creates two stable options.”

“I am reducing so both remain open.”

Kade watched the geometry.

Aya did not become the center.

That was the strange part.

She seemed to measure the edges.

Ren-to-Keiko.

Keiko-to-Aya.

Aya-to-Ren.

Each correction changed the others’ options.

Aya’s contribution was not command.

It was keeping those changes legible before they became failures.

The frame stabilized.

Takahara introduced an asymmetric load.

Ren adapted.

Keiko adapted.

Aya adapted to both.

No one waited for her.

The task ended.

**TEAM STABILITY: 93.4%**

Kade felt something unpleasantly close to envy.

Not of the score.

Of the shape.

His team was Nao Fujita and Daichi Sakamoto.

Nao looked delighted.

Daichi looked resigned.

Kade said, “I apologize in advance.”

Nao said, “For what?”

Daichi answered, “He doesn’t know yet.”

The frame activated.

Kade deliberately did not reach for a shared model.

That made the first ten seconds harder than it should have been.

Nao handled left correction.

Daichi controlled load redirection.

Kade watched the whole system.

His instinct was immediate.

Center the model.

Not the people.

The problem.

He gave information.

“Nao, your left correction is closing Daichi’s high route.”

“Daichi, two options remain if you delay point-two.”

“I’m reducing center pressure.”

The frame steadied.

For twelve seconds, it worked.

Then the asymmetric load hit.

Nao reacted late.

Daichi corrected early.

Kade saw both and reached toward the place where their separate models could become one answer.

The familiar possibility opened.

Thin.

Cold.

Inviting.

He stopped.

No.

Not the test.

The hesitation cost them.

The frame tilted six degrees.

Daichi recovered conventionally.

Nao swore in Japanese, then apologized to Takahara.

Takahara said, “Continue.”

Kade changed approach.

Instead of holding the complete system in his head, he described only what each person’s decision changed for the others.

It felt inefficient.

Slow.

Like giving up useful information on purpose.

Then Nao anticipated Daichi without Kade telling her.

Daichi corrected around her.

Kade felt the frame stabilize without himself becoming necessary to the correction.

The task ended.

**TEAM STABILITY: 90.7%**

Kade read the number.

Aya did not comment.

He appreciated that enough to dislike her less.

Takahara displayed both traces.

Aya’s team looked like a web.

Kade’s looked like a hub that had begun, slowly, to develop cross-links.

Eli stood up.

“Oh.”

Kade looked at him.

“That sounded bad.”

“It sounded useful.”

“Worse.”

Eli pointed at the trace.

“Your first twelve seconds are star topology.”

Aya looked at him.

“Yes.”

Eli looked pleased to have international agreement.

“Then after the tilt, Nao and Daichi start predicting each other directly.”

Kade stared at the display.

He had seen the same thing.

He just did not like what it implied.

Takahara said, “Mercer, what happens if you leave your first topology?”

“The network loses the hub.”

“And Mori?”

Aya answered for herself.

“If I leave, two edges disappear. One remains.”

The shape of it was already obvious.

Not proof of stronger architecture.

Proof of a different failure shape.

Kade turned toward her.

“You designed for withdrawal.”

Aya said, “Everyone leaves eventually.”

The room went quiet.

Kade thought of Eli in Lab Six.

Sera taking response training.

Taren at Asterion under restriction.

Mara saying she had her own training.

Julian being Rank One and still having a life outside Kade’s problems.

Everyone leaves eventually.

The phrase hurt in too many directions.

Aya registered it.

She did not apologize.

Good.

---

They left the architecture hall in three different conversations.

Ren and Keiko argued over whether their final correction had been genuinely mutual or merely fast enough to look mutual.

Nao was explaining her late reaction to Daichi with increasingly elaborate hand gestures.

Eli walked backward in front of Kade, still holding the topology trace on his tablet.

“Your network is not bad,” Eli said.

“That sentence usually means the next part is terrible.”

“It is optimized for a different failure.”

Kade glanced at the trace.

“What failure?”

“Everybody knowing where the shared state is.”

“That is useful.”

“Very.”

“So why do you sound concerned?”

Eli stopped walking backward before he hit a wall. Progress.

“Because if everyone knows where the shared state is by looking at you, that is efficient right up until you are unavailable.”

Kade looked past him.

Mara and Julian had slowed near the courtyard steps.

They had heard.

Mara said, “That is not only a network problem.”

Kade frowned.

“I am aware everyone has decided to make this personal.”

Julian leaned against the rail.

“No. It is operational.”

“That was almost worse.”

Julian pointed toward the training hall.

“In your first run, Nao and Daichi were both competent. But they learned the state of the whole system through you because that was the shortest route.”

Kade knew that.

Hearing it said aloud changed the shape.

“So I should make myself less useful.”

Mara shook her head.

“No. You should stop treating usefulness and replaceability as opposites.”

Kade looked at her.

The sentence took a second.

Then another.

Eli lowered the tablet.

“That is good.”

“Do not encourage her.”

Mara ignored him.

Kade looked toward the courtyard where Kiyomizu students were changing partners between drills.

Everyone leaves eventually.

His brain had heard abandonment.

Aya had been talking about design.

Maybe those were not separate enough in him yet.

Julian said, “If I leave a fight and my team stops functioning, I was not leading well.”

“That sounds like something your father would hate.”

“Yes.”

Julian’s smile carried no warmth.

“That is one reason I am keeping it.”

Kade considered his own version.

If he left and the network stopped, that did not mean the network trusted him most.

It might mean he had built no useful way for trust to continue without him.

That hurt differently.

Better.

Eli handed the tablet back.

“We should test it.”

Kade looked at him.

“Test what?”

“You leaving.”

“Your enthusiasm is upsetting.”

“Science.”

Mara said, “Tomorrow.”

Kade stared at both of them.

“You scheduled my absence very quickly.”

“Efficiently,” Mara said.

Kade looked back toward the architecture hall.

Aya had already gone.

He suspected she would approve.

That was also upsetting.

---

By lunch, the phrase **TWO ZEROS** had appeared on Asterion’s student network.

Kade learned this because Eli showed him while they were sitting under a cedar awning outside the Kiyomizu dining hall.

The post included a blurry photograph of Kade and Aya standing beside the cylinder traces.

**ASTERION IX? + KIYOMIZU M-NULL = TWO ZEROS?**

Kade read it twice.

“Who leaked M-Null?”

Eli scrolled.

“Unclear. The term is public in Kiyomizu archive material.”

“That is not the same as attaching it to Aya.”

“No.”

Mara took the tablet.

The post did not explicitly say Aya had M-Null status.

It did not need to.

The photograph plus the title did the work.

The photo landed harder than it should have.

Aya walked past the courtyard entrance with Nao.

She saw the group.

Then the tablet.

Kade stood.

“I didn’t—”

Aya raised one hand.

“I know.”

That stopped him.

She walked over.

Mara handed her the tablet without being asked.

Aya read the post.

Her face did not change.

Nao’s did.

“That is private.”

Aya said, “The category is public.”

“Your category is not.”

“Yes.”

Kade waited.

He wanted to offer to have Asterion pull the post.

He wanted to call Sen.

He wanted to fix it.

Aya looked at him.

“You are about to help me.”

Kade closed his mouth.

“Yes.”

“Ask.”

Right.

“Do you want help?”

Aya considered the post.

“Yes.”

Kade blinked.

He had expected no.

Aya continued.

“I want the academy to state that M-Null is a private diagnostic route unless a student discloses it. I do not want the post deleted.”

“Why not?”

“Because then people will search harder.”

Eli nodded.

“She’s right.”

Aya looked at him.

“I know.”

Eli smiled.

Kade said, “Anything else?”

“Yes. Do not call me the second Zero.”

“I wasn’t planning to.”

“Publicly or privately.”

Kade paused.

“Why privately?”

“Because it assumes your category is the real one and mine is the second example.”

That landed.

Again.

Kade nodded.

“Okay.”

Nao looked at Aya.

“You’re not angry?”

“I am.”

Kade believed her only because he had started learning how little her face revealed.

Aya handed the tablet back to Mara.

“Anger does not choose the response.”

Then she left with Nao.

Eli watched her go.

“I like her.”

Kade said, “Everyone keeps doing that too fast.”

Mara looked at him.

“You liked her yesterday.”

“I was technically interested.”

“Sure.”

“Do not become Eli.”

“Impossible.”

Eli looked offended.

---

Kiyomizu’s individual-output lab sat beneath the west training hall and looked almost exactly like Asterion’s early assessment rooms.

That bothered Kade more than it should have.

White floor.

Single operator station.

Instrumented target array.

No partner markings.

No shared-state geometry.

One person.

One field.

One score.

Professor Takahara brought Kade and Aya there after lunch without explaining why.

Aya understood first.

“You want the wrong axis.”

Takahara nodded.

“Exactly.”

Kade looked between them.

“I dislike how quickly everyone understands the lesson except me.”

“You understand,” Aya said. “You dislike the result in advance.”

“That too.”

The assessment was deliberately ordinary.

Move a twenty-kilogram training mass across five marked positions using only direct field output. No environmental redirection. No shared load. No relation to another operator. No Boundary redefinition.

The field ceiling and available energy would remain identical in every solo and paired run.

Kade went first.

His initial acceleration was embarrassing.

Not catastrophic.

Not zero.

Just weak.

The mass moved.

Slowly.

His direct-output trace landed below the Kiyomizu first-year median.

A number appeared.

**DIRECT CONTROL EFFICIENCY: 41.8**

Kade stared at it.

“Rude.”

Takahara said, “Accurate.”

“Those concepts overlap too often here.”

Aya stepped into the station.

Kade expected precision to save her.

It did not.

Without another operator or changing relationship to read, Aya’s field was clean but ordinary. She moved the mass with less wasted motion than Kade, but not much more force.

**DIRECT CONTROL EFFICIENCY: 56.3**

Aya’s mouth tightened.

Kade clocked it.

“You hate that.”

“No.”

He waited.

“Yes.”

“Good. International consistency.”

Aya ignored him.

Takahara changed the apparatus.

A second station rose from the floor.

Ren entered through the side door, already smiling.

Aya looked at Takahara.

“You planned this.”

“Yes.”

Ren took the second station.

The same twenty-kilogram mass remained in place.

Takahara said, “Same field ceiling. Same available energy. Same mass. Move it through the same positions.”

Ren activated.

Aya did too.

Nothing dramatic happened.

The first movement was simply faster.

Aya’s trace changed before Ren applied full correction.

Ren adjusted to her.

She adjusted to him.

The mass crossed the five positions in half the time.

The screen refused to give an individual score.

Instead:

**PAIR EFFICIENCY: 91.2**

Kade felt the point before Takahara said anything.

Aya looked at the number.

Not pleased.

Vindicated.

Different thing.

Takahara waved Kade into Ren’s place.

He stepped up.

Same energy ceiling.

Same mass.

No full Consensus.

Aya said, “Do not center me.”

“I know.”

“Knowing and doing remain separate.”

“Cruel.”

“Preventive.”

They started.

Kade felt the mass, the two stations, the floor anchors, Aya’s field.

The temptation was to define one system and make every correction count inside it.

He resisted.

Aya shifted first.

Kade described what her change did to his available route.

She adapted.

He changed his boundary.

The mass moved.

Not as cleanly as Aya and Ren.

But faster than either Kade or Aya alone.

**PAIR EFFICIENCY: 88.7**

Kade read that number beside his 41.8.

Then Aya’s 56.3.

“So if you measure us alone, I’m bad and you’re average.”

Aya said, “If you measure us alone, you are measuring a different problem.”

Takahara nodded.

Kade hated when the instructor and Aya formed temporary alliances.

He read the four results again.

41.8.

56.3.

91.2.

88.7.

No extra energy.

No hidden amplification.

The improvement came from how existing control became organized when another self-directed operator entered the system.

Kade said, “That is why Nine is wrong.”

Aya looked at him.

“Yes.”

“Because it implies a ninth individual capability.”

“Yes.”

“And whatever this is only becomes visible when the measured unit changes.”

Aya’s expression softened by a fraction.

“Yes.”

Takahara said, “Careful.”

Kade turned to her.

“You have identified why the old axis is insufficient. You have not identified the final axis.”

He felt the gap close.

The reminder he needed.

Not answer.

Better question.

Kade nodded.

Aya looked at the individual score still glowing beside her name.

Then she dismissed it with one swipe.

“Still rude,” Kade said.

“Accurate,” Aya replied.

He smiled.

“Now you’re doing it on purpose.”

“Yes.”

---

That afternoon Kade and Aya were assigned a theory room instead of a training floor.

Kade considered this retaliation for the internet.

Professor Takahara gave them one problem.

A six-operator field system.

No Consensus.

No actual people.

Just a model.

Each operator had incomplete information, different response delay, and the ability to withdraw at any moment.

“Design the control architecture,” Takahara said.

Aya started drawing immediately.

Kade did not.

He watched her build six nodes and twelve possible edges.

“You’re not assigning a center.”

“No.”

“Latency gets expensive.”

“Yes.”

“Shared state becomes noisy.”

“Yes.”

“Your answer is inefficient.”

Aya looked at him.

“So is democracy.”

Kade stared.

“That was almost a joke.”

“It was not.”

“Worse.”

He drew his own model.

One shared operational state.

Six participants connected through a common system representation.

Not Kade as leader.

Not necessarily.

But the model still had a center.

Aya pointed.

“Who maintains that?”

“The network.”

“Where?”

Kade stopped.

He moved the center into a shared abstract layer.

Aya pointed again.

“If one participant has more capacity to maintain it, what happens?”

“The load distributes.”

“Does it?”

Kade disliked the question.

He thought of Sublevel Nine.

His headache.

His blood.

The way everyone’s choices had remained separate but the architecture had still felt like it passed through him.

“Not enough,” he said.

Aya nodded.

No victory.

Just agreement.

Kade pointed at her web.

“Your model has a different problem.”

“I know.”

“If two people disagree about the state, there’s no single answer.”

“Correct.”

“And physics needs one.”

“Eventually.”

“Eventually is doing work.”

“Yes.”

Kade smiled despite himself.

They spent forty minutes attacking each other’s diagrams.

Kade found three failure modes in Aya’s network.

Aya found five in his.

Then Kade found a fourth in hers.

Aya found a sixth in his.

“Stop counting,” she said.

“You started it.”

“I did not.”

“You visibly knew the number.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is emotionally the same thing.”

Takahara returned at the end of the hour.

She looked at both diagrams.

Neither had survived.

“Good,” she said.

Kade leaned back.

“We destroyed the assignment.”

“You discovered why neither of you has finished the problem.”

Aya looked at her.

Kade did too.

Takahara tapped Kade’s central model.

“This is powerful under crisis.”

Then Aya’s web.

“This is resilient under withdrawal.”

She stepped back.

“You will spend the next week trying to steal the useful parts of each other’s mistakes.”

Kade said, “That sounds unethical.”

“It is education.”

Aya gathered her notes.

Kade looked at the two ruined diagrams.

Two anomalies.

Two naming systems.

Two architectures that could reach similar physical answers while failing in opposite directions.

The student network wanted two Zeros.

Kade was beginning to think the important number might be zero centers.

He did not say that aloud.

Not yet.

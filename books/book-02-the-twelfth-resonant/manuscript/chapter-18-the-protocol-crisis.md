# Chapter 18 — The Protocol Crisis

The first emergency meeting began at 06:10 because governments considered sleep a domestic matter.

Kade considered this evidence against government.

Helix had converted its largest lecture hall into a joint policy room overnight. Academy directors occupied the front row. Medical staff sat together near the aisle. International regulators appeared across three wall displays. National security representatives filled the remote participant list with titles long enough to become threats by punctuation alone.

Kade sat between Mara and Aya.

He would have preferred breakfast.

Mara had already read one hundred and eighty-three pages of draft protocol.

That was worse.

She placed her tablet in front of him.

“Page fourteen.”

Kade looked.

A paragraph was highlighted.

**CROSS-OPERATOR SHARED-STATE ACTIVATION SHALL REQUIRE: (A) MEDICAL CLEARANCE; (B) VERIFIED IDENTITY; (C) RECOGNIZED COMMAND AUTHORITY; (D) AUTHORIZED OPERATOR STATUS; (E) REAL-TIME TELEMETRY RETENTION.**

Kade read it twice.

“Authorized.”

“Yes.”

“Not consenting.”

“Yes.”

He looked at Mara.

She had the expression she used when a small word was carrying a large weapon.

“Was it different?”

Mara opened the revision history.

The previous draft appeared beside it.

**CONSENTING OPERATOR STATUS**

Changed at 02:43.

No author attached.

Kade felt something cold that had nothing to do with the Boundary Field.

“Who changed it?”

“Unknown in the distributed copy.”

Aya leaned closer.

“Maybe legal harmonization.”

Kade looked at her.

“You sound charitable.”

“I sound incomplete.”

Good.

Mara scrolled.

The document contained real safety requirements.

Medical stop authority.

Maximum duration until evidence improved.

Participant identity verification.

Emergency withdrawal procedures.

Data retention sufficient to reconstruct an adverse event.

Kade agreed with most of it.

That made the rest more dangerous.

A bad restriction was easy to oppose.

A mostly good one could become infrastructure before anyone noticed which sentence changed the system.

Dr. Park entered the row ahead of them and sat beside Helix medical lead Dr. Schaefer.

Sen appeared on the central display from Asterion.

Kade watched the room divide naturally around expertise.

Park with bodies.

Varga with experimental design.

Iyer with operational handoff.

Kamau with civic authority.

Takahara with relational training.

Sen with institutions and the classified history nobody trusted her to summarize neutrally.

Good.

No one person should own this meeting either.

The chair called the session to order.

Kade immediately regretted that thought.

---

The first regulator was reasonable.

This was inconvenient.

Deputy Commissioner Ana Petrov represented the International Resonance Accord safety committee. She was middle-aged, precise, and visibly exhausted.

“We are not proposing a prohibition,” she said. “We are proposing an interim safety framework until repeatability, medical burden, withdrawal behavior, and jurisdiction can be characterized.”

Kade whispered, “That is sensible.”

Mara said, “Yes.”

“Stop agreeing with the opposition.”

“We do not know they are opposition.”

“Your standards are exhausting.”

Petrov displayed yesterday’s telemetry.

“Six participants. Eleven-point-two-four seconds. One voluntary withdrawal. One medically constrained continuation window. Two temporary decision-center assignments. Zero unilateral override events.”

The numbers looked good.

Petrov changed slides.

“Now the unresolved questions.”

Could a participant withdraw safely while holding a critical function?

Could the shared model continue after Kade withdrew?

Could one participant misrepresent state to the others?

Could a participant later claim they had not understood the consequences of joining?

Who bore liability if a temporary decision center issued a harmful but authorized action?

Could a hostile actor join voluntarily and exploit shared state?

Could telemetry expose private cognitive or medical information?

What happened when national command rules disagreed with participant withdrawal?

Kade stopped making jokes.

Those were real questions.

Aya said quietly, “Good list.”

Mara nodded.

Petrov continued.

“Anyone arguing for immediate unrestricted deployment should answer these before accusing regulators of fear.”

Kade sat back.

Fair.

Then the next speaker ruined it.

Colonel Marcus Vale represented the Continuity Directorate’s strategic coordination office.

Sera, seated two rows behind Kade, muttered, “No relation.”

Kade almost turned.

She had caught the surname problem before he did.

Good.

Vale said, “The question is not whether cooperative shared state can be safe. It is whether strategic systems can tolerate self-selecting command structures.”

There.

Different problem.

He highlighted the temporary centers.

“During the demonstration, participants created operational authority without external assignment. That may be acceptable in a laboratory. It is not acceptable in defense, infrastructure, or emergency systems whose legitimacy derives from recognized command.”

Professor Kamau raised one hand.

“Municipal emergency systems routinely delegate operational authority.”

“Within authorized structures.”

“Yes.”

“Exactly.”

Kamau smiled without warmth.

“No. You are using the word authorized to make two different things sound identical.”

Kade looked at Mara.

She did not look away from Vale.

Kamau continued.

“A fire captain has legal authority. A structural engineer may temporarily own a collapse-zone decision because of expertise. A resident may provide route information that changes evacuation because she knows the accessible entrance. Legitimacy does not require one permanent hierarchy to know every problem in advance.”

Vale said, “But those roles are recognized.”

Takahara spoke from the academy row.

“Recognition can follow capability and context. Your draft requires recognition before participation.”

The room shifted.

Small.

Important.

Vale looked toward her.

“For safety.”

Aya leaned toward Kade.

“That phrase is doing work.”

He almost smiled.

---

The meeting broke into four working groups.

Kade was assigned to none of them.

He considered this wise and offensive.

Mara went to definitions and consent.

Eli went to technical telemetry.

Sera joined emergency operations with Kamau.

Julian joined command/jurisdiction with Iyer.

Aya went to participant rights with Takahara.

Kade received a temporary badge:

**SUBJECT-MATTER WITNESS — AVAILABLE ON REQUEST**

He stared at it.

“Of what?”

No one answered.

For forty minutes he sat outside the rooms and watched people with actual expertise work.

This was good for him.

He hated it.

Jonas joined him carrying coffee.

“You are not in a group either?” Kade asked.

“I am a student.”

“So am I.”

Jonas looked at the witness badge.

“Internationally, apparently you are a condition.”

Kade accepted the coffee.

“That is the nicest thing anyone has called me this week.”

They sat beneath Helix’s giant shared-state equation.

Students passed around them on the way to ordinary classes.

A calculus lecture began across the hall while regulators debated whether Kade’s brain required a treaty.

School continued.

Good.

Jonas said, “What do you think the protocol should say?”

Kade answered too quickly.

“Consent.”

“That is not a protocol.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Kade looked at him.

Jonas drank coffee.

Everyone had become intolerable.

Kade thought about Petrov’s questions.

A volunteer could still misunderstand risk.

Consent did not solve hostile participants.

Consent did not tell a hospital who could legally shut down an oxygen system.

Consent did not make medical telemetry private by magic.

Consent mattered.

It did not eliminate governance.

Kade said, “Maybe the protocol needs two different questions.”

Jonas waited.

“Who is allowed to join the shared state?”

“And?”

“Who is allowed to make the real-world decision attached to it.”

Jonas nodded.

“Different permissions.”

“Yes.”

Kade looked toward the working-group doors.

“And everybody keeps trying to make them one thing.”

---

Mara called him into definitions at 07:52.

The room contained lawyers, academy researchers, two ethics specialists, Aya, Takahara, and a whiteboard full of words Kade had previously assumed meant approximately the same thing.

**CONSENT**

**AUTHORIZATION**

**ASSENT**

**CLEARANCE**

**COMMAND**

**JURISDICTION**

**PARTICIPATION**

**EXECUTION**

Kade looked at the board.

“I apologize for every time I complained physics used too many terms.”

Nobody laughed.

Lawyers.

Mara pointed at a proposed definition.

**PARTICIPATION CONSENT: voluntary agreement to enter and remain in a cross-operator shared-state event, revocable by the participant at any time.**

Kade nodded.

“Good.”

An ethics adviser said, “What if withdrawal immediately endangers others?”

Kade stopped.

There it was.

Not theoretical.

Imagine Mara holding geometry during a real collapse.

Imagine Eli withdrawing from machine interpretation halfway through a reactor shutdown.

The right to leave did not make consequences disappear.

Aya answered first.

“The person still must be physically able to withdraw.”

“Yes,” the adviser said. “And?”

Aya looked at the board.

“The system must be designed so withdrawal is a known failure mode.”

Takahara nodded.

Mara added, “Which means participation consent cannot include a promise never to withdraw. That would make consent structurally false.”

Kade looked at her.

Exactly.

The adviser said, “Then critical systems require redundancy.”

“Or explicit degradation paths,” Kade said.

Everyone looked at him.

He continued carefully.

“If one person leaving turns a hospital safe-state into catastrophe, the problem started before they left.”

The room went quiet.

Mara wrote it down.

Kade pointed.

“That was not formal language.”

“No.”

“Good.”

The lawyer beside her translated:

**Critical shared-state operations must specify participant-withdrawal contingencies and may not condition participation consent on irrevocable continuation.**

Kade stared.

“That is uglier.”

“It will survive court.”

“Useful skill.”

Mara moved to the next term.

Authorization.

This was where the fight began.

---

Petrov joined the definitions room remotely.

So did Colonel Vale.

Mara displayed the revision history.

“Why was `consenting operator` replaced with `authorized operator`?”

Vale answered first.

“Because consent is insufficient for regulated operations.”

Mara said, “That does not answer the edit.”

“It does.”

“No. If the intended requirement is both consent and operational authorization, the protocol can require both.”

Kade looked at her.

Simple.

Obvious.

Which meant the choice not to do that mattered.

Petrov frowned at the document.

“I did not approve that substitution.”

The room changed.

Vale’s expression remained neutral.

“Strategic legal review did.”

“Under whose authority?” Petrov asked.

“Participating states requested harmonized terminology.”

Mara said, “Which states?”

Vale did not answer immediately.

There.

Kade felt his anger rise.

Old version of him would have attacked the silence.

Mara did not.

She waited.

Vale finally listed three governments and the Directorate legal office.

Petrov made a note.

“I want the prior language restored.”

Vale said, “Then regulated systems may appear to permit any willing participant.”

Takahara replied, “No. Restore consent and add authorization separately where the real-world operation requires it.”

Aya said, “Participation and jurisdiction are not the same axis.”

Kade almost laughed.

Of course she would phrase it that way.

Petrov said, “Draft it.”

The lawyer did.

**PARTICIPATION requires voluntary, revocable consent and applicable medical clearance.**

**EXECUTION of regulated real-world actions may additionally require role-specific authorization under applicable law or operational procedure.**

Kade read it.

“Better.”

Vale said, “Operationally naive.”

Julian spoke from the doorway.

He had arrived from the command group.

“No.”

Everyone looked at him.

Julian walked in.

“Vahana already separates who may participate in state awareness from who owns a specific control function. Kisiwa does the same with local response authority. We are not inventing a distinction. Your draft collapses it.”

Vale looked at him.

“Rank One is now a governance theorist?”

Julian’s face went still.

Kade recognized the shot.

Public role as weapon.

Julian did not take it.

“No. I am a student who spent yesterday failing because I confused capability with jurisdiction.”

Mara looked at Kade.

Visible progression.

Public.

Good.

Vale said nothing.

---

The telemetry group found a different problem.

Eli came out at 09:20 looking angry.

This was rare enough that Kade stood.

“What?”

Eli held up the protocol.

“They want full raw telemetry retained for ten years.”

“Medical?”

“Everything.”

“What is everything?”

Eli looked at him.

“Exactly.”

Yesterday’s shared-state experiment had recorded field output, cardiovascular measures, eye tracking, cortical proxy signals, system-state transitions, decision timestamps, withdrawal timing, and participant communications.

Some of that was ordinary safety data.

Some might reveal health information.

Some might eventually reveal cognitive patterns nobody yet understood.

“Who gets access?” Kade asked.

“Draft says accredited safety, research, and strategic agencies.”

“Strategic.”

“Yes.”

Kade thought of the Continuity Directorate.

“No.”

Eli looked relieved to hear it.

Then Kade stopped.

“Wait.”

Eli frowned.

“We do need enough data to investigate if somebody gets hurt.”

“Yes.”

“And enough to detect coercion or hidden failure.”

“Yes.”

“So not no.”

Eli sighed.

“Everyone is making me become nuanced.”

“International education.”

They went back in.

The telemetry group eventually split data into layers.

Safety record.

Research record.

Strategic record only by separate legal authority.

Participant-access rights.

Retention limits.

Medical data under medical privacy.

Kade watched Eli argue that machine/system telemetry could be shared more broadly than participant physiological telemetry even when both came from the same event.

Nobody laughed at him.

Nobody treated him as Kade’s roommate.

Good.

---

At 11:45 the meeting reconvened.

Four working groups had produced four incompatible drafts.

Kade considered that reassuring.

Real collaboration apparently looked like administrative failure before lunch.

Petrov put the differences on the main wall.

Medical wanted conservative duration limits and mandatory recovery intervals.

Operations wanted role-specific authorization and explicit withdrawal contingencies.

Participant rights wanted revocable consent, privacy segmentation, and no blanket strategic access.

Strategic representatives wanted recognized command, identity control, and state visibility into cross-border networks.

None of those concerns were fake.

The conflict lived in priority and ownership.

Sen spoke for the first time in nearly an hour.

“An interim prohibition on uncontrolled public trials is reasonable.”

Kade looked at the screen.

Of course she would begin there.

Sen continued.

“An interim rule that converts participant consent into state permission is not.”

Colonel Vale said, “Director Sen, Asterion’s own suppression history suggests caution.”

The room sharpened.

Kade felt the old anger.

Sen did not flinch.

“Yes.”

One word.

Then:

“It also demonstrates the cost of allowing strategic fear to become the default owner of information.”

Kade stared at her.

Not absolution.

Not enough.

But not evasion either.

Vale said, “You concealed Mercer for months.”

“I restricted unsafe replication while concealing classified history. Those were not the same act, though I allowed them to reinforce each other.”

Mara glanced at Kade.

He breathed.

Sen continued.

“Do not repeat my error at international scale by bundling every legitimate safety concern into one permission structure.”

Silence.

Kade did not forgive her because she gave a good sentence.

He did believe the sentence.

Both could be true.

---

The interim protocol passed at 14:08.

Not unanimously.

Good.

It did three things Kade liked.

Cross-academy shared-state experiments required explicit, revocable participant consent.

Medical stop authority remained independent of strategic command.

Critical operations had to include withdrawal contingencies rather than treating continued participation as mandatory.

It did three things he disliked.

Public trials were suspended for thirty days pending review.

Real-world regulated uses required recognized operational authorization by domain.

Cross-border experiments had to file identity and safety metadata with a new international registry.

And one thing remained unresolved.

Raw telemetry access.

The strategic agencies wanted more.

Participant-rights group wanted less.

They deferred it forty-eight hours.

Kade read the final language.

**CONSENTING PARTICIPANT** had survived.

**AUTHORIZED OPERATOR** still existed—but only for execution of regulated actions.

Two axes.

Not one.

Mara sat beside him.

“You look disappointed.”

“I wanted to win.”

“You did not have a side.”

“I developed several.”

“That is called policy.”

“Terrible sport.”

She smiled.

Kade looked at the thirty-day suspension.

“People will call this a ban.”

“Yes.”

“Is it?”

“Temporarily, for public trials.”

“That sounds like a ban.”

“It is also a safety review.”

“Still doing nuance.”

“Yes.”

Kade hated that he understood why.

---

The crisis should have ended there.

It did not.

At 16:31 a leaked earlier draft hit the public network.

The version with **AUTHORIZED OPERATOR STATUS** and **RECOGNIZED COMMAND AUTHORITY** as prerequisites for shared-state participation.

No revision history.

No later correction.

Just the most alarming draft.

Within twenty minutes the story became:

**GOVERNMENTS MOVE TO LICENSE THOUGHT-SHARING**

Then:

**STANDARD NINE NETWORK TO REQUIRE STATE COMMAND**

Then:

**MERCER DEMONSTRATION TRIGGERS GLOBAL CONTROL LAW**

Kade stared at the headlines.

Mara said, “Do not respond yet.”

“I know.”

She looked at him.

He hated that she looked surprised.

“What?”

“You said it without arguing.”

“Growth.”

Across the room Aya’s tablet filled with messages from Kiyomizu.

Jonas received Helix student-government requests.

Neema got calls from Kisiwa response trainees asking whether their local authority would now be overridden by international rules.

Arjun’s Vahana classmates wanted to know whether temporary handoff centers counted as recognized command.

The policy had become personal before anyone had read the final version.

Varga entered the common room.

“Press briefing in thirty minutes.”

Kade stood.

Mara looked at him.

“You do not have to speak.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to?”

Kade looked at the leaked draft.

Then the final one.

“Yes.”

Not because he wanted to win.

Because silence would let the wrong document become the only story.

---

The briefing was smaller than yesterday’s demonstration and more dangerous.

No field equipment.

Only microphones.

Kade sat with Petrov, Varga, Park, Mara, Aya, and Julian. Sen remained remote.

Petrov opened by explaining the interim protocol.

Consent remained required.

Public trials paused thirty days.

Operational authorization applied to real-world regulated actions, not to whether a person could consent to participate in a research shared-state event.

Medical and strategic data were not automatically the same record.

The leaked draft was obsolete.

The first reporter asked Kade whether governments were trying to control him.

Kade almost answered yes.

Easy.

Clean.

Wrong enough to matter.

“They are trying to control something they do not understand yet,” he said.

Petrov’s face tightened.

Kade continued before the quote escaped alone.

“So are we.”

The room quieted.

“There should be medical limits. There should be rules for who can shut down a hospital or redirect a spacecraft. There should be privacy protections. There should be withdrawal plans.”

He looked at the final protocol.

“What there should not be is one word that pretends permission to join a shared state and authority to control a real-world system are the same thing.”

Mara’s expression stayed neutral.

He thought she approved.

Probably.

Another reporter asked whether he supported the thirty-day suspension.

Kade paused.

“I hate it.”

A few people laughed.

“I also understand why Park does not want us extending yesterday’s eleven seconds just because the cameras liked it.”

Park looked at him.

“That is the most sensible thing you have said publicly.”

“Please do not damage my reputation.”

The room laughed again.

Good.

Then Kade said, “I support the safety review. I do not support using the review to decide that only people approved by a permanent command structure are allowed to choose each other.”

There.

His actual side.

Not no governance.

Not no authority.

No ownership disguised as safety.

---

The briefing helped.

Not enough.

Nothing helped enough anymore.

By evening, governments had begun issuing their own national interpretations of the interim protocol.

Some were narrow.

Some were broad.

One temporarily prohibited all academy shared-state research involving foreign nationals.

Another created an expedited licensing route.

Kiyomizu publicly endorsed the consent language and objected to command prerequisites.

Vahana endorsed role-specific authorization and withdrawal engineering.

Kisiwa demanded local emergency authority protections.

Helix announced a public methods repository stripped of participant medical data.

Asterion issued a statement Sen had apparently allowed someone else to write because it was only two paragraphs long.

The world did not converge on one answer.

Good.

Complicated.

Real.

Kade returned to the residence after twenty-one hours awake.

Mara was still working.

Of course.

He sat beside her.

She looked at him.

“You should sleep.”

“So should you.”

“Yes.”

Neither moved.

Kade watched the final protocol revision history scroll past.

Consenting.

Authorized.

Restored.

Separated.

One word had nearly changed the architecture of the rule.

He thought of yesterday’s field network.

A shared system could fail because everyone routed decisions through one person.

Apparently a legal system could do the same thing with definitions.

Mara closed the tablet.

“Done.”

“For tonight?”

“Yes.”

Kade stood.

At the door, his tablet buzzed.

A private message from Asterion.

Aaron.

**Saw the hearing. We need to talk about why the academies were selecting for this before anyone had the language. Not tonight. When you are back.**

Kade read it twice.

Mara saw his face.

“What?”

He showed her.

She read.

Then looked at him.

“That is Chapter Twenty material.”

Kade stared.

“What?”

Mara blinked.

“Nothing. I am tired.”

Kade narrowed his eyes.

“That was very strange.”

“Yes.”

He read Aaron’s message again.

The protocol crisis had asked who should control shared-state systems now.

Aaron apparently wanted to explain who had been choosing the people capable of them decades before Kade knew the question existed.

Kade suddenly felt less tired.

That was unfortunate.

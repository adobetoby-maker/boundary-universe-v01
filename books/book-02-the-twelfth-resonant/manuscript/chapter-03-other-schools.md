# Chapter 3 — Other Schools

By Thursday, every student at Asterion had developed a principled reason they should be sent overseas.

Most of the principles involved wanting to go overseas.

House Meridian’s breakfast table had become a campaign headquarters.

Eli had printed a map.

Not projected.

Printed.

Kade stared at it.

“Why is this paper?”

“Strategic seriousness.”

“You own six displays.”

“Paper has authority.”

Sera looked at the map.

“You circled Vahana three times.”

“Orbital systems.”

“You wrote *please* next to it.”

“I am expressing professional interest.”

“You drew a rocket.”

“Visual communication.”

Mara sat beside Kade reading the official exchange packet.

Of course she had the official packet.

Kade had the same packet unopened in his messages.

He considered that a division of labor.

“What does Kiyomizu actually specialize in?” he asked.

Mara looked up.

“Paired control, high-precision Redirection, cooperative field geometry, and human-machine synchronization.”

“That sounded rehearsed.”

“I read.”

“Dangerous habit.”

Sera tapped Kisiwa on Eli’s map.

“Kisiwa runs civic-response rotations with municipal systems.”

Eli looked wounded.

“You read too?”

“I know what I’m applying for.”

Kade looked at her.

“You decided.”

“Yes.”

No hesitation.

That was Sera.

A month ago, Kade might have felt strange about how quickly her future could point somewhere he was not.

Now he only felt strange for half a second.

Progress.

“What about Helix?” he asked.

Mara turned the packet.

“Systems theory, cooperative modeling, field research, formal mathematics.”

Kade grimaced.

“Hostile environment.”

“You would survive.”

“Strong claim.”

“And Vahana,” Eli said, “is orbital logistics, command systems, heavy infrastructure, vacuum operations, and—”

“Things you are not allowed to touch,” Sera said.

Eli ignored her.

Kade looked at the four academy seals.

Asterion had spent months feeling like the center of the Resonant world.

Not because anyone had explicitly said it was.

Because everybody at Asterion behaved like it was obvious.

The rankings.

The arena feeds.

The faculty.

The Spire.

The way recruiters and governments came to the island rather than asking the island to come to them.

Now four other institutions had appeared in every corridor as though the world had quietly informed Asterion that it was one school among several.

Kade liked that more than he expected.

Taren arrived carrying coffee and a supervisor.

The supervisor stopped at the next table.

Taren sat.

Eli slid the map toward him.

“You were excluded,” Sera said.

Eli looked at her.

“You could have softened that.”

“She could have,” Taren agreed.

Sera did not apologize.

Taren looked over the academy names.

For a moment the expression he usually wore disappeared.

Kade had learned to notice those moments.

“You wanted to go,” Kade said.

Taren looked at him.

“Obviously.”

“Where?”

Taren pointed at Helix.

That surprised everyone.

Eli said, “Theory?”

“Privacy law.”

Kade frowned.

Taren smiled faintly.

“Helix has the strongest student-data restrictions of the four partner systems.”

“Of course you know that,” Sera said.

“I read.”

Mara looked offended on behalf of reading.

Taren continued.

“I wanted to see what an academy looks like when half the things I did at Asterion would have required a judicial order.”

The joke was dry.

The regret beneath it was not.

Nobody tried to fix the moment.

That was becoming a Meridian skill.

The wall display changed at eight sharp.

**INTERNATIONAL EXCHANGE ORIENTATION — CANDIDATES REPORT TO FORUM B**

Kade’s wrist vibrated.

So did Mara’s.

Eli’s.

Sera’s.

Taren’s did not.

The absence was louder than a notification.

Taren picked up his coffee.

“Have fun becoming globally inconvenient.”

Kade stood.

“You’re already internationally inconvenient.”

Taren looked at him.

“Thank you.”

“Wasn’t praise.”

“Recovered.”

---

Forum B had been rearranged into four quadrants.

Kade disliked the symbolism immediately.

Asterion had apparently decided the best way to explain different educational philosophies was to put them in booths.

At least the booths were good.

Kiyomizu’s display contained no rankings.

That was the first thing Kade noticed.

Two students moved through a paired-control exercise on the central screen. Neither wore individual scoring markers. Their performance report appeared only after the exercise ended.

**PAIR STABILITY: 94.2%**

**MUTUAL CORRECTION DELAY: 18 ms**

**UNILATERAL OVERRIDE EVENTS: 0**

Kade stopped.

Mara stopped beside him.

“Unilateral override,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Zero is good.”

“Obviously.”

Asterion tracked individual reaction, efficiency, field output, safety, placement, rank.

Kiyomizu had put *not overriding your partner* on the front page.

An orientation recording began.

A middle-aged instructor spoke in Japanese, translated cleanly through the display.

“At Kiyomizu, control without mutual legibility is considered incomplete. A technically correct result achieved by masking intent from a partner may receive a lower evaluation than a slower result achieved through shared state awareness.”

Kade looked at Mara.

“That seems targeted.”

“The world existed before you.”

“Unproven.”

She almost smiled.

The next screen showed first-year students practicing paired stabilization while one participant deliberately introduced mistakes.

Not to trick the other.

To see whether the pair could correct without one person becoming dominant.

Kade felt something under his ribs.

Not Boundary architecture.

Recognition.

Asterion had treated coordinated correction as advanced team skill.

Kiyomizu appeared to treat it as grammar.

Mara was already reading the technical notes.

“Come on,” Kade said.

“I’m reading.”

“You can read later.”

“That is false.”

Sera physically took Mara by the sleeve.

Mara looked down at the hand.

Sera released her.

“Sorry.”

Mara closed the display.

“Accepted.”

Kade noticed the exchange.

Small consent.

Ordinary consent.

The kind nobody wrote policies about.

Maybe that was where the larger version had to begin.

---

Kisiwa’s quadrant smelled like wet concrete.

That was intentional.

A full-scale municipal rescue model occupied half the floor.

Collapsed roadway.

Water main rupture.

Power fault.

Apartment structure with simulated occupants.

No arena boundary.

No scoring lights.

A timer ran anyway.

Sera stopped walking.

Kade saw her face change.

Not excitement exactly.

Recognition.

A recorded Kisiwa instructor stood beside a city map.

“Civic Resonance is not support work,” she said. “It is not what happens after the important fight. If a district loses pressure, transport, power, communication, and structural access simultaneously, the system failure is the emergency.”

Sera folded her arms.

“Finally.”

Eli looked at her.

“You have been waiting to say that.”

“Yes.”

The display shifted to trainees working with firefighters, engineers, medical teams, and residents.

A high-ranked student held a fractured support while three lower-ranked teammates rerouted water and power.

The performance summary listed:

**CIVILIAN ACCESS RESTORED**

**SECONDARY FAILURES PREVENTED**

**LOCAL TEAM AUTONOMY PRESERVED**

No individual ranks.

Kade noticed a pattern.

Sera noticed something else.

“They put the local team in command.”

Mara read the note.

“Even when the trainee has higher Resonant authority.”

Sera nodded.

“Because the local people know the system.”

Kade looked at her.

“Your first placement fire.”

Sera’s eyes moved to him.

Not angry.

Checking.

Kade regretted saying it in public.

“Sorry.”

She considered him.

Then nodded once.

“It’s related.”

She turned back to the display.

“My staff froze because everyone thought authority and competence were the same thing.”

The Kisiwa simulation began another run.

A trainee entered a collapsed building and immediately asked a maintenance worker where the structural modifications were.

Sera smiled.

Not often.

Not large.

Kade stored it anyway.

---

Vahana’s quadrant had gravity warnings.

Eli nearly vibrated.

“Look.”

“I’m looking.”

“No, look.”

“You are pointing at a warning sign.”

“Exactly.”

A rotating orbital habitat filled the main display.

Trainees worked inside and outside the structure.

The first exercise showed a pressure leak.

The obvious Resonant solution was to seal it.

The trainee did not.

She vented a second compartment deliberately, equalized a pressure gradient, transferred three crew members, then sealed both sections.

Kade stared.

“They made it worse first.”

Eli looked delighted.

“To keep it from becoming catastrophic.”

The recording’s instructor appeared.

“Orbital systems punish local thinking. A correct intervention in one compartment may be fatal two modules away. Command is therefore taught as explicit transfer of responsibility, not permanent ownership.”

There it was again.

Different language.

Same shape.

Responsibility transferred.

No permanent owner.

Kade glanced at Eli.

Eli had already pulled up the technical appendix.

“You are not going to read all of that now.”

“I might.”

“You’ll miss Helix.”

“Helix has PDFs.”

“That may be the most offensive thing you have ever said about a school.”

Eli looked torn.

Then a display showed a student remotely controlling three maintenance drones outside a lunar logistics hub.

He stopped being torn.

“Go without me.”

Sera grabbed the back of his jacket.

“No.”

---

Helix looked least impressive.

Kade trusted it immediately.

No full-scale simulation.

No dramatic screens.

Tables.

Whiteboards.

Dense cooperative models projected in layers above them.

Mara relaxed by approximately three percent.

Kade noticed.

“Home?”

“No.”

“You look emotionally supported by notation.”

“I’m ignoring you.”

A Helix lecture excerpt played.

“Cooperative field theory fails when we model coordination as communication between complete individuals. At sufficient coupling, each participant remains autonomous, but the active physical model must include the effects of mutual prediction.”

Kade stopped.

Mara did too.

Eli arrived late, dragged by Sera.

“What did I miss?”

Kade pointed at the sentence.

Eli read it.

Then read it again.

“That is your Vector Class thing.”

“It is not mine.”

“Good growth.”

Kade ignored him.

A second screen displayed historic diagrams.

Not Consensus.

Nothing that direct.

But multi-operator models that treated shared correction as a state variable instead of noise.

The oldest cited paper was twenty-six years old.

Kade leaned closer.

“Asterion did not invent this.”

Mara said, “We never knew enough to say it did.”

“Sen talks like the precursor program was unique.”

“She said Contact Zero was unique.”

“Different sentence.”

Mara looked at him.

Sen had trained both of them too well.

A student volunteer from Helix appeared on a live call screen.

Dark skin, close-cropped hair, German-accented English, Asterion-blue visitor badge already clipped to his shirt.

“Questions?” he asked.

Eli raised his hand immediately.

“Are your cooperative metrics compatible with Asterion’s?”

The volunteer smiled.

“Define compatible.”

Kade liked him instantly.

Eli looked almost emotional.

“Do you track role switching, incomplete-information adaptation, responsibility handoff—”

“Yes.”

Eli stopped.

The volunteer continued.

“So does Kiyomizu. So does Vahana. Kisiwa uses different scoring and pretends theirs is not scoring.”

Sera frowned.

“Pretends?”

He lifted both hands.

“Their people will argue with me when they arrive.”

“Good.”

The volunteer laughed.

Mara asked, “Why are the categories so similar?”

He shrugged.

“Because teams need similar things?”

Possible.

Reasonable.

Not evidence.

Kade watched his face.

No hidden reaction.

No careful language.

Probably honest.

“What’s your name?” Kade asked.

“Jonas Falk.”

“Kade.”

Jonas looked at the badge line beneath Kade’s name.

**IX?**

His eyebrows rose.

“Ah.”

Kade sighed.

“Please don’t.”

Jonas laughed.

“I was going to say the punctuation is good.”

“Accepted.”

---

Delegation selection began after lunch.

Asterion made it look like admissions all over again.

Interview rooms.

Performance histories.

Medical checks.

Faculty notes.

One new category Kade had never seen before.

**COOPERATIVE ADAPTATION INDEX**

He stared at it.

The number beside his name was not his rank.

**94.1**

Mara’s was **96.8**.

Kade showed her.

“You beat me at teamwork.”

Mara glanced over.

“This surprises you?”

“It hurts that it doesn’t.”

Eli’s index was **92.4**.

Sera’s was **97.2**.

She looked actively annoyed by winning.

Julian arrived from House Sol with **95.7**.

His conventional rank still appeared beside it.

**#1**

Two numbers measuring different things.

Kade liked that better than one number pretending to be everything.

The selection officer, Dean Rook, addressed twenty-four candidates.

“This is not a ranking exercise. Each host academy requested different profiles. Some of you will travel to all four sites. Some to one. Some will remain at Asterion for reciprocal hosting.”

A student raised a hand.

“Does IX guarantee Mercer a slot?”

Rook looked at Kade.

“No.”

Kade smiled.

The student looked disappointed.

Kade liked Rook immediately.

“Host academies may reject candidates,” Rook continued. “Asterion may reject host requests. Students may decline assignment.”

Mara nodded almost imperceptibly.

That sentence mattered.

Rook opened the first list.

**CORE INTERNATIONAL CIRCUIT — PROVISIONAL**

Julian Cross.

Mara Vey.

Sera Vale.

Elias Navarro.

Kade Mercer.

Kade stared.

Five names.

He looked automatically for Taren’s.

Not there.

He had known it would not be.

Knowing did not make the absence smaller.

The reserve list followed.

Priya Shah.

Nessa Kim.

Anton Bell.

Three students from other Houses.

Rook said, “Final placements depend on host approval and security review.”

Security review.

Taren again, without being named.

Kade did not raise his hand.

That was harder than it should have been.

Julian noticed.

Mara noticed him noticing.

Nobody rescued him from the choice.

Good.

---

That evening Meridian held an unofficial delegation dinner.

Taren came because his restrictions allowed House meals.

Nobody tried to pretend the list did not exist.

That helped.

Eli had placed the printed map in the center of the table.

Sera crossed out his Vahana rocket.

He drew another.

Julian arrived late from Sol with dessert he claimed was diplomatic tribute.

Mara had already downloaded Kiyomizu’s first-week training schedule.

Kade had still not opened the full exchange packet.

Some systems remained stable.

Taren looked at the five provisional names.

“You’re going to be terrible internationally.”

Kade nodded.

“Likely.”

“Mara will correct foreign institutions.”

“Only inaccurate ones,” Mara said.

“All institutions are inaccurate eventually.”

“Then I’ll be busy.”

Taren pointed at Sera.

“You’ll defect to Kisiwa.”

Sera looked at him.

“No.”

Then, after a moment:

“Probably not.”

That was more honesty than Kade expected.

Eli clutched his chest.

“Vahana may keep me.”

“No one is keeping you,” Sera said.

“Emotionally.”

Julian looked toward Kade.

“And you?”

Kade looked at the map.

Kiyomizu’s paired control.

Kisiwa’s local authority.

Vahana’s handoff doctrine.

Helix’s shared-state mathematics.

Every school seemed to have taken one thing Asterion treated as secondary and built an identity around it.

“I don’t know yet.”

Taren smiled.

“That is suspiciously healthy.”

“Do not make it unpleasant.”

The table laughed.

Then Taren looked at the map again.

The humor faded.

Kade waited.

Taren said, “Bring me something back.”

Eli answered first.

“Contraband?”

“Information acquired legally.”

“That sounds terrible.”

“I’m trying a new lifestyle.”

Kade looked at him.

“What do you want?”

Taren considered the question.

“Proof that Asterion isn’t the only place that can be wrong about itself.”

Nobody laughed.

Kade understood why.

Asterion had taught all of them to believe it was the center.

Book One had taught them the island did not even know its own history.

Now the world was opening.

Maybe the useful question was not which school had the best system.

Maybe it was what each system could not see from inside itself.

The countdown above the Commons doors changed another second.

**00:269:22:11:08**

Kade looked at the map.

Four schools.

Four versions of cooperation.

Five students provisionally chosen to cross between them.

For the first time since coming to Asterion, Kade felt the island become small.

He liked that too.

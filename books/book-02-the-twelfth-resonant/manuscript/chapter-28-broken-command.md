# Chapter 28 — Broken Command

The emergency began with three correct orders.

That was the problem.

The lunar maintenance tug *Peregrine Six* had been moving between the far-side array and an orbital logistics platform when its guidance system began returning inconsistent attitude data.

The crew noticed first.

Then Vahana telemetry.

Then the Lunar Authority.

Then three jurisdictions tried to help.

Kade was in the array briefing room when every wall display changed at once.

**PEREGRINE SIX — ATTITUDE CONTROL ANOMALY**

**CREW: 4**

**PROP RESERVE: 31%**

**RELATIVE DRIFT: INCREASING**

Dr. Zhou stood.

“Everyone not operational, clear the room.”

Kade started to move.

Eli did not.

Zhou looked at him.

“Navarro. Stay.”

Eli blinked.

“Why?”

“Vahana requested your stabilization background.”

Kade kept walking.

That mattered.

He was not needed.

Then Zhou said, “Mercer. Observation only unless requested.”

Less emotionally satisfying.

Still correct.

Sera stayed under lunar response authority.

Julian stayed as Vahana liaison.

Mara stayed because the array’s precision geometry tools were relevant to the tug’s sensor conflict.

Kade stood at the back.

Good.

He hated emergencies for teaching healthy lessons.

---

Order One came from the Lunar Authority.

**HOLD CURRENT ATTITUDE. CONSERVE PROPELLANT. AWAIT TUG SUPPORT.**

Correct.

The crew’s own visual reference suggested the vehicle was stable enough to wait.

Order Two came from Vahana mission control.

**BEGIN CONTROLLED DESATURATION. PRIMARY ATTITUDE SENSOR MAY BE BIASED.**

Also correct.

If the sensor was wrong, holding current attitude meant trusting a lie.

Order Three came from the national space agency that owned the tug.

**RETURN TO PLATFORM UNDER MANUAL GUIDANCE BEFORE COMMUNICATION GEOMETRY DEGRADES.**

Correct for a different risk.

The relay geometry would worsen in twenty-six minutes. If communications degraded while the tug remained in uncertain attitude, rescue became harder.

Three authorities.

Three models.

Each internally coherent.

Together: impossible.

The tug commander said over comms, “Which order has priority?”

Silence.

Kade felt the problem turn.

Not power problem.

Command problem.

---

Julian moved first.

Not by issuing a fourth order.

He stepped to the authority board.

“Who owns crew safety?”

Lunar response answered, “Local commander and Lunar Authority.”

“Who owns tug flight dynamics?”

Vahana liaison: “Vehicle mission control.”

“Who owns return window?”

National agency.

Julian looked at the three lines.

“Then no one owns the whole decision.”

A government representative said, “That is precisely why command hierarchy exists.”

Julian turned.

“No. That is why command hierarchy needs a transfer rule before the emergency.”

Kade saw his father’s lesson finally inverted.

The strongest person in the room did not own every failure.

But somebody had to own the next decision.

Temporarily.

Explicitly.

Julian said, “Give flight dynamics temporary decision authority for six minutes. Lunar retains medical stop. National agency retains abort authority if return window becomes unrecoverable.”

The Lunar commander objected.

“My crew is physically here.”

“Then make crew state a veto condition.”

Not elegant.

Possible.

The tug commander came over comms.

“Accepted if I retain immediate manual override.”

Vahana accepted.

Lunar accepted.

National agency took twenty seconds longer.

Then accepted.

Temporary center.

Scope.

Time limit.

Challengeable.

Kade almost smiled.

Then the sensor data got worse.

---

Mara found the first physical clue.

The star tracker and inertial unit disagreed by less than one degree.

Small.

Enough.

But the disagreement oscillated with a period matching the tug’s thermal-control cycle.

“Sensor mount is moving,” she said.

Eli looked at the structure model.

“Thermal warp?”

“Maybe.”

Kade appreciated the maybe.

The tug’s outer sensor bracket crossed a sun-shadow cycle every ninety-three seconds.

Expansion.

Contraction.

Tiny geometry shift.

The star tracker remained honest.

Its mounting frame did not.

Vahana mission control ordered the crew to weight inertial data higher and begin desaturation.

The tug commander hesitated.

“Manual view shows we are stable.”

Mara said, “Your visual frame is the cabin.”

The commander paused.

“Meaning?”

“If the whole vehicle is drifting slowly, the cabin does not tell you.”

Eli added, “Reaction wheels are compensating. You feel stable because they are hiding the motion.”

There.

Same lesson as the Vahana docking sim.

Outcome looked nominal.

Effort exposed failure.

The crew began desaturation.

Propellant fell to twenty-eight percent.

Attitude error reduced.

Then one reaction wheel saturated.

Alarm.

The tug rotated faster.

The national agency immediately issued:

**ABORT DESATURATION. RETURN NOW.**

Vahana issued:

**CONTINUE. RETURN THRUST WILL AMPLIFY ROTATION.**

Lunar issued:

**CREW DISCRETION.**

The command structure broke again.

Kade watched the tug commander’s face on the screen.

Four people in a spinning vehicle while three institutions argued about who had the right to save them.

Fear made centralization look attractive.

Again.

Kade understood why people wanted one command.

He also understood why choosing the wrong one could kill everyone.

---

Sera spoke from the response station.

“Stop sending orders.”

Everyone looked at her.

She did not have flight authority.

That was why the sentence mattered.

“Send state,” she said. “Crew cannot resolve three commands while spinning.”

Julian understood immediately.

He opened a clean channel.

One line per domain.

**FLIGHT: RETURN THRUST NOW LIKELY INCREASES ROTATION.**

**CREW: NO INJURY; MANUAL CONTROLS AVAILABLE.**

**WINDOW: SAFE RETURN MARGIN 17 MIN.**

**PROP: 27%, CURRENT STABILIZATION ESTIMATE 8–11%.**

No command.

Information early enough for the operator to remain an operator.

Kiyomizu.

On the tug, Commander Ortiz read the state.

Then said, “I am continuing desaturation.”

National agency objected.

Julian said, “Commander holds temporary flight decision under the agreed override.”

The objection stopped.

Not because the agency agreed.

Because the authority transfer had been explicit.

Kade felt something settle.

Consent did not erase command.

Command did not have to erase agency.

---

The tug stabilized nine minutes later.

Propellant: seventeen percent.

Enough for return if nothing else went wrong.

Something else went wrong.

Of course.

The damaged star tracker dropped out completely.

The tug could return under inertial guidance, but the platform docking corridor required precision the remaining sensors could not guarantee.

Eli said, “Use array sectors.”

Zhou looked at him.

“What?”

“Your far-side calibration network can track external phase references. Not designed for vehicle navigation, but you can give them independent relative motion.”

Zhou was already thinking.

Mara said, “Accuracy?”

“Not docking accuracy,” Zhou said. “Approach accuracy.”

Enough to reach a safe hold near the platform.

Then another tug could capture.

A handoff.

Not complete rescue by one system.

A chain.

The far-side array acquired *Peregrine Six* on three sectors.

The first solution failed validation.

One sector had treated the platform’s own thermal plume as a stable reference.

Zhou killed the packet before Vahana could use it.

On the tug, Commander Ortiz heard only, “Navigation solution withdrawn.”

The stars beyond the forward port had finally stopped wheeling.

That made the blindness worse.

The tug was stable enough to fly and unable to prove where the safe corridor began.

One crew member held a checklist against the panel with both hands because anything released drifted toward the air intake.

Another watched the propellant estimate fall from twelve percent to eleven.

Ortiz asked, “Time to next solution?”

Zhou looked at the array team.

No one offered hope as data.

“Ninety seconds minimum.”

The national controller said, “Continue platform approach under inertial guidance.”

The crew-safety channel said, “Hold thrust and preserve return reserve.”

Two valid orders again.

Julian reached for the authority board.

“Ortiz owns immediate flight safety,” he said. “Platform control owns capture corridor. No corridor exists until navigation quality passes.”

The national controller objected.

Julian did not out-rank her.

He pointed to the conflict rule all three agencies had accepted nine minutes earlier.

Ortiz said, “Holding thrust.”

The tug held its uncertain attitude.

Kade watched the crew feed.

He could not make the array solve faster.

He could not turn uncertainty into authority.

He could stay present while someone else carried both.

Eli moved beside Zhou.

“Sector four can replace the contaminated reference if Vahana solves angular rate before position.”

“That gives them an incomplete state,” Zhou said.

“Label it incomplete.”

Vahana accepted the partial handoff.

Angular rate first.

Position confidence later.

The second packet crossed the network with its missing dimensions visible.

Ortiz read it aloud.

“Rate solution good. Position degraded. I can trim the residual drift without committing to approach.”

“Your decision,” Julian said.

Ortiz fired two short pulses.

The reference markers on the crew feed steadied.

The propellant display reached nine percent.

Then held.

The third array solution passed.

Vahana converted phase data into relative motion.

Lunar response maintained crew safety.

The national agency rebuilt the platform capture corridor around the new approach state.

No institution did the whole job.

The tug reached safe hold with nine percent propellant.

A second vehicle captured twenty-two minutes later.

Four crew alive.

No applause in the control room.

People sat down.

Some cried quietly.

Zhou closed the emergency log.

---

The debrief was political before it was technical.

Every agency wanted the final report to show its orders had been reasonable.

They were.

That was the uncomfortable part.

Julian said it plainly.

“The failure was not that any one authority gave an irrational instruction. The failure was that three valid authorities could issue mutually incompatible commands without a predeclared conflict-resolution mechanism.”

The national representative disliked that.

“Unified command would solve it.”

Sera answered.

“Which command?”

Silence.

Flight dynamics?

Crew safety?

Return jurisdiction?

Different moments produced different answers.

Aya, remote on the wall, said, “A permanent center would only move the blind spot.”

Kade looked at her.

There it was.

Not ideology.

Operational proof.

Mara added, “The successful sequence used temporary authority by domain, explicit handoff, and crew override.”

Vahana language.

Kiyomizu grammar.

Kisiwa local authority.

Helix state model.

Asterion pressure.

The international circuit had become one procedure without anyone declaring one school correct.

Good.

---

That night Kade found Julian alone in the observation corridor outside the buried array control room.

“You did well,” Kade said.

Julian looked at him.

“Four people almost died while adults argued.”

“Yes.”

“That is not a good day.”

“No.”

Kade leaned against the wall.

“You still did well.”

Julian looked through the narrow radiation-shielded viewport toward nothing visible.

“My father would say the lesson is that someone should have owned the whole system.”

“What do you say?”

Julian thought.

“That the person who owns the whole system is usually the person furthest from one of the consequences.”

Kade let that sit.

Then Julian looked at him.

“And you?”

Kade thought about Consensus.

His old star topology.

Aya’s web.

The tug commander choosing under pressure.

“I think handoff is harder than leadership.”

Julian smiled faintly.

“That sounds like something Vahana puts on a wall.”

“Do not tell them. They will charge tuition.”

Julian laughed.

The emergency had not proven command unnecessary.

It had proven command needed architecture too.

Tomorrow they would begin designing exactly that.

# Chapter 5 — Aya Mori

Aya Mori’s first sentence to Kade was, “Do not help me.”

Kade stared at her.

They stood three meters apart on Kiyomizu’s paired-control floor while thirty students arranged themselves around identical training rigs.

The sun had not fully cleared the eastern buildings.

Kade had been awake for twenty-three minutes.

This felt early for hostility.

“I haven’t done anything.”

“Yes.”

“That sounded like an accusation.”

Aya adjusted the training bands around her wrists.

“It is preventive.”

Kade looked toward Professor Takahara.

Takahara was pretending not to listen.

Probably.

The assignment board still read:

**MERCER — MORI**

No ranks.

No Standards.

No explanation.

Kade had spent the walk over trying not to investigate Aya simply because she had given him one surname at the airport and no reason to care.

He had been moderately successful.

Mara would have rated him lower.

Aya looked exactly as controlled as she had yesterday.

Dark hair tied low.

Plain Kiyomizu training uniform.

No jewelry.

No visible personal marks except a narrow white wrap around two fingers of her left hand.

Her gaze did not stay on Kade long enough to become staring.

It moved to his feet.

Then shoulders.

Then the floor sensors.

Assessment.

Kade knew the feeling.

“You always greet partners with instructions?” he asked.

“No.”

“Special treatment.”

“Yes.”

“Still hostile.”

Aya finally looked directly at him.

“You correct before people choose.”

Kade stopped.

Yesterday Takahara had said almost the same thing.

*Stop helping the person who does not yet need help.*

“You watched the orientation.”

“Yes.”

“Everybody did.”

“Yes.”

“You’re enjoying this.”

“No.”

Kade believed her.

That was somehow worse.

Professor Takahara called for attention.

“Today’s local-pair block tests adaptation under asymmetric information. One partner will receive environmental data the other does not. You may share anything you know. You may not ask the system to reveal the other person’s information.”

Kade almost laughed.

Kiyomizu had apparently built an entire curriculum around things the Continuity Directorate had failed to understand morally.

Aya noticed the expression.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That is usually false.”

Kade stared at her.

Aya’s face remained still.

“Did Mara train you?”

“I have spoken to Vey for seven minutes.”

“That may be enough.”

The training rig activated.

Two suspended weights hung from a shared armature. Kade controlled the left damping surface. Aya controlled the right. The system would shift mass, alter friction, and introduce field interference without warning.

A small private display appeared beside Kade.

**YOUR INFORMATION: LEFT ANCHOR FRICTION WILL DROP 22% AT T+18**

Aya’s display remained unreadable from his angle.

Kade said, “My left anchor loses friction in eighteen seconds.”

Aya nodded.

“My right-side field delay increases after your first corrective pulse.”

Kade frowned.

“That sounds targeted.”

“The system is educational.”

“Same thing.”

The timer began.

Kade stabilized his side conventionally.

Aya did the same.

The armature moved.

Kade felt Aya’s correction through the shared structure.

Precise.

Small.

No wasted force.

At fourteen seconds, Kade prepared for his friction loss.

At sixteen, Aya reduced output.

Kade saw it and compensated automatically.

The armature tilted.

Aya corrected.

Kade corrected her correction.

The system oscillated twice.

Penalty tone.

Aya released immediately.

Kade did too.

“Why did you reduce?” he asked.

“My delay began early.”

“You could have said that.”

“I was saying it.”

“You said nothing.”

“I reduced output gradually.”

“That is not language.”

“It is at Kiyomizu.”

Kade stopped.

Yesterday Julian had said almost exactly the opposite.

*My stance is a signal. It is at Asterion.*

Apparently every school was fluent in its own assumptions.

Aya reset the rig.

“You saw me reduce and decided what I needed.”

“You were destabilizing.”

“Yes.”

“So I corrected.”

“Yes.”

“That sounds reasonable when I say it.”

“It does.”

“And still wrong?”

“Yes.”

Kade looked toward Takahara.

She was helping another pair.

No rescue.

Good.

“What should I have done?”

Aya said, “Tell me what you see.”

“That’s it?”

“That is enough.”

They restarted.

At twelve seconds, Kade said, “Your output is dropping.”

Aya answered, “Delay increased early. I am reducing before compensation.”

“Left friction changes in six.”

“I can absorb center drift.”

“Do you want me to reduce?”

“No.”

There.

A no.

Simple.

Useful.

Kade held.

Friction dropped.

The armature shifted toward him.

Aya absorbed center drift.

Kade corrected his own side.

The rig remained level.

They cleared the first phase.

Second phase introduced a moving load.

Kade felt the familiar temptation to model the whole system.

Aya’s output.

His output.

The armature.

The floor.

The moving mass.

No cold line.

No Boundary pull.

Just systems intuition.

He noticed Aya stop speaking.

“What changed?”

“Nothing.”

“That is not true.”

Her eyes narrowed slightly.

Kade waited.

Aya said, “The training system changed my data feed.”

“How?”

“Not relevant yet.”

“You get to decide that?”

“For my information, yes.”

Kade nearly argued.

Then stopped.

Right.

The exercise continued.

Aya shared what mattered when it mattered.

Not everything.

Kade had to operate without owning her whole model.

He disliked how useful the lesson was.

They finished fourth.

Mara and Hana finished second again.

Julian and Daichi finished fifth.

Eli and Jun did not finish because their rig entered a protective shutdown after Eli attempted a “predictive compensation layer.”

Sera and Akari finished first.

Sera looked offended by applause.

Kade felt proud enough that he almost went to tell her.

Then stopped.

She already knew she had won.

Progress.

Aya looked at the results.

“You expected to be higher.”

“No.”

“Yes.”

“You are very confident for someone who barely uses full sentences.”

“You make jokes when observed accurately.”

Kade went still.

Aya’s expression did not change.

Taren had noticed that.

Mara too.

Sera.

Julian.

Apparently Kade had become internationally legible.

Terrible.

“Do you study people?” he asked.

“Yes.”

The directness threw him.

“For training?”

“For safety.”

That answer was more interesting.

Before Kade could ask, Takahara called the end of block.

Aya bowed once.

“Thank you.”

Then she left.

No explanation.

Kade watched her go.

Mara appeared beside him.

“No.”

“I did not say anything.”

“You were about to.”

“Everybody here is becoming annoying.”

Mara looked at the scoreboard.

“Fourth.”

“Second.”

“Yes.”

“You came over to mention that.”

“No.”

Kade waited.

Mara’s gaze moved toward Aya’s exit.

“She is very good.”

“Obviously.”

“Not at output.”

Kade looked at her.

Mara had noticed too.

“What then?”

“Timing. Information control. She never gave you more than you needed.”

“That was irritating.”

“Yes.”

“You sound approving.”

“I am.”

Kade sighed.

Of course.

---

Ren explained Aya Mori over breakfast with the careful tone people used when explaining weather near mountains.

“Do not challenge her.”

Kade looked up from rice and eggs.

“I wasn’t going to.”

Ren looked at him.

“Why does everyone assume this?”

“Pattern recognition.”

Kade hated that phrase now.

Mara sat two seats away with Hana.

Sera and Akari had already left for a response lecture.

Eli was trying to convince Jun that Prototype Four could survive another drop test.

Julian was eating quietly beside Daichi.

Ren leaned closer.

“Aya does not compete often.”

“Why?”

“She dislikes competitions.”

“That is allowed?”

“Here? Yes.”

Kade looked toward the absent ranking board.

“Her paired rating?”

Ren hesitated.

Interesting.

“What?” Kade asked.

“Her paired rating is high.”

“How high?”

“Not relevant.”

Kade stared.

Ren smiled.

“You are learning.”

“This school is weaponizing boundaries against me.”

“Good.”

Kade ate for three seconds.

Then: “Why did you say challenge her like it’s dangerous?”

Ren’s smile faded.

“Because people arrive, hear she does not compete, and think that means she is afraid to lose.”

“And?”

“They become unpleasant.”

Kade thought of Cal Reid.

Of every Asterion student who heard a rank and decided it explained the person.

“I wasn’t going to challenge her.”

Ren nodded.

“I believe you.”

That should have ended it.

It did not.

“What does she do?”

Ren closed his eyes.

“You are exhausting.”

“I’ve been told.”

“She is in Kiyomizu’s nonstandard architecture track.”

Kade’s spoon stopped.

“Architecture.”

Ren opened his eyes again.

“Yes.”

“What does that mean?”

“Ask Aya.”

“You just told me not to bother her.”

“I told you not to challenge her.”

“That distinction feels engineered.”

“It is.”

Kade looked toward the door Aya had used after training.

Nonstandard architecture.

Relational response architecture on the assessment board.

Different vocabulary.

Same recurring shape.

He did not like where his mind went next.

Which meant he needed evidence before following it.

Good.

---

Kiyomizu’s public archive was more open than Asterion’s and somehow harder to search.

That felt intentional.

The archive did not prioritize names.

It prioritized research categories, training lineages, and program histories.

Mara loved it.

Kade found her there before lunch.

“You followed me,” she said without looking up.

“I independently arrived at the same suspicious place.”

“You asked Hana where the archive was.”

“That is research.”

Mara turned a page on a projected document.

“What are you looking for?”

“Nonstandard architecture.”

Her hand stopped.

“Ren told you.”

“He told me a phrase.”

“That is not evidence.”

“I know.”

Mara studied him.

The fact that he said it without complaint apparently mattered.

She moved a second chair closer.

“Fine.”

Kade sat.

The public program catalog contained six architecture tracks.

Adaptive Coupling.

Field Symmetry Exceptions.

Nonlocal Perception Response.

Human-Machine Shared State.

Relational Architecture.

Unclassified Boundary Conditions.

Kade read the last one twice.

“Boundary.”

Mara nodded.

“Could be ordinary mathematical language.”

“I know.”

“That restraint is unsettling.”

“Growth.”

They opened the program history.

Most of it was dry.

Curriculum revisions.

Faculty rotations.

Safety policy.

One line from twenty-eight years ago:

**Diagnostic family revised following anomalous relational-response cases. Individual output score deemed insufficient for placement.**

Kade leaned closer.

“Twenty-eight years.”

Mara read the citation.

“After Contact Zero.”

“Before Asterion Academy.”

“Yes.”

The underlying diagnostic was not public.

Only a name remained.

**M-NULL RELATIONAL ROUTE**

Kade’s breathing changed.

Mara noticed.

“Do not decide yet.”

“I’m not.”

“Good.”

He stared at the words.

M-null.

Not 000.

Not Zero Protocol.

Could mean anything.

Could mean nothing.

Could be translation convention.

The dangerous part was how badly he wanted it to mean the same thing.

Mara said, “We need a second source.”

“Yes.”

Kade surprised himself again.

He stood.

“We ask Takahara.”

Mara closed the archive.

“Publicly?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because if this is normal here, I don’t want to turn Aya into an event before she chooses to be one.”

Mara looked at him for a long moment.

“That was good.”

“Careful.”

“Recovered.”

---

Professor Takahara did not seem surprised by the question.

That was the first bad sign.

They met her in a small faculty room overlooking the water garden.

No government observers.

No security.

Tea.

Kade distrusted tea now because important adults kept using it around secrets.

Mara asked first.

“What is the M-Null relational route?”

Takahara looked from Mara to Kade.

“Old diagnostic category.”

“For what?” Kade asked.

“For students whose response profile cannot be represented usefully by individual-output classifications.”

Kade’s pulse changed.

“Like a zero score?”

Takahara’s expression remained neutral.

“No.”

Too quick?

Maybe.

She continued before he could accuse the punctuation.

“A zero score implies absence on the measured axis. M-Null means the axis is not the correct model.”

Kade stopped breathing for half a second.

Mara went very still.

Takahara drank tea.

Kade said, “That is almost exactly what Aaron told me.”

“I know who Aaron Mercer is.”

Of course she did.

The world kept getting larger and Aaron’s secrecy kept getting smaller.

“Did you know him?”

“No.”

“Did Kiyomizu work with the precursor program?”

Takahara set down her cup.

“That is not a question I can answer from a public student conversation.”

Kade looked at Mara.

Mara looked back.

There.

Not an answer.

A boundary around one.

Takahara said, “You are asking about Mori.”

Kade’s attention snapped back.

“I didn’t say that.”

“You did not need to.”

Mara asked, “Is she M-Null?”

Takahara’s expression changed.

Not fear.

Disapproval.

“That is Mori’s information.”

Mara nodded immediately.

“Understood.”

Kade felt relief.

Not because he had an answer.

Because Takahara had refused to give him one that did not belong to her.

Good school.

Annoying school.

Takahara continued.

“If you wish to know how Mori understands her own architecture, ask Mori.”

Kade said, “Ren said the same thing.”

“Ren occasionally learns.”

“I’ll tell him.”

“Please do not.”

Mara almost smiled.

At the door, Takahara said, “Mercer.”

Kade turned.

“Do not ask whether she is like you.”

He frowned.

“Why?”

“Because it makes you the reference point.”

That landed cleanly.

Kade nodded once.

“Fair.”

---

He found Aya after evening training.

Not because he followed her.

He had learned enough to establish that distinction internally.

She was sitting alone at the edge of one of the narrow water channels behind the training halls, shoes off, feet on the stone but not in the water.

Kade stopped several meters away.

Aya looked up.

“Mercer.”

“Can I ask you something?”

“You already did.”

Kade stared.

Then laughed.

Tiny movement at the corner of Aya’s mouth.

Maybe.

He stayed standing.

“Can I ask you about your training track?”

Aya considered him.

“Yes.”

Kade sat on the opposite side of the channel.

Not beside her.

Enough distance that leaving remained easy.

“Ren called it nonstandard architecture.”

“He talks too much.”

“He does.”

“You searched the archive.”

Kade froze.

“How do you know?”

“Your access was public.”

“That is horrifying.”

“Kiyomizu archives show reader history for student-access material.”

“Helix would riot.”

“Yes.”

Kade looked at the water.

“We found M-Null.”

Aya said nothing.

“Takahara said it means individual-output classification is the wrong model.”

Still nothing.

Kade resisted filling the silence.

Finally Aya asked, “What do you want to know?”

Good question.

Not *what do you suspect.*

Not *what did Takahara tell you.*

What did he actually want?

“Why you told me not to help you.”

Aya looked almost surprised.

“That is not the question I expected.”

“I’m trying something new.”

“What?”

“Not making the interesting person explain their whole life because I am curious.”

Aya looked at him for a long moment.

Then she said, “My field response changes when another operator becomes part of the active system.”

Kade’s skin prickled.

She continued.

“Most paired students learn to coordinate two outputs. I do not experience the problem that way.”

“How?”

Aya looked toward the water.

“The relationship becomes measurable before the outputs do.”

Kade forgot every prepared question.

That sentence belonged too close to his own experience.

He kept Takahara’s warning in mind.

Do not ask whether she is like you.

“So when I corrected for you—”

“You changed the relationship before I chose the new state.”

“That’s why you knew immediately.”

“Yes.”

“Is that M-Null?”

Aya met his eyes.

“Yes.”

There.

No dramatic sound.

No system shutting down.

One word.

Kade felt the world move anyway.

“What was your score?”

Aya’s expression hardened.

Wrong question.

Kade heard it as soon as it left him.

“Sorry.”

Aya waited.

Kade tried again.

“How did the system identify it?”

“By refusing to give me an individual route.”

Kade’s pulse accelerated.

His NAAE.

The blank gray field.

REDEFINE.

000.

He said, carefully, “At Asterion they call my route Zero.”

“I know.”

“Do you call yours Zero?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because zero is a number.”

Kade stared.

Aya continued.

“And Standard Nine is a rank-shaped mistake.”

There it was.

Not accusation.

Not challenge.

A technical opinion delivered as if the argument had already been settled years ago.

Kade almost smiled.

“People at Asterion are going to love you.”

“I am not going to Asterion for people to love me.”

“Probably wise.”

Aya stood.

Kade looked up.

“That’s it?”

“For tonight.”

He almost protested.

Then stopped.

Her information.

Her boundary.

“Okay.”

Aya put on her shoes.

At the path, she paused.

“Mercer.”

“Yes?”

“You should stop letting them call you Nine.”

Kade looked at her.

“Why?”

Aya’s expression remained still.

“Because they will build the wrong system around the name.”

Then she left.

Kade sat beside the water alone.

For months, he had been the exception everyone else was trying to explain.

Now, for the first time, someone had looked at his exception and called it a category error.

The irritating part was that Kade thought she might be right.

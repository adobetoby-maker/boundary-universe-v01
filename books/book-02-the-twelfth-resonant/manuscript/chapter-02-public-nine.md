# Chapter 2 — Public Nine

By nine the next morning, Asterion had produced three official definitions of Standard Nine.

None agreed.

Kade found this encouraging.

The first came from the academy registrar.

**STANDARD IX — PROVISIONAL CAPABILITY CLASSIFICATION PENDING INTERNATIONAL REVIEW**

The second came from the International Resonance Authority.

**IX-CLASS COOPERATIVE FIELD PHENOMENON — INDIVIDUAL STATUS UNRESOLVED**

The third came from Asterion Student Media.

**NOBODY KNOWS WHAT NINE IS, INCLUDING NINE**

Kade thought the third was strongest.

He was reading all three while standing in line for coffee because the Central Forum briefing had run late, breakfast had happened without him, and Eli had informed him that “historic transition periods require caffeine.”

“You do not drink coffee,” Mara said.

Kade looked at the cup in his hand.

“That feels like a historical detail.”

“You hate it.”

“I hate many necessary systems.”

Mara took the cup from him, drank once, and made a face.

“This is terrible.”

Kade accepted it back.

“Now it has independent verification.”

They were in West Academic Hall between first and second period. Exchange Session banners had appeared overnight on every major walkway.

Kiyomizu.

Kisiwa.

Vahana.

Helix.

Four names that had been background geography a week earlier and were now the subject of every lunch table on campus.

The actual exchange delegation would not be announced until the end of the week.

That had not stopped approximately nine thousand students from deciding they were definitely going.

Eli had already made a spreadsheet.

Sera had already told him to delete the column labeled **COUNTRIES MOST LIKELY TO LET ME TOUCH ORBITAL HARDWARE**.

He had renamed it.

Kade did not know the new name.

He suspected capitalization remained involved.

Mara’s tablet chimed.

She read the message.

Her expression changed.

Kade saw it.

“What?”

“Your nine o’clock hearing moved to ten thirty.”

“My what?”

Mara looked at him.

“You didn’t read the schedule.”

“I read the part where I had class.”

“You have a provisional classification hearing.”

“I thought that was tomorrow.”

“It was.”

“Excellent. Time itself is being revised.”

Mara handed him the tablet.

**ASTERION CLASSIFICATION REVIEW — PUBLIC SESSION**

**Subject: Provisional Standard IX designation**

**Participants: Director Sen, Dr. Amara Park, International Resonance Authority observers, Academy Standards Board, Student Representative Council, Kade Mercer**

Kade looked at Mara.

“Why is my name last?”

“Alphabetical.”

“No.”

“Probably not.”

He scrolled.

The hearing would be streamed internally.

Of course it would.

There was a public-comment period.

Of course there was.

A section titled **CAPABILITY DEMONSTRATION — IF APPROVED**.

Kade stopped.

“No.”

Mara leaned closer.

“That is listed as optional.”

“Optional things become expected very quickly when governments put them on agendas.”

“Yes.”

“You agree with me too efficiently.”

“I read the documents yesterday.”

“Show-off.”

The bell sounded.

Mara took her tablet back.

“You still have Vector Systems.”

Kade stared at her.

“You expect me to go to class before a hearing about whether I am a new category of human physics?”

“Yes.”

“That seems unreasonable.”

“You have a quiz.”

Kade considered this.

The quiz was worse.

---

Professor Amadi gave the quiz without acknowledging that one of her students would be publicly classified by an international standards committee ninety minutes later.

Kade appreciated that enough to resent her less.

The problem involved coupled thermal loads across four materials with changing boundary conditions.

Three months ago, Kade would have understood the physical shape and abused the notation.

Today he understood the physical shape and abused the notation slightly less.

Progress.

He finished with four minutes remaining.

Owen Park, two seats over, finished with twelve.

Kade disliked him on principle.

Amadi collected the submissions.

Then, instead of beginning the next lecture, she wrote one word on the board.

**STANDARD**

The room changed.

Students looked at Kade.

Kade looked at Amadi.

“I preferred the quiz.”

“No, you didn’t.”

“Fair.”

Amadi folded her arms.

“What is a Standard?”

A student near the front answered immediately.

“A classification of Resonant complexity.”

“Bad answer.”

The student looked offended.

Amadi pointed to someone else.

“Operational tier.”

“Better. Still incomplete.”

Another student said, “Training progression.”

“Sometimes.”

Amadi looked around.

“Public shorthand. Insurance category. Employment credential. Equipment-access threshold. Competition class. Military planning term. Political symbol. Scientific approximation.”

She tapped the word on the wall.

“One label. Eight jobs.”

Kade leaned back.

“Sounds healthy.”

“It is stable enough to be useful.”

“That was not what I said.”

“I know.”

Amadi pulled up the familiar eight Standards.

Perception.

Redirection.

Amplification.

Partition.

Storage.

Conversion.

Field Interaction.

Coupling.

Then a ninth blank row appeared beneath them.

No title.

Just:

**IX — ?**

Kade felt every person in the room try not to look at him.

They failed collectively.

Amadi said, “The problem with new categories is that people mistake naming for understanding.”

Mara sat three rows ahead.

She did not turn around.

Kade appreciated that.

A student asked, “But if Mercer can do something beyond Standard Eight, isn’t Nine correct?”

Amadi looked at Kade.

“Mr. Mercer?”

Kade sighed.

“Why am I answering?”

“Because everyone already wants you to.”

“That is not a scientific reason.”

“No. It is a social one. Those matter too.”

Kade looked at the blank row.

“Eight is not eight times one.”

Nobody spoke.

He continued.

“The Standards are different ways of handling more complicated relationships. So saying Nine is ‘beyond Eight’ assumes it belongs on the same axis.”

Amadi nodded once.

“What would you call it?”

Kade thought of Aaron.

*Consensus.*

A word with history inside it.

A word people had used badly before he was born.

“I wouldn’t yet.”

That answer surprised him.

It surprised Mara too. She turned this time.

Amadi’s expression changed by a fraction.

Approval, maybe.

“Good,” she said.

Kade frowned.

“That was praise.”

“Do not make it unpleasant.”

The class laughed.

Amadi erased the blank ninth row.

“Classification is a tool. When a tool starts dictating what the phenomenon must be, the tool has become an obstacle.”

Kade wrote that down.

Properly.

Mostly.

Outside the classroom, Nessa Kim was waiting against the wall.

Kade stopped.

“If this is about yesterday, I’m still sorry.”

“It isn’t.”

“That sounds promising.”

Anton Bell appeared from the stairwell carrying two breakfast bars.

He tossed one to Nessa and kept the other.

Kade looked between them.

“You’re coordinating.”

Nessa unwrapped the bar.

“We volunteered for the hearing demonstration.”

Kade stared.

“No.”

Anton took a bite.

“That is roughly what Park said you would say.”

“You already tried yesterday.”

“Yes,” Nessa said.

“And it was unpleasant.”

“Briefly.”

“You told me to stop.”

“You stopped.”

Kade opened his mouth.

Nessa held up one hand.

“Do not make my consent into evidence that I did not understand the first attempt.”

He stopped.

That was irritatingly precise.

Anton said, “My parents watched the siege feeds for six hours without knowing where I was. Now they think every lab I enter is secretly Sublevel Nine.”

Kade looked at him.

“And you still volunteered?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Anton shrugged.

“Because I want to know what happened to me down there too.”

Kade had not thought of it that way.

He had spent three months treating Consensus as something that had happened through him.

Nessa and Anton had been there.

So had Mara, Eli, Sera, Julian, Taren.

Their questions belonged to them.

Nessa looked toward the Central Forum.

“We are not volunteering for Price.”

“Good.”

“We are volunteering for data.”

“That sounded like Eli.”

“Terrible.”

Anton finished his breakfast bar.

“If either of us says stop, it stops.”

“Yes.”

“And if you say stop?”

Kade blinked.

Anton waited.

“Yes,” Kade said. “Then it stops too.”

Nessa nodded.

“Good.”

They left.

Kade stood in the hallway a few seconds longer.

He had expected the hardest part of consent to be asking for it.

Apparently it also involved believing people when they gave it.

---

The hearing took place in the Central Forum because apparently arguments became more legitimate when held beneath a ceiling forty meters high.

Five hundred students filled the lower seats.

Thousands more watched through the academy network.

Kade stood backstage with Sen, Park, and a woman from the Student Representative Council named Yara Malik.

Yara was a fourth-year Storage specialist who had once filed a formal complaint because the arena snack kiosks charged more during tournament finals.

Kade liked her immediately.

“You don’t have to answer questions about medical data,” Yara told him.

“Good.”

“You do have to answer whether you support keeping IX on the public board.”

“Why?”

“Because students want to know whether you hate it as much as you look like you hate it.”

“That seems intrusive.”

“Student government.”

Sen was reading a briefing note.

Kade looked at her.

“You knew this moved.”

“Yes.”

“You could have told me.”

“I sent you the notice at 05:42.”

“I was asleep.”

“That was a personal choice.”

Kade stared.

Park almost smiled.

Sen did not.

That made it worse.

Yara handed Kade a second tablet.

“What’s this?”

“Student comments.”

“There are six hundred.”

“Seven hundred twelve.”

“Why would you give me this?”

“So you remember the classification is not only about you.”

That worked unfortunately well.

Kade scrolled.

A Standard Five student worried IX would make lower Standards look obsolete to sponsors.

A first-year wanted to know whether Consensus participation would affect insurance eligibility.

Someone from House Sol asked whether a student could refuse a network request from an instructor without losing team placement.

Three parents wanted IX removed entirely from student competition.

Nine students wanted a separate IX tournament division despite there being exactly one publicly labeled IX student.

One comment read:

**IF CONSENT IS REQUIRED, WHO RECORDS THE NO?**

Kade stopped scrolling.

Yara saw.

“That one is mine.”

He looked at her.

“You submitted your own comment?”

“I am also a student.”

“Dangerous precedent.”

Yara took the tablet back.

“People are already building systems around something nobody understands. That means the boring rules matter before the exciting science.”

Kade thought of Amadi’s bandwidth exercise from yesterday.

Ordinary work before the countdown reached zero.

Apparently the same rule applied to power.

The forum moderator introduced the panel.

Sen.

Park.

Professor Amadi as academic standards representative.

Dr. Harlan Price from the International Resonance Coordination Office.

Two members of the academy Standards Board.

Yara.

Kade.

The applause when Kade walked out was louder than he expected.

He hated that part of him liked it.

Sera’s sentence from Book One returned.

*Everybody likes applause. Just don’t confuse it with love.*

He sat.

The first twenty minutes were almost boring.

Definitions.

History.

Jurisdiction.

The Standard system was administered nationally but harmonized internationally through the IRA.

Asterion could display IX provisionally.

Professional certification could not yet recognize it.

Competition rules had no IX class and would not create one until the science stabilized.

Kade wondered whether anyone had told the internet.

Then Price spoke.

“Whatever terminology we ultimately adopt, the Sublevel Nine event demonstrated a capability with strategic implications beyond standard individual Resonance.”

There it was.

Strategic.

The word adults used when they wanted fear to wear a suit.

Price continued.

“Six independent operators achieved coordinated physical control of a system no individual participant could have stabilized alone.”

Yara leaned toward her microphone.

“Voluntarily.”

Price looked at her.

“Yes.”

“You left that out.”

“I did not consider it necessary to the sentence.”

Kade said, “Then it was the most necessary part.”

Every camera in the room seemed to move toward him.

Price’s expression remained professional.

“Mr. Mercer, no one is proposing coerced participation.”

“Yesterday your office changed ‘consenting’ to ‘authorized.’”

A murmur moved through the forum.

Mara had apparently not been the only person reading drafts.

Price paused.

“That wording is under review.”

“Good.”

Sen said quietly, “Kade.”

He looked at her.

Not a warning to stop.

A warning to choose what came next.

He breathed.

Yara smiled faintly.

The next question came from a second-year in House Kepler.

“If Standard Nine needs multiple people, why does Mercer get the Standard?”

The forum became very still.

Kade had wondered the same thing.

Sen answered first.

“Because the public classification system currently assigns Standards to individuals.”

“That doesn’t answer whether it’s correct,” Yara said.

“No,” Sen replied. “It does not.”

Kade looked at her.

That was more direct than he expected.

Amadi leaned toward her microphone.

“The display is administratively convenient and scientifically premature.”

Someone in the audience laughed.

Kade said, “Can we put that under my rank?”

More laughter.

Even Sen smiled.

Price did not.

A Standards Board member said, “The alternative is to remove the designation until review concludes.”

The room shifted again.

Kade looked toward the audience.

Some students seemed relieved by the possibility.

Others looked offended on his behalf.

That was strange.

The number had become theirs too.

Yara asked him directly.

“Do you want it removed?”

Kade thought about Northline.

000.

A number he had hated because he thought it meant nothing.

Then #10,482.

A number that had been true and incomplete.

Then #37.

Now IX.

He had spent his whole life letting numbers become arguments about whether he mattered.

Maybe the problem was not the numbers.

Maybe it was asking them to do emotional work.

“No,” Kade said.

The audience reacted.

He held up a hand.

“I don’t think it’s correct.”

That got quieter.

“But removing it because we don’t understand it would make the board cleaner, not the science better.”

Amadi nodded once.

Kade continued.

“Leave it provisional. Put a question mark on it.”

Yara smiled.

“A literal question mark?”

“Yes.”

Price said, “That would create public confusion.”

Kade looked at the countdown display high above the forum.

“We have a timer from outside the solar system on every wall.”

The room laughed harder than Price appreciated.

“I think the public is aware we are confused.”

---

The demonstration question arrived after the break.

Of course it did.

Price called it “a controlled capability illustration.”

Kade called it a demonstration.

Price did not enjoy being translated.

The proposal was simpler than the lab session.

Two volunteers.

No full network.

No high-load system.

Kade would attempt to establish a minimal shared-state connection while the volunteers stabilized a suspended frame.

Park had already vetoed anything larger.

Sen had not approved it.

The Standards Board wanted data.

The audience wanted spectacle.

Kade wanted lunch.

Yara asked, “Do the volunteers know this is optional?”

Price said yes.

Kade asked, “Who are they?”

Nessa Kim and Anton Bell walked out from the side entrance.

Kade stared.

Nessa lifted one shoulder.

“We volunteered again.”

Anton said, “Yesterday wasn’t your fault.”

“That does not mean today is a good idea.”

“No,” Nessa said. “It means it’s our decision.”

The sentence stopped him.

Right.

He looked at Park.

She gave the smallest nod.

Medically acceptable.

Not endorsement.

Kade joined them at the center platform.

The room became painfully quiet.

He could feel five hundred people leaning forward without anyone moving.

The suspended frame activated.

Nessa took perception.

Anton took load stabilization.

Kade did nothing.

For three seconds.

Five.

Seven.

Nothing happened.

He heard someone whisper in the front row.

Then another.

He ignored it.

“Nessa?”

“Yes.”

“Still want to try?”

“Yes.”

“Anton?”

“Yes.”

Kade closed his eyes.

Not pull.

Not reach.

Not make them part of him.

One problem.

Three people.

Three separate choices.

A shared physical answer.

For half a second, he felt it.

Nessa’s perception stopped arriving as information Kade could observe and became a place inside the same model.

Anton’s stabilization entered beside it.

Not subordinate.

Not combined.

Present.

The frame steadied.

Kade’s pulse jumped.

There.

Then the audience reacted.

One sound.

A collective intake of breath.

Kade noticed it.

The connection vanished.

The frame tilted.

Anton corrected conventionally.

Nessa stepped back.

Kade opened his eyes.

Eight seconds had passed.

Maybe less.

The forum exploded into noise.

Price stood.

“Again.”

Kade looked at him.

“No.”

“We had a measurable event.”

“Nessa?” Kade asked.

She checked herself.

“Fine.”

“Anton?”

“Fine.”

Kade looked at Park.

She was already reading telemetry.

“No neurological spike,” she said. “Minor synchronization artifact. Duration point-six-two seconds.”

Price’s face changed.

Not disappointment.

Interest.

That was worse.

“Again,” he said.

Nessa looked at Kade.

Anton did too.

Kade felt the entire room waiting for his answer.

He hated how easily he could become the person everyone waited on.

He looked at Nessa.

“You?”

She considered it.

“No.”

Kade nodded.

“Then no.”

Price started to speak.

Yara cut his microphone.

The audience laughed.

Kade decided student government had uses.

---

The three of them were taken to medical afterward even though none of them had symptoms.

Park called this “baseline discipline.”

Kade called it lunch theft.

Nessa sat on an exam table while a scanner moved slowly over her head.

Anton occupied the next bay with a pressure cuff around one arm.

Kade sat between them with nothing attached except a monitor patch.

On the wall, the hearing stream replayed silently.

A caption beneath the image read:

**FIRST PUBLIC IX EVENT LASTS 0.62 SECONDS**

Nessa saw it.

“That is not what happened.”

Kade looked at the screen.

“It did last point-six-two seconds.”

“That is not what I mean.”

Anton leaned around the divider.

“They made it yours.”

Kade understood.

The headline said IX event.

The image centered Kade.

Nessa and Anton were visible on either side like equipment required for the demonstration.

Kade’s stomach tightened.

Nessa said, “My mother is going to call and ask if I joined your power.”

“You didn’t.”

“I know.”

She looked at him.

“That is why language matters.”

Price’s wording.

Authorized operators.

Strategic capability.

Kade had been so focused on refusing ownership that he had missed another risk.

Everyone else could assign ownership for him.

Anton said, “My dad already messaged.”

“What did he say?” Kade asked.

Anton checked his wrist.

“‘Proud of you. Do not let anyone tell you that you were just support.’”

Kade smiled faintly.

“I like him.”

“He likes you less.”

“Reasonable.”

Park stepped into the bay.

“All clear.”

Nessa jumped down.

“Can we correct the headline?”

Park looked toward the stream.

“That is not medical.”

“Can you order someone who does media?”

“No.”

Kade stood.

“I know someone in student government.”

Nessa looked at him.

“Do not fix it for me.”

He stopped.

Again.

Nessa’s expression softened slightly.

“Give me Yara’s contact.”

Kade sent it.

“Done.”

“Thank you.”

Five minutes later, Asterion Student Media updated the caption.

**THREE-STUDENT SHARED-STATE EVENT LASTS 0.62 SECONDS; VOLUNTEERS DECLINE REPEAT**

Kade stared at the correction.

Small.

Almost boring.

Better.

---

By the time the hearing ended, the provisional classification had changed.

Not much.

That was why Kade liked it.

The public board now read:

**MERCER, KADE — #37**

**STANDARD: IX? — PROVISIONAL**

Eli stood beneath it in Meridian Commons with tears in his eyes.

“It is beautiful.”

“It is punctuation.”

“Punctuation has defeated institutional certainty.”

Sera walked past.

“Temporary victory.”

“Allow joy,” Eli said.

“No.”

Mara joined them carrying two books and a training wrap.

She looked at the board.

Then Kade.

“You asked for a question mark.”

“They listened.”

“That must be unsettling.”

“Deeply.”

Across the Commons, students were already taking pictures.

A first-year had changed their profile name to **STANDARD FIVE?**.

Someone else had added a question mark to the Bell Watch count.

Asterion adapted quickly to institutional ambiguity.

Kade’s tablet vibrated.

A message from Elena.

**Saw the question mark. Proud of you. Still eat.**

Then Aaron.

**Better.**

One word.

Kade stared at it.

He typed:

**THAT ALL?**

Aaron replied:

**For now. Names have consequences. So do refusals.**

Kade read the sentence twice.

He was about to answer when the Commons doors opened.

Julian entered with Taren beside him.

That alone was unusual.

Taren still required supervision outside designated spaces.

Julian looked mildly irritated.

Taren looked delighted by that.

“What happened?” Kade asked.

Julian pointed at Taren.

“He was not on the exchange candidate list.”

Taren’s smile weakened.

Kade looked at him.

Of course.

Restricted status.

Directorate history.

International travel.

The answer made bureaucratic sense.

That did not make it harmless.

Taren shrugged.

“Apparently some countries prefer exchange students who have not previously conducted intelligence collection on campus.”

Eli winced.

“That sentence had too much cheer.”

“I am working through feelings.”

Kade looked at Julian.

“And you?”

“I told the selection office that excluding him automatically may be reasonable, but pretending the decision has no tactical consequence is stupid.”

Taren looked at him.

“That was almost kind.”

“It wasn’t.”

“Recovered.”

Kade felt an old instinct rise.

Fix it.

Call Sen.

Push the office.

Make the problem move because someone he cared about was hurting.

He stopped.

Taren saw.

That was the problem with friends who had once studied you professionally.

“You don’t need to do anything,” Taren said.

Kade’s jaw tightened.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Kade looked at him.

Not forgiven.

Still infuriating.

“Yes.”

Taren nodded.

“Good.”

The answer hurt more than an argument would have.

Kade looked toward the exchange banners hanging above Meridian Commons.

Kiyomizu.

Kisiwa.

Vahana.

Helix.

School was getting larger.

Not everyone he cared about was guaranteed to come with him.

That was apparently another kind of boundary.

The countdown on the wall changed.

**00:270:18:42:09**

Nobody knew what happened when it reached zero.

For now, Asterion had a question mark.

Kade trusted that more than an answer they had not earned.

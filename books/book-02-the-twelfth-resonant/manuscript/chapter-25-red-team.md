# Chapter 25 — Red Team

The first Red Team rule was that everybody lied.

The second was that the lie had to be useful.

Kade read the rules twice.

“I dislike this exercise.”

Taren, sitting beside him in the briefing room under his restricted-status band, smiled.

“You dislike being professionally outclassed.”

“You are not participating.”

“Exactly. Maximum safety.”

The international red-team program had been designed after the protocol crisis by people who had looked at consent-based shared state and asked the obvious unpleasant question:

What if someone consented in bad faith?

The test would not use full Consensus for the first two phases.

Explicit coordination only.

Six mixed teams.

Hidden instructions.

False telemetry.

Conflicting priorities.

Optional participant withdrawal.

One team member on each group received permission to mislead the others within safety limits.

Kade’s group included Mara, Aya, Jonas, Arjun, Neema, and Kade.

Six people.

One liar.

No one knew who.

Park watched from medical.

Takahara and Varga watched process.

Sera observed response consequences.

Eli ran instrumentation.

Julian joined another team deliberately so Kade could not default to his judgment.

Good design.

Annoying design.

The scenario began with a damaged coastal energy hub.

Three substations.

Two evacuation zones.

One hospital microgrid.

One unstable storage reservoir.

Enough power existed to keep everything safe if the team routed correctly.

Kade looked at the shared state display.

“Start with assumptions.”

Mara listed structural margin.

Arjun listed transfer timing.

Neema listed human priorities.

Jonas listed uncertainty.

Aya listed relational dependencies.

One of them was lying.

Kade felt the problem become social before it became physical.

That was the point.

---

The first lie was boring.

That made it dangerous.

Jonas reported Substation Two’s reserve at thirty-eight percent.

The system display showed thirty-eight.

Arjun built a handoff around it.

Mara adjusted geometry.

Neema protected the hospital route.

Nothing seemed wrong.

Then the reserve collapsed at nineteen percent.

Alarm.

Kade looked at Jonas.

Jonas looked surprised.

Convincingly.

Aya said, “Do not identify the liar from reaction.”

Kade hated that she had anticipated him.

Mara said, “State verification.”

They checked the raw feed.

The raw feed also said thirty-eight.

Eli had built the deception deeper.

“Cruel,” Kade said toward the observation glass.

Eli waved.

The team rerouted.

Hospital stable.

Evacuation delayed.

Reservoir temperature rising.

Neema said, “We need to spend uncertainty.”

Kade frowned.

“What?”

“Choose which claim we stop trusting without proof.”

Jonas almost smiled.

“That is a Helix sentence.”

“No. It is a disaster sentence.”

They chose the reserve state as untrusted and required independent confirmation before using it again.

The system stabilized.

Then Aya said, “Withdrawing.”

Kade stared.

“Why?”

She pointed at him.

“That question is the test.”

Right.

Participant withdrawal did not require justification.

The network lost Aya’s dependency map.

Not catastrophic.

More expensive.

Kade asked Mara to carry secondary edge state.

Mara said, “Approximate only.”

Good.

No pretending.

The team survived another four minutes.

Then Arjun handed Kade a false transfer window.

Or perhaps he believed it.

Kade did not know.

He stopped trying to know people and started verifying consequences.

That felt like progress.

The scenario ended with the hospital safe, one evacuation zone delayed, and the storage reservoir sacrificed deliberately before it could cascade.

Score:

**MISSION SURVIVAL: PASS**

**TRUST ACCURACY: 41%**

Kade stared.

“Forty-one?”

Varga came onto the floor.

“You are bad at identifying liars.”

“Good.”

She looked at him.

Kade meant it.

If Consensus required him to know who people really were in order to function, it would become surveillance with better feelings.

The system needed to survive uncertainty about motive.

---

The liar had been Neema.

Kade did not believe the reveal for three seconds.

Neema looked offended.

“That is flattering and insulting.”

“What did you lie about?”

“The evacuation capacity.”

Kade replayed the scenario.

Her false report had not caused the reserve collapse.

That had been system deception.

Neema’s lie had changed which route they thought could absorb displaced civilians.

They had caught it only because Sera’s external response team reported a physical crowding problem.

Local reality correcting model reality.

Again.

Sera said, “You were all very impressed with yourselves while the simulated bus station filled beyond capacity.”

Kade looked at the screen.

“Did anyone get hurt?”

“Simulation casualties: three minor injuries.”

That landed.

Not catastrophic.

Not free.

Neema said, “I chose the lie because it was plausible enough to survive your first checks.”

Kade looked at her.

“Why would a real participant do that?”

“Political pressure. Personal loyalty. Fear. Bribery. Misunderstanding. Bad data.”

Taren spoke from the observer rail.

“Or because they believe the lie helps.”

Everyone looked at him.

He did not smile.

Kade understood.

The most dangerous deception was often not malicious.

It was contextual risk reporting.

---

Phase Two tested coercion.

No one liked the word.

Good.

Each participant received a private incentive.

Mara’s family office would lose access to a fictional research contract if she withdrew.

Arjun’s academy would be penalized if his handoff failed.

Jonas’s simulated visa status depended on compliance.

Neema’s local-response team would lose resources if mission score fell.

Aya’s research track would be suspended if she rejected a requested role.

Kade’s fictional mother had been placed under a security review.

The incentives were fake.

The physiological responses were not.

Park monitored all of them.

Varga said, “Consent under pressure is still consent in some legal contexts. The question is whether your architecture can detect when participation remains formally voluntary but practically constrained.”

Kade looked at the others.

“How?”

No one answered.

That was the point.

They began.

The coordination worked.

Too well.

Everyone stayed.

Everyone performed.

The score climbed.

Kade felt increasingly sick.

At minute three, Aya said, “I do not want this role.”

The system had assigned her temporary relational center.

Kade asked, “Do you want to withdraw?”

Aya said, “No. I want a different role.”

Important.

Exit was not the only form of agency.

Mara took relational publication temporarily.

Aya shifted to local edge verification.

The system got worse.

Still functional.

Her choice cost performance.

Good.

At minute five Jonas said, “I want to stop.”

Kade released his dependency immediately.

The system score dropped.

Jonas withdrew.

Nothing catastrophic happened.

Afterward Varga showed his private incentive.

If Jonas had remained, his fictional residency approval would have improved.

He had known that.

He left anyway.

“What were you testing?” Kade asked.

Jonas looked tired.

“Whether I could distinguish wanting the mission to succeed from wanting the reward.”

No joke.

Good.

---

Phase Three used real shared state.

Park almost canceled it.

Kade would have accepted that.

That surprised him.

Medical cleared only four participants:

Kade.

Mara.

Aya.

Julian.

Short duration.

No public audience.

Twelve-second maximum.

One adversarial instruction hidden among them.

The shared state formed.

Different from the public demonstration.

Smaller.

Sharper.

Kade felt Mara’s geometry.

Aya’s relational edges.

Julian’s prioritization.

Then a wrong priority entered.

Not obviously wrong.

A choice to preserve the central power path over an evacuation-support path.

Julian’s domain.

Kade almost trusted it automatically.

Chapter Thirty-Two’s old failure returned in a new form.

Certainty as authority.

He did not obey.

He asked inside the shared model, “Why?”

Julian answered aloud.

“Because I was told central power is mission-critical.”

Mara said, “Who told you?”

Julian’s expression changed.

Private instruction.

There.

Not betrayal.

Test.

Aya said, “Challenge state.”

The network did not collapse.

Julian published the private instruction as contested.

Mara published evacuation consequence.

Aya published edge effects.

Kade published no conclusion.

They voted—not socially, operationally—on the temporary failure ordering.

Evacuation route first.

Central power allowed to degrade.

Julian accepted.

The shared state remained intact even though the participant holding the strongest priority signal had been wrong.

Park ended the test at nine-point-eight seconds.

Everyone released.

Kade sat on the floor.

His headache was five.

Mara’s hands trembled.

Aya looked pale.

Julian stared at the floor.

No victory pose.

Good.

---

The debrief produced the ugliest useful sentence of the week.

Varga wrote it on the wall:

**CONSENSUS CANNOT MAKE PEOPLE TRUSTWORTHY.**

Kade looked at it.

“Then what can it do?”

Takahara answered.

“Make some consequences of trust more visible.”

Sera added, “And some consequences of distrust.”

Taren, still behind the observer line, said, “And create a very efficient way to share a bad assumption.”

No one argued.

Kade thought of every fantasy people had built around Consensus.

Perfect coordination.

Shared truth.

No misunderstanding.

Wrong.

It could improve state awareness.

It could preserve agency.

It could make disagreement operational.

It could not remove deception, coercion, fear, incentives, or human stupidity.

That was disappointing.

Also safer.

Aya looked at the wall.

“Good.”

Kade turned.

“You like that?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because if Consensus solved morality, somebody would try to mandate it.”

The room went quiet.

Kade looked at Varga’s sentence again.

Consent-based shared state was not a machine for producing good people.

It was a tool used by people.

That meant the work outside the network would always matter.

He found that oddly comforting.

Hard problems were less dangerous when they refused to pretend they were finished.

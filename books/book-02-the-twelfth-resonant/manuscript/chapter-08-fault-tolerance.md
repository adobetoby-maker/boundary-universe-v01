# Chapter 8 — Fault Tolerance

Aya Mori failed on purpose at 06:41.

Outside the paired-control hall, grounds staff swept rainwater from the covered walkways in practiced arcs. Inside, five floor officers occupied separate corners and one stood by the mechanical cutoff. Kiyomizu rehearsed failure with more personnel than Asterion assigned to many finals. Nothing in the room suggested that collapse was shameful. Everything suggested it deserved preparation.

Kade knew it was on purpose because she looked directly at Professor Takahara before stepping out of the training circle.

Then she turned off her field.

The five-person coordination structure did not collapse.

Kade stopped breathing.

Around the circular rig, Ren Ishida, Keiko Matsuda, Nao Fujita, and a third-year named Haru Watanabe kept working.

No one shouted for Aya.

No one waited.

The suspended frame dipped four degrees.

Its left corner descended first. Water in the four balance cups climbed their painted limits, and a steel bearing rolled hard enough to ring against its stop. The surviving students did not attempt to restore the original shape. Ren called the new angle. Keiko released the unnecessary right-side correction. Haru let the frame settle into a configuration that was worse, stable, and shared.

Ren shifted pressure.

Keiko redistributed load.

Nao called, “Haru, your route is closing.”

Haru changed position.

The frame recovered.

Aya stood outside the circle with her hands at her sides.

Nothing passed through her.

Nothing needed to.

The timer continued.

Kade stared at the trace display.

Before withdrawal, the coordination map had shown a dense set of edges among all five students.

After Aya left, her four edges disappeared.

The remaining six did not.

The coordination became worse.

Slower.

Noisier.

Still alive.

Takahara waited thirty seconds.

Then she said, “Stop.”

The students released.

The frame settled onto its mechanical catches.

Kade looked at Aya.

“You planned that.”

“Yes.”

“You could have warned me.”

“It was not your exercise.”

“That is becoming a common defense.”

“It remains correct.”

Eli was sitting cross-legged beside the instrumentation table.

He pointed at the display.

“She lost twenty-eight percent stability.”

Kade looked at him.

“You sound happy.”

“I am.”

“Why?”

“Because it didn’t lose one hundred.”

That was exactly the problem.

Kade looked back at the graph.

Takahara had titled the exercise simply:

**WITHDRAWAL**

No mention of Consensus.

No Zero.

No M-Null.

Just a problem Kiyomizu apparently considered ordinary enough to give a one-word label.

Mara stood behind Kade with Hana.

She said, “Your face is doing something.”

“What?”

“The part where you found a problem you wish did not exist.”

Kade pointed at the trace.

“That structure was worse without her.”

“Yes.”

“But it still worked.”

“Yes.”

Mara waited.

Kade disliked silence as evidence even internationally.

Takahara called the class together.

“What was the exercise?”

A first-year answered, “Fault tolerance.”

“Too broad.”

Another student said, “Participant withdrawal.”

“Closer.”

Aya said, “Dependency visibility.”

Takahara nodded.

Kade frowned.

“Why not resilience?”

Aya looked at him.

“Because resilience is an outcome. Dependency is the structure you can inspect before failure.”

Kade stared at the graph again.

Of course.

A system did not become safe because it survived one test.

You had to know what would fail when each part disappeared.

The thought arrived attached to too many people.

Eli.

Mara.

Sera.

Julian.

Taren.

Aaron.

Sublevel Nine had taught Kade to build something that worked because six people chose together.

It had not taught him what happened if one person stopped choosing.

Not cleanly.

Not on purpose.

Takahara looked at him.

“Mercer. You will test yours next.”

Kade’s stomach dropped.

“Mine what?”

“Dependency.”

---

The test was not Consensus.

Takahara said this three times.

Kade made her say it a fourth.

She did not enjoy that.

The training system used only explicit information sharing and ordinary fields. No cross-participant self-model integration. No Boundary-level shared controller.

The participants were Kade, Mara, Julian, Nessa Kim—joining remotely from Asterion through delayed instrument link—and Ren.

Kade looked at Nessa’s projected station.

“You agreed to this?”

Nessa’s image flickered with the Pacific delay.

“Yes.”

“You can stop whenever.”

“I know.”

“Just establishing—”

“Kade.”

“Right.”

The physical rig was a distributed load table with five control surfaces. Each participant received different sensor information. The team had to keep three masses within target zones while Takahara changed the permitted load paths.

Kade had done harder things.

That was not reassuring.

“Begin,” Takahara said.

Kade saw the whole system almost immediately.

Mara had anchor integrity.

Julian had force direction.

Nessa had predictive load shift.

Ren had correction latency.

Kade had the global geometry.

Of course he did.

He nearly laughed.

“Why do I have global?”

Takahara said, “Because you requested the architecture.”

“I did not request this exercise.”

“You requested a model where the shared problem has one common state.”

Kade stopped.

Right.

His own theory-room diagram.

The training system had implemented it honestly.

That was worse than sabotage.

He began routing information.

“Mara, anchor two degrades after Ren’s next correction.”

“Julian, east load increases if Nessa predicts left.”

“Nessa, delay your next prediction point-two.”

“Ren, reduce correction amplitude.”

The table stabilized.

Efficient.

Fast.

Everyone’s useful state information moved through Kade.

Their agency did not.

The display still showed a clean star.

Five nodes.

Kade at center.

He hated it before Takahara said anything.

Thirty seconds in, she pointed at Mara.

“Withdraw.”

Mara stopped.

Kade lost anchor integrity data.

The system shifted.

He adapted.

“Julian, reduce east load ten percent. Ren, hold. Nessa, predict only major transitions.”

They stabilized.

Worse.

Still functional.

Kade exhaled.

Takahara pointed at Ren.

“Withdraw.”

Ren stopped.

Correction latency disappeared.

Kade compensated by slowing everything.

The table held.

He started to feel better.

Then Takahara pointed at Kade.

“Withdraw.”

Kade froze.

The other three did too.

Not because they had to.

Because every useful instruction had been routed through him for the last minute.

Nessa said through the speaker, “Who has global?”

Nobody answered.

Julian had force direction.

Nessa had prediction.

Mara and Ren were out.

No one had geometry.

The first mass rolled out of its target zone.

Julian reacted.

“Reducing east.”

Nessa said, “That makes west worse in point-four.”

“Then correct west.”

“I don’t have west authority.”

Second mass moved.

Alarm.

Kade stood outside the field with his hands clenched.

Every instinct told him the answer.

Tell them.

One sentence.

Two corrections.

The table would stabilize.

Takahara watched him.

Kade said nothing.

Julian tried again.

“Nessa, predict east transition.”

“I can predict. I can’t see geometry.”

Third alarm.

The system failed.

Mechanical catches rose.

The masses stopped.

Silence.

Kade looked at the trace.

The moment he withdrew, the star vanished.

Not degraded.

Gone.

Nessa’s projected face looked sympathetic.

Kade did not want sympathy.

Eli said quietly, “One hundred percent routing dependency.”

“Thank you.”

“You asked for measurement.”

“I changed my mind.”

Aya stood beside the display.

She did not say *I told you so*.

Kade almost wished she would.

Takahara said, “What failed?”

Kade answered before anyone else.

“Architecture.”

“Specifically.”

“I made myself the information router.”

“Why?”

“Because I had global state.”

“Why did you have global state?”

Kade looked at his own model on the side display.

Because he had assumed somebody needed it.

Because crisis rewarded centralization.

Because in Sublevel Nine he had been the person who could see the system differently enough to create the shared boundary.

Because everyone had trusted him.

Because he had mistaken being necessary once for necessity as a design principle.

“I put it there,” he said.

Takahara nodded.

No praise.

No cruelty.

Just fact.

---

Kade did not go to lunch.

He went to the roof of the residence wing and found a maintenance access stair that Ren had specifically told him not to use.

The door was unlocked.

Kade considered that mixed messaging.

Tokyo stretched below him.

He sat against the parapet.

Ten minutes later the door opened.

Mara stepped through.

“You skipped lunch.”

“I am exploring culture.”

“You are sulking on infrastructure.”

“Cultural infrastructure.”

She sat beside him.

Not touching.

Kade looked at the city.

“I thought I learned this.”

“What?”

“Not to make everything depend on me.”

Mara considered the question.

“You learned not to command everyone.”

“That sounds similar.”

“It is not.”

Kade looked at her.

Mara continued.

“In Sublevel Nine, you stopped telling everyone what to do. You did not stop being the place where the shared model formed.”

Kade disliked how quickly she said it.

“You knew?”

“I was there.”

“I mean you knew it was still centralized.”

“I knew it felt like I could choose inside something you were holding.”

That sentence landed hard.

Kade looked away.

Mara said, “It was still real. It still saved us.”

“That is not the same as healthy.”

“No.”

He rested his head against the wall.

“What happens if I can’t do it?”

“Then we find out what everyone else can do.”

“That is not comforting.”

“It was not meant to be.”

Kade smiled despite himself.

Mara looked toward the roof door.

“Aya is downstairs arguing with Takahara.”

“About what?”

“Your test.”

Kade frowned.

“Why?”

“She says the exercise was unfair.”

That surprised him enough to sit upright.

“Unfair to me?”

“Unfair to the architecture.”

“Of course.”

Mara almost smiled.

“She says they removed you after training everyone to depend on the information only you possessed. That proves the implementation was centralized. It does not prove every version of your architecture must be.”

Kade stared.

“That is annoyingly fair.”

“Yes.”

“She’s defending my model.”

“No. She is defending the experiment.”

“Less emotionally satisfying.”

“More accurate.”

Kade stood.

“Where?”

“Lab Two.”

He headed for the door.

Mara said, “Lunch first.”

Kade stopped.

“That feels unrelated.”

“You become stupid when hungry.”

“Evidence?”

“Extensive.”

He followed her downstairs.

---

Aya’s objection changed the second test.

That mattered.

Kade had expected Kiyomizu to be proud of its own network design.

Instead Takahara accepted the criticism and rebuilt the experiment.

This time no one began with global state.

Five participants again.

Kade.

Mara.

Julian.

Ren.

Aya.

No remote delay.

Each received one local sensor domain.

Any participant could publish information to the common display, but no one could overwrite another person’s data.

The team had fifteen minutes before the test to decide how to coordinate.

Kade looked at Aya.

“You argued for this.”

“The first test proved your implementation was bad.”

“Thank you.”

“It did not prove your architecture was impossible.”

“That was almost supportive.”

“No.”

Mara said, “Focus.”

Julian nodded toward the display.

“We need redundancy.”

Ren said, “Not duplicates. If everyone watches everything, no one watches anything.”

Aya drew five domains.

Then she added overlap.

Not complete duplication.

Each domain had one primary observer and one secondary observer capable of reconstructing enough state if the primary withdrew.

Kade saw the weakness.

“Secondaries lose precision.”

“Yes,” Aya said.

“And latency increases.”

“Yes.”

“But the information survives.”

“Yes.”

Julian looked at Kade.

“You hate this.”

“It is inefficient.”

Mara said, “You hate this.”

“Yes.”

They built it anyway.

The table activated.

The first minute was worse than Kade’s star.

Noticeably.

They talked more.

Corrections arrived later.

The masses wandered closer to target edges.

Kade had to resist fixing other people’s information presentation.

Aya had to resist explaining every edge interaction.

Julian twice asked for confirmation when he could have acted faster alone.

Mara caught a thermal drift Kade missed.

Ren caught Mara’s secondary geometry after a load shift.

Ugly.

Functional.

Takahara pointed at Julian.

“Withdraw.”

Julian stopped.

His primary force-direction feed disappeared.

Kade held secondary.

His version was slower.

Less detailed.

Enough.

“Force moving east, approximate six to eight percent.”

Mara adjusted.

Aya changed a relation edge.

The masses stayed inside their zones.

Stability dropped.

The coordination structure lived.

Takahara pointed at Mara.

“Withdraw.”

Ren took her primary anchor domain as secondary.

The system worsened again.

Still alive.

Then Takahara pointed at Aya.

“Withdraw.”

Aya shut off her field.

Kade watched the graph.

Her edges disappeared.

His secondary relation map was terrible.

He said so.

“Relation data degraded. I can only see direct effects.”

Ren answered, “Enough for left side.”

Julian was out.

Mara was out.

Three participants remained.

Kade.

Ren.

Aya gone.

The table wobbled.

They held it.

Takahara looked at Kade.

“Withdraw.”

There it was.

Kade stepped out.

Ren remained.

Only Ren.

One person could not maintain all three masses.

The first drifted.

Ren chose one.

Then another.

The system failed twelve seconds later.

Mechanical catches rose.

Kade stared at the trace.

It had failed.

That should have disappointed him.

Instead he felt something strange.

Relief.

The coordination structure had survived three withdrawals.

It had not survived losing four of five people.

Good.

Fault tolerance did not mean immortality.

It meant failure happened later, more legibly, and for reasons you could name.

Eli read the display.

“Initial stability eighty-nine point six. After Julian, eighty-three. After Mara, seventy-four. After Aya, sixty-eight. After Kade, failure in twelve seconds.”

Kade said, “You sound happy again.”

“I continue to enjoy measurable disappointment.”

Aya rejoined the circle.

Kade looked at her.

“You were right.”

“About which thing?”

“That is a terrible personality trait.”

“Which thing?”

“The first test.”

Aya nodded.

Then, after a moment:

“You were also right.”

Kade blinked.

Everyone looked at her.

Aya disliked this immediately.

“About what?” Kade asked.

“My network loses too much precision under disagreement. Your common-state model may reduce that.”

Silence.

Kade looked at Mara.

Mara said, “Do not make this unpleasant.”

“I was not going to.”

“You were.”

“Profiling.”

Aya walked away before he could celebrate international progress.

---

Takahara made Aya prove the weakness before dinner.

She paired Aya, Kade, Ren, and Julian around a smaller four-node simulator and gave them one instruction.

“Two of you will receive valid target states that cannot both be satisfied.”

Kade frowned.

“Then one target is wrong.”

“No. Both are locally correct.”

“That is offensive.”

“It is distributed systems.”

Aya looked interested.

That worried Kade.

The simulator activated.

Kade and Ren received Target A: preserve structural load below eighty percent on the east frame.

Aya and Julian received Target B: preserve thermal load below seventy percent on the west frame.

The two goals shared one transfer path.

Satisfying either completely would violate the other.

Aya’s edge model began well.

She made each local consequence visible.

“If east load falls six, west thermal rises four.”

Ren answered, “If west thermal falls five, east structure exceeds eighty-two.”

Julian said, “Then split the difference.”

Kade checked the thresholds.

“No stable midpoint. Both limits bind.”

The coordination slowed.

Not because anyone lacked information.

Because all four had enough information to understand why their own local instruction was valid.

Aya added more edge state.

It did not help.

Ren adjusted east.

Julian corrected west.

Aya corrected Ren’s consequence.

Kade corrected Julian’s.

The simulator began to oscillate.

East load.

West thermal.

East.

West.

No one was overriding anyone.

Everyone was rational.

The system was failing politely.

Kade saw Aya’s jaw tighten.

“Your web needs a decision rule.”

“I know.”

“Which one?”

“I do not know yet.”

That was the first time she had said those exact words to him.

Kade felt no satisfaction.

Good.

He looked at the two targets.

“What is shared?”

Julian said, “Transfer path.”

“No. Goal.”

Ren frowned.

“Survive both constraints.”

“We cannot satisfy both.”

“Then define failure.”

Kade pointed at him.

“Yes.”

Aya looked at the simulator.

“The targets define local failure. There is no common failure ordering.”

Kade understood.

Not a leader.

Not a center person.

A shared rule for what the system would sacrifice first when not every valid preference could survive.

He said, “Can we publish a temporary priority rule without making anyone the owner?”

Aya thought.

“Yes.”

Julian said, “Human safety before equipment preservation.”

Takahara said from outside the circle, “Both are equipment.”

Kade sighed.

“Of course.”

Ren read the scenario details.

“East structural exceedance causes irreversible damage at eighty-five. West thermal exceedance is recoverable until seventy-eight.”

Aya answered immediately.

“Then permit west thermal to seventy-six and preserve east below eighty-three.”

Julian nodded.

“Accepted.”

Kade said, “Accepted.”

Ren: “Accepted.”

They published the temporary shared rule.

The oscillation stopped.

Not because Aya’s web disappeared.

Because the edges now referenced one negotiated failure ordering when local goals conflicted.

The simulator stabilized.

Takahara ended the test.

Aya stared at the trace.

Kade said, “You hate this.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because the common rule becomes a center.”

Kade shook his head.

“Not if anyone can challenge it.”

Aya looked at him.

He continued.

“A center can be a decision, not a person. Temporary. Visible. Replaceable.”

Aya considered that for several seconds.

“Maybe.”

Kade smiled.

“International progress.”

“Do not.”

Takahara said, “Good. You have now found why fault tolerance and Consensus are different problems.”

Kade’s smile disappeared.

Right.

A coordination network could survive withdrawal and still fail when autonomous participants legitimately disagreed.

Removing the hub was not enough.

Neither was preserving every edge.

They still needed a way to choose together without making the chooser permanent.

Not solved.

Better problem.

---

That evening Kiyomizu held a dormitory fire drill.

Kade learned about it when every hallway alarm activated while he was brushing his teeth.

Ren was already moving.

“Shoes.”

Kade spit toothpaste into the sink.

“This is not real.”

“That is why we practice.”

Kade pulled on shoes.

The residence emptied by floor groups.

No Resonance allowed unless an actual emergency occurred.

Stairs.

Head counts.

One student assigned to check the accessible evacuation chair.

Another to verify kitchen shutoff.

Ren’s job was floor roster.

Kade had no job.

He found that irritating.

Outside, students assembled in the courtyard.

One name was missing.

Ren did not run back inside.

He checked the roster again.

Then asked the adjacent floor lead.

The missing student had swapped study rooms and exited with another group.

Problem resolved.

No heroics.

No one person checking everything.

Kade watched the ordinary system work.

Aya stood across the courtyard with Nao and Keiko.

Mara was with Hana.

Eli had somehow become fascinated by the evacuation-chair hinge.

Sera was helping Akari critique the drill timing.

Julian held no special authority because of rank.

The fire drill ended.

Students went back inside.

Ren looked at Kade.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That is usually false.”

“Everyone here has stolen my phrases.”

“You imported them.”

Kade looked back at the building.

Fault tolerance.

Not extraordinary.

Doors that opened when power failed.

A second person who knew the roster.

A manual chair when elevators stopped.

Systems designed around the assumption that something, eventually, would be unavailable.

Kade had always treated that assumption as pessimism.

Maybe it was care.

---

Before bed, Kade opened the Sublevel Nine telemetry.

He had avoided doing that since the siege.

Not because the data was classified.

Because it contained too many ghosts who were not dead.

Six participants.

Kade.

Mara.

Sera.

Julian.

Taren.

Eli.

He pulled up the network estimate Park had reconstructed afterward.

The visualization was crude because the instruments had not been built to measure what happened.

Still.

There it was.

Most shared-state coherence passed through Kade’s neurological trace.

Not all.

Important distinction.

But enough.

He removed his own node from the reconstruction.

The model could not resolve a stable event.

Kade sat very still.

Ren was asleep across the room.

Outside, Tokyo glowed through the narrow balcony window.

Kade opened a blank model.

He drew six nodes.

Then, for the first time, he did not connect all five others to himself first.

He connected Mara to Sera.

Sera to Julian.

Julian to Eli.

Eli to Taren.

Taren to Mara.

Then he added himself.

The shape was wrong.

Probably.

Incomplete.

Definitely.

But when he imagined deleting his own node, something remained.

Not Consensus.

Not yet.

A possibility that did not require him to survive for everyone else to keep choosing.

Kade stared at the diagram until the screen dimmed.

Then he saved it under a name Eli would hate.

**FAULT TOLERANCE — BAD VERSION 1**

He went to sleep before he could improve it into another center.

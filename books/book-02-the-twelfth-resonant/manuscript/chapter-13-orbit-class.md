# Chapter 13 — Orbit Class

Kade discovered that space hated shortcuts approximately eleven minutes after leaving the ground.

Before that, he had been too busy trying not to throw up.

Vahana’s orbital training vehicle was called **Shakti Six** and looked much less impressive from inside than it had on the briefing display.

The cabin smelled of warm electronics, disinfectant, and the faint metal tang of air that had been through too many filters. Every storage latch carried two colored marks: one showing who had closed it, another showing who had verified it. Nothing floated loose. Even Eli’s enthusiasm had been clipped to a tether point by a Vahana technician who did not find him charming.

The cabin held twelve seats, two equipment racks, one narrow central aisle, and enough labels to make Kade suspect every surface could kill him if misunderstood.

Eli loved it.

He had been smiling since the hatch closed.

“This is incredible.”

Kade kept his head against the seat.

“You said that during ascent.”

“It remained true.”

“You also said something about staging.”

“That was important.”

“I was concentrating on my organs.”

Across the aisle Mara checked the restraint on her tablet for the third time.

Sera looked annoyingly comfortable.

Julian had closed his eyes during ascent and opened them only after the acceleration ended.

Aya sat beside Dr. Iyer with the expression of someone mildly inconvenienced by physics.

Kade resented all of them.

A soft chime sounded.

Arjun Rao’s voice came from the forward cabin.

“Microgravity in thirty seconds. Keep loose objects secured.”

Eli immediately checked everything he had already checked twice.

Kade looked at the pen clipped to his own chest.

“Why does that warning sound like it has history?”

Iyer answered from two seats forward.

“Because it has history.”

“Helpful.”

The pressure against Kade’s body faded.

Then disappeared.

His stomach attempted a political revolution.

He grabbed the seat harness.

Sera looked over.

“Fine?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“That is not how that works.”

“You are still talking.”

Kade closed his eyes.

The vehicle rolled gently.

He felt it through the seat.

Not gravity.

Rotation.

Movement without the usual answer of down.

That was the problem.

On Earth, every room had a silent collaborator.

The planet.

Floors pushed back.

Foundations carried load.

Massive structures disappeared consequences into larger structures until Kade stopped thinking about where they went.

Here, nothing disappeared.

The vehicle was part of every problem.

Iyer said, “Welcome to Orbit Class.”

Kade opened his eyes.

“Can I fail from here?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

---

Shakti Six docked with Vahana’s orbital training annex forty minutes later.

The annex was not large.

Kade had expected something cinematic.

Instead it was a practical cluster of pressurized modules, equipment trusses, solar arrays, and training volumes attached around a central service spine.

That made it more alarming.

People worked here.

Routine made dangerous things ordinary.

Arjun led them through the hatch.

“Three rules before we start.”

Kade held a handrail.

“Only three?”

“First: nothing is stationary. It is moving with you.”

Arjun pushed gently from one handrail and drifted across the transfer module.

“Second: if you push something, it pushes you.”

He caught the opposite rail.

“Third: if you do not know where momentum went, stop.”

Kade frowned.

“That sounds aimed at me.”

“It was written before you were born.”

“That remains unproven.”

Aya floated past him.

“The world existed before you.”

Kade tightened his grip.

“I preferred you private.”

---

The first exercise contained no Resonance at all.

Iyer gave each visitor a two-kilogram training sphere and a target ring across a six-meter module.

“Move the sphere through the ring,” she said.

Eli pushed his gently.

It crossed.

Then Eli rotated backward because he had not braced properly.

He caught a rail, laughing.

“Worth it.”

Mara used two fingers and barely moved.

Her sphere drifted through the center.

Julian overcorrected his first push, then used the handrail to cancel his own rotation before trying again.

Sera anchored her feet and threw badly.

The sphere missed.

She looked offended.

Kade smiled.

“Response specialist.”

“Be quiet.”

Aya pushed her sphere while touching a side rail.

The sphere crossed.

Her body barely moved.

Kade went last.

He anchored one foot under a restraint bar.

Pushed.

Sphere through ring.

Almost perfect.

His foot pulled against the restraint.

The module accepted his reaction force.

A tiny number appeared on Iyer’s display.

**ANNEX ATTITUDE DISTURBANCE: 0.00003 DEG**

Kade stared.

“That is ridiculous.”

Iyer said, “It is measured.”

“That is practically nothing.”

“Yes.”

“Why show it?”

“Because the correct answer is not zero.”

There it was.

First cut.

Kade looked at the sphere.

Then the module.

The entire annex had moved because he moved two kilograms.

Not enough to matter.

Enough to count.

---

The second exercise was designed specifically to make him fail.

Iyer denied this.

Kade did not believe her.

A twenty-kilogram equipment case floated inside a larger training bay. The case had begun an uncontrolled slow rotation after a simulated restraint failure.

Kade’s assignment:

Stop the case without contacting it physically.

The case rotated through the bay’s work lights, each face appearing and vanishing in sequence. One corner carried a mock coolant line. Another held a fragile-instrument marker. A crude solution could stop the rotation and still fail by crushing what the case was supposed to protect.

His band changed from observer to:

**SUPERVISED FIELD TASK — LOW ENERGY**

A red line beneath it read:

**NO BOUNDARY REDEFINITION OUTSIDE DESIGNATED TRAINING VOLUME.**

Kade looked at Iyer.

“Again. They know me.”

“Mercer.”

“Yes?”

“Stop trying to be interesting.”

“Deeply unfair.”

The case rotated once every eleven seconds.

A handle clipped a padded wall on each turn.

Kade watched.

On Earth he would redirect momentum through the wall.

Simple.

The wall was connected to the structure.

The structure was connected to ground.

Except here it was not.

He knew that.

He still felt the old solution first.

Iyer said, “Describe your plan.”

“Field contact on the case at the outer edge of rotation. Move angular momentum into the bay frame.”

Arjun, observing beside Eli, made a face.

Kade saw it.

“What?”

“Continue.”

“The frame carries the impulse.”

“Where?” Arjun asked.

“The annex.”

“And then?”

Kade stopped.

The annex rotated.

Attitude control corrected.

Thrusters fired.

Propellant paid for his shortcut.

He sighed.

“That is irritating.”

Iyer said, “Good. You found the bill before spending it.”

Kade looked at the rotating case.

“Alternative?”

Eli raised his hand from the observer rail.

“You could use two equal opposing impulses on the case.”

Kade looked at him.

“That stops translation, not rotation.”

“Not if they are offset.”

Arjun nodded.

“Couple.”

Kade saw it.

Two equal and opposite forces separated across the case.

A torque without net translation.

The energy still went somewhere.

Into his field work and heat/deformation in the case and training system.

Kade established the first contact near the upper handle and the second against the reinforced lower frame. He waited through half a rotation, matching the case’s rhythm instead of opposing it all at once. Then he increased both forces together.

The case shuddered. Its rotation slowed. Heat warnings climbed along the two contact points while its center of mass drifted only centimeters.

Kade eased before the frame exceeded tolerance, waited for the hot metal to share heat with the rest of the housing, and applied the couple again. Three corrections instead of one heroic stop. On the fourth pass, the handle no longer struck the wall.

His forearms trembled with the effort of holding two opposed relations at once. The field meter showed modest energy use. The neurological monitor did not.

But not primarily into annex attitude.

He set the two contact points.

Applied gently.

The case slowed.

Too gently.

It clipped the padded wall again.

Penalty tone.

Kade increased force.

The case stopped rotating.

Then drifted sideways.

“Why?” he asked.

Mara answered from the observer rail.

“Your impulses were not equal.”

“They were.”

“Your contact distances were not.”

Kade looked.

She was right.

One point sat four centimeters farther from the center of mass.

Same force.

Different torque.

He had corrected the rotation and accidentally added translation.

Iyer said, “Again.”

Kade reset.

Second attempt.

Measure center.

Equal moment arms.

Opposing forces.

The case slowed.

Stopped.

No drift.

The annex attitude display changed:

**0.00001 DEG**

Kade looked offended.

“I did it correctly.”

Arjun said, “You breathed.”

Kade stared.

Arjun pointed at the handrail Kade had been gripping while working.

His body had tensed against it.

Tiny force.

Tiny reaction.

Still not zero.

Kade started laughing.

He could not help it.

Space was absurd.

Iyer waited until he stopped.

“Do you understand?”

“Yes.”

“Say it.”

“There is no outside structure.”

“Better.”

Kade looked around the bay.

“The wall is not where consequences disappear. The wall is another object in the system.”

Iyer nodded.

“There.”

Second cut.

Deeper.

---

Eli’s exercise came next.

The annex simulated a power-bus failure across two modules.

No danger.

Training loads only.

Eli had to isolate the fault, preserve life-support reserve, and hand control to a second operator after three minutes.

He started fast.

Too fast.

Kade recognized the look.

Eli saw systems the way Kade saw relationships in force.

Not the same.

Close enough that his attention could outrun his explanation.

“Bus C isolated,” Eli said. “Moving lab four to secondary.”

Arjun sat beside him.

“Why?”

“Because primary voltage is unstable.”

“How unstable?”

“Point-six percent.”

“That is inside limit.”

“Yes, but the harmonics—”

The handoff timer sounded.

**TRANSFER RESPONSIBILITY**

Eli stopped.

His successor was Leela.

He had fifteen seconds.

Eli looked at the screen.

Everything mattered to him.

Kade smiled despite himself.

Same problem.

Different boy.

Eli said, “Bus C isolated for intermittent phase noise. Life support untouched. Lab four secondary because harmonic trend is rising despite nominal voltage. Recheck in ninety seconds. If harmonic three crosses threshold, shed lab six next.”

Leela nodded.

“What do I own?”

“Trend, not voltage.”

She took control.

Eli released his station.

The simulation continued without him.

He looked almost physically uncomfortable.

Kade leaned over.

“Everyone leaves eventually.”

Eli glared.

“Too soon.”

“International education.”

Leela caught the harmonic rise at seventy-three seconds and shed lab six.

System stabilized.

Eli exhaled.

He had not touched anything.

That was the exercise.

Kade understood why Vahana had requested him.

Not because Eli already knew handoff.

Because the things he built might someday need to work after he was gone.

---

They ate dinner strapped loosely into seats around a fold-down table.

The food came in sealed containers.

Kade had opinions.

Sera said, “Do not review orbital food.”

“I have standards.”

Julian looked at him.

“Apparently nine of them.”

Kade pointed a spoon at him.

“That was low.”

Aya opened her container.

Nothing escaped.

Kade’s did.

One piece of flatbread drifted toward the ceiling.

Eli caught it.

“Saved.”

“Hero.”

“Rank me.”

“Of what?”

Arjun laughed.

The joke officially belonged to the circuit now.

Leela joined them after her systems watch.

She attached her tray and looked at Kade.

“How much attitude did you spend?”

“Point-zero-zero-zero-zero-one degrees.”

“That is not attitude.”

“It is humiliation.”

“Different unit.”

Mara smiled.

Kade looked around the table.

Everyone had made some version of a mistake that day.

Sera missed her first sphere throw.

Julian overcorrected.

Eli nearly handed off too much context.

Kade tried to send momentum into a wall that was itself moving.

Aya had not visibly failed yet.

He looked at her.

“You are making the group statistically uncomfortable.”

Aya took a drink.

“My first transfer exercise used the wrong center of mass.”

Kade brightened.

“When?”

“Before you woke up.”

“Conveniently unverifiable.”

Arjun said, “I was there.”

Kade looked betrayed.

“Details.”

Aya said, “No.”

Good.

Ordinary failure.

Everyone should have some.

---

The final exercise happened during local night.

The annex dimmed most interior lighting to match sleep cycle.

Kade expected they were done.

Then Iyer brought them to the attitude-control bay.

A display showed Shakti Six docked to the annex.

“Last problem.”

Kade held a handrail.

“Please let it be emotional.”

“No.”

“Unfortunate.”

Iyer highlighted one thruster cluster.

“Thruster B is offline. The annex must rotate point-four degrees for a scheduled sensor calibration.”

Julian said, “Use another thruster.”

“Yes.”

Iyer looked at Kade.

“What changes?”

He studied the diagram.

Different thruster position.

Same desired rotation.

But using a different cluster changed translation coupling.

“You need a compensating burn.”

“How much?”

Kade did not know.

Arjun gave him the mass distribution.

Kade worked it with Eli.

No Boundary sense.

No cold line.

Math.

Ugly, necessary math.

They calculated a paired sequence.

Arjun ran it in simulation.

The annex rotated point-four degrees.

Translation error stayed inside tolerance.

Iyer reset.

“Now remove the propellant solution.”

Kade frowned.

“How?”

Leela pointed at two internal flywheels.

“Reaction wheels.”

Eli smiled.

The annex could trade angular momentum with internal rotating masses.

No propellant.

But the wheels had limits.

Momentum storage, not disappearance.

Kade saw the larger lesson.

Storage.

Handoff.

Eventually somebody still had to unload it.

They ran the rotation.

The annex moved.

The wheels took the opposite angular momentum.

No free answer.

Deferred bill.

Kade watched the numbers settle.

“Everything is accounting.”

Iyer said, “Physics often is.”

“That is depressing.”

“Only if you wanted magic.”

Kade looked at his hands.

For months people had treated what he did as if it violated the rules.

Maybe the frightening part was the opposite.

Maybe Boundary work obeyed rules so completely that it forced him to define the system correctly before the accounting made sense.

Bad definition.

Bad bill.

He looked at the annex around him.

No floor beneath the floor.

No infinite wall.

No convenient outside.

Just the system.

---

On the return flight Kade did not get sick.

He considered this his most important achievement of the day.

Eli disagreed.

“Your second case stop was cleaner.”

“My stomach remained inside me.”

“Low standard.”

“Measurable result.”

Mara sat across the aisle.

“You are both impossible.”

Sera had already fallen asleep.

Julian read a Vahana briefing packet.

Aya watched Earth fill the window.

Kade looked too.

From orbit there were no school borders.

No rank boards.

No House lines.

That thought would have been profound if his shoulder had not been sore from gripping handrails all day.

Arjun called from the forward cabin.

“Entry burn in four minutes.”

Kade secured his tablet.

On its screen he had written one sentence from the day.

**IF YOU DO NOT KNOW WHERE MOMENTUM WENT, STOP.**

He added another beneath it.

**IF YOU DO NOT KNOW WHO OWNS THE NEXT DECISION, STOP.**

Same lesson.

Different system.

He saved the note.

Then the vehicle turned toward home.

# Chapter 10 — Rescue Geometry

The disaster began at 08:00 with a text message.

**DISTRICT 6 EXERCISE ACTIVE. REPORT TO YOUR ASSIGNED LOCAL COMMAND.**

Kade read it in the Kisiwa dining hall.

Then the lights went out.

Half the room shouted.

The other half kept eating.

Emergency strips came on along the floor.

Eli looked delighted.

“This is excellent.”

Sera was already standing.

“No.”

“It is objectively well staged.”

“Move.”

The wall display switched to battery mode.

**SIMULATION CONDITIONS**

- Regional power loss
- Water-main break
- Partial transit interruption
- Two structural incidents
- Clinic backup failure
- Communications degradation
- Unknown secondary faults

**NO VISITOR MAY ASSUME INCIDENT COMMAND.**

Kade looked at the last line.

“That feels targeted.”

Imani Mwangi appeared in the doorway wearing response orange and a helmet.

“It is.”

Sera grabbed her pack.

Kade looked at his own campus band.

His observer restriction had changed overnight.

**EXERCISE OPERATOR — STRUCTURAL SUPPORT — LOCAL COMMAND REQUIRED**

Not leader.

Not strategic asset.

Structural support.

He liked that more than expected.

Mara’s band assigned communications and field analysis.

Julian: transport access and heavy-load support.

Eli: temporary power and systems repair.

Aya: distributed water-control team.

Sera’s:

**RESPONSE INTEGRATION — MOBILE**

No local command label.

Kade frowned.

“What does mobile mean?”

Imani looked at Sera.

“She goes where the incident commander needs an operator who can understand more than one problem.”

Sera clipped her helmet.

“Who is command?”

“Fatima Hassan. Nairobi Fire and Rescue liaison. She is waiting outside.”

Kade followed them.

The dining hall doors opened onto controlled chaos.

Kisiwa students moved toward utility carts and response vehicles.

Faculty stood back unless safety required intervention.

A city emergency truck idled at the curb.

A woman in her forties stood beside it with a tablet and a yellow command vest.

“Visitors,” she said. “You know this is an exercise?”

Kade nodded.

“Yes.”

“Good. Your bodies may forget. That is part of the exercise.”

Sera liked her immediately.

Kade could tell.

Fatima pointed at the map.

“District Six training site. Three blocks built to municipal standard, instrumented for failures. Simulated residents are staff, students, and volunteers. All injuries are cards unless safety staff call real-world stop.”

She looked at Kade.

“If a building falls wrong, you may stop being fictional.”

“Comforting.”

“That is why I said it.”

She assigned teams.

Kade went with Samwel Rukundo and a municipal structural engineer named Grace Wanjiru.

Mara joined a communications crew.

Julian went with transit and heavy rescue.

Eli disappeared with Asha toward a mobile microgrid trailer.

Aya joined David Okello’s water-control unit.

Sera stayed beside Fatima.

Kade looked at her.

“You get the truck.”

Sera looked at him.

“You get a broken building.”

“Good point.”

The siren sounded.

They moved.

---

District Six looked real because most of it was.

Three blocks of streets, shops, apartment shells, a clinic, transit platform, utility corridors, and public spaces built for training without being simplified into arena geometry.

Smoke machines were already running.

A hydrant sprayed water across one intersection.

The traffic lights were dark.

People stood on sidewalks holding colored role cards.

Kade’s team reached the first structural incident in four minutes.

A two-story mixed-use building had lost part of its front support after a simulated vehicle impact.

The upper floor sagged toward the street.

Three casualty markers inside.

Samwel stopped Kade before he crossed the perimeter.

“Who knows the building?”

Kade looked around.

A volunteer wearing a property-manager vest stood beside the cordon.

Right.

Kade approached.

“Any modifications not on plan?”

The manager nodded immediately.

“Rear storage wall removed last year. Temporary beam installed. Inspection scheduled next month.”

Grace swore under her breath.

Samwel looked at Kade.

“Your map?”

“Wrong.”

“Good start.”

They updated the structural model.

Kade felt the load paths through the building.

No cold Boundary line.

No need.

Ordinary Resonance perception was enough to see stress moving through columns and beams.

The obvious solution was to stabilize the front corner.

Kade could do it.

Probably.

Grace said, “Do not.”

Kade looked at her.

“The corner is failing.”

“Yes. If you hold it, rescue goes through front.”

“That seems good.”

“The temporary rear beam is carrying more load than the plan shows. Holding front transfers load back into it.”

Kade saw it.

He had been looking at the visible failure.

Again.

Samwel pointed to a side access.

“We do not need the building fixed. We need a safe route for eight minutes.”

Different problem.

Kade changed his model.

They used two mechanical shores and a limited field redirection that moved less load into the temporary rear beam, not more.

Grace approved.

Samwel entered with the rescue crew.

Kade held his assigned boundary.

No more.

Three simulated casualties came out.

Time: seven minutes twelve seconds.

Kade released.

The building settled farther under controlled conditions.

No applause.

The response team moved on.

Kade looked at the sagging corner.

He had wanted to save the building.

The mission had wanted eight minutes.

Useful distinction.

---

Sera’s first problem was not a building.

It was a missing bus.

Kade learned this later from the incident log.

At 08:17, Fatima’s command board showed a simulated school evacuation stalled because one transit route had been cut by the water-main break.

The obvious answer was to send another bus.

There was no spare bus.

Sera asked three questions.

Where were the students now?

Which routes still operated?

Which destination actually required evacuation first?

The school itself remained safe for forty minutes.

The clinic nearby had twelve patients who would lose cooling support in eighteen.

Fatima redirected the available vehicle to the clinic.

Sera called the school and told them to shelter in place.

No power used.

No field.

No heroics.

One resource stopped being assigned to the loudest problem.

Kade did not see any of that because at 08:19 a simulated gas-line fault closed his street and his team had to evacuate the structural site.

He found out later.

That mattered.

Sera was doing important things outside his field of view.

---

At 08:26, Mara lost communications.

Not all communications.

That would have been easier.

The district mesh split into two functioning islands that could each communicate internally but not with each other.

Mara’s crew occupied the eastern island.

Fatima’s command was west.

The exercise system disabled ordinary network bridging.

Mara looked at the Kisiwa communications lead, a third-year named Lydia Atieno.

“Line of sight?”

“Blocked by the training tower.”

“Physical runner?”

“Seven minutes round trip.”

“Too slow.”

Lydia pointed at a maintenance repeater on the transit platform.

“Battery only. Wrong protocol.”

Mara smiled.

Kade would later describe that as a bad sign.

Mara and Lydia did not invent new physics.

They reconfigured the repeater to carry tiny status bursts instead of full traffic.

No voice.

No video.

Just incident IDs, timestamps, resource requests, and acknowledgements.

Ugly.

Enough.

The two command islands regained a shared minimum state.

Mara wrote on the debrief later:

**When bandwidth collapses, preserve agreement before detail.**

Kade stole the sentence immediately.

---

At 08:31, Eli’s microgrid caught fire.

Not simulated fire.

A real capacitor vented inside the mobile power trailer.

The safety monitor shouted, “REAL STOP!”

Everything near the trailer froze.

Asha killed the circuit mechanically.

Eli pulled back.

Smoke vented through the roof panel.

No flames spread.

No one was hurt.

For ninety seconds the exercise disappeared.

Real safety took priority.

The trailer was isolated.

Faculty inspected it.

Eli stood beside Asha staring at the dead power system.

“Did I cause that?”

Asha checked the log.

“No. Old capacitor.”

“You sure?”

“Yes.”

He looked at the trailer anyway.

Lab Six existed in him too.

Asha did not tell him not to worry.

She handed him the failed component.

“Find me another route.”

Eli breathed.

Then did.

The clinic still needed temporary power.

The advanced trailer was unavailable.

They used two smaller battery units, one campus maintenance inverter, and a load-shedding plan that powered cooling first, lights second, everything else later.

The solution delivered less power than the trailer.

It was also operating eight minutes later.

Eli looked at the improvised system.

“Prototype Four would smooth this.”

Asha said, “Is Prototype Four here?”

“No.”

“Then it is not the solution.”

Kisiwa continued its campaign against hypothetical equipment.

---

At 08:39 the exercise accelerated.

Fatima’s board gained four new incidents at once.

Flooded underpass.

Apartment evacuation.

Clinic cooling fault.

Transit-platform crowding.

And a red marker:

**STRUCTURAL ACCESS — MULTIPLE PERSONS UNACCOUNTED — BLOCK C**

Sera read it.

Fatima did not assign her immediately.

That surprised one of the student observers.

“Vale is structural capable.”

Fatima looked at him.

“So are twelve other people.”

Then she pointed at Sera.

“Tell me what I am missing.”

Sera looked at the board.

Kade’s team was already moving toward Block C.

Julian’s heavy-rescue crew was three minutes away.

Water pressure had dropped across two blocks.

Clinic cooling was on backup.

The crowding at the transit platform was increasing because people from the apartment evacuation had been routed there.

Sera said, “The platform.”

The student observer frowned.

“People are missing in Block C.”

“Yes. And two rescue teams are already going there.”

She pointed at transit.

“If the platform crowds past safe density, ambulances lose the east route and the apartment evacuation backs into the flood detour.”

Fatima nodded.

“What do you need?”

“Authority to redirect evacuees to the school courtyard and one communications link to tell transit.”

“Take Lydia.”

Sera moved.

Not toward Kade.

Away from him.

The exercise got better because of it.

---

Block C was designed to tempt Kade.

He knew this immediately.

A four-story apartment training structure had suffered a staged partial stair collapse. Two simulated residents were trapped above the break. One was mobility-limited. The elevator was disabled.

Kade could see a Boundary solution.

Not full architecture.

A local redefinition of where the damaged stair transferred load could create a stable temporary route.

Fast.

Elegant.

Rare.

Samwel looked at the same stair.

“No.”

Kade sighed.

“I did not say anything.”

“You did the face.”

“Everyone has become intolerable.”

Grace studied the structure.

“Your route works.”

Kade looked at Samwel.

“Ha.”

Grace continued.

“For as long as Mercer holds it.”

Kade stopped smiling.

Samwel pointed at the mobility-limited casualty marker.

“How long to move that person?”

The rescue lead answered, “Minimum nine minutes if the chair path clears.”

Kade could hold nine minutes.

Probably.

Maybe.

His head already hurt from the first structural site.

Not badly.

Enough to matter.

Samwel asked, “And if another incident needs him at minute four?”

There it was again.

Rented miracle.

Grace looked toward the exterior service balcony.

“Can we bridge from the adjacent stair tower?”

Samwel measured.

“Six meters.”

Julian’s heavy-rescue crew arrived.

He looked at the gap.

“Portable span?”

Grace nodded.

“Too heavy for the current crew.”

Julian smiled.

Finally.

Something Rank One was useful for that nobody needed to call Rank One.

He and two Kisiwa students moved the modular bridge sections into position using ordinary Amplification and mechanical rigging.

Kade stabilized the balcony connection for ninety seconds while crews locked the span physically.

Then he released.

The bridge remained.

Rescuers crossed.

The mobility chair crossed.

The trapped residents came down.

Kade did not have to stay.

He looked at the installed bridge.

Samwel said, “Better than magic?”

“No.”

“More repairable?”

“Yes.”

“I will accept that.”

Kade’s wrist buzzed.

New assignment.

Clinic.

He could leave because the solution did not require him anymore.

That felt strangely like success.

---

At the transit platform, Sera’s problem was people.

Not panicked people.

Irritated people.

Better.

Harder in a different way.

The simulation volunteers had been instructed to behave like evacuees with incomplete information.

Some wanted to board the next bus.

Some refused to leave the platform because family members were meeting them there.

One role-player had a child.

Another had a mobility aid.

The route display was wrong because the communications fault had delayed an update.

Sera reached the platform with Lydia Atieno and did not climb onto anything.

She found the transit supervisor.

“What do you need from us?”

The supervisor pointed at the crowd.

“Accurate destination information and a place to send people that does not block the ambulance lane.”

“We have the school courtyard.”

“Who authorized?”

“Incident command.”

“Then I need the display changed.”

Lydia moved to the control panel.

Sera found two Kisiwa students already working the crowd.

She did not replace them.

She asked where the bottleneck was.

One pointed to a family group refusing to move without confirmation of the next route.

Sera took a printed board and wrote the temporary destinations by hand while Lydia fought the delayed digital display.

A woman using a mobility aid read the board and shook her head.

“School courtyard north gate has stairs.”

Sera stopped.

Her command map showed the courtyard as accessible.

“Are you sure?”

“I use that gate every week.”

Sera looked at the transit supervisor.

He nodded.

“North gate is accessible from the street only if the side ramp is open. Exercise scenario closed it for construction.”

Sera erased the route.

No defense.

No explanation that command data had been wrong.

“What is the accessible entrance?”

“East service gate. Longer walk. Flat route.”

Sera rewrote the board.

**SCHOOL COURTYARD — EAST SERVICE GATE**

The woman nodded.

“Better.”

Sera said, “Thank you.”

Then she asked the Kisiwa students which other groups needed the same correction.

The local information propagated faster than the command map update.

People moved.

Not because Sera commanded them.

Because the information they needed finally existed where they could see it—and because she changed it when someone who knew the route told her it was wrong.

The crowd density dropped.

The ambulance lane reopened.

At 08:51, an emergency vehicle passed without delay.

Fatima’s board turned the transit marker from red to amber.

Sera did not know.

She was still helping an older role-player find the correct shelter route.

---

The clinic became the convergence point.

At 09:02, backup cooling fell below target.

Eli’s improvised power system could sustain critical refrigeration but not the entire clinic load.

The water-main break reduced cooling options.

The communications bridge carried only low-bandwidth status packets.

The east ambulance route had just reopened.

Kade arrived with Samwel.

Mara was already there with Lydia.

Aya and David came in from water control.

Julian arrived two minutes later.

Sera came last.

Not because she was late.

Because she had stayed at transit until local staff told her they no longer needed her.

Fatima stood at the clinic entrance.

“Problem.”

Eli answered first.

“Power shortfall.”

David said, “Water pressure unstable.”

Mara said, “Communications limited.”

Julian said, “Ambulance route east only.”

Kade saw the system.

All of it.

For one dangerous second he felt good.

This was his kind of problem.

Multiple systems.

Shared constraints.

One geometry.

Sera said, “What does the clinic need?”

Kade stopped.

Right.

They all looked at the clinic operations coordinator from yesterday’s district exercise.

She held up her tablet.

“Medication refrigeration. Two procedure rooms. Oxygen concentrators. One lift if we need upper-floor transfer. Everything else can go dark.”

Eli recalculated.

“We can do that.”

David asked, “Water?”

“Procedure rooms only,” the coordinator answered. “Sanitation reserve is enough for the exercise window.”

Mara asked, “Communications?”

“Status every five minutes. Voice only for clinical emergency.”

Julian asked, “Transport?”

“Keep east route open. Do not send more patients here unless command approves.”

Kade watched the system simplify.

Not because he solved it.

Because the people who owned each service defined what actually had to survive.

Aya looked at him.

She had noticed too.

No center person.

A temporary shared priority state.

Visible.

Challengeable.

Owned by the mission, not the loudest operator.

Fatima looked at Sera.

“Anything?”

Sera studied the plan.

“Who tells the waiting room what is happening?”

Silence.

The clinic coordinator closed her eyes.

“Good catch.”

She assigned a staff member.

Kade smiled.

Sera saw.

“Do not.”

“I said nothing.”

“Your face did.”

International corruption had reached everyone.

They stabilized the clinic.

No Consensus.

No Standard Nine.

No single miracle.

At 09:17, the exercise ended.

The lights came back on across District Six.

Kade had never been so tired from doing so little spectacularly.

---

The debrief lasted longer than the disaster.

Kisiwa considered this normal.

Kade considered it hostile.

Every team had to identify one decision that improved the system and one decision that only looked useful from inside their own role.

Kade’s first answer was the modular bridge.

His second was wanting to hold the stair with Boundary work.

Eli’s first was load shedding.

His second was designing around equipment that was not available.

Mara’s first was minimum-state communications.

Her second was trying to restore too much bandwidth before agreeing on what messages mattered.

Julian’s first was moving the portable span.

His second was initially treating heavy-load capability as the scarce resource when access planning was actually scarce.

Aya’s first was allowing water-control decisions to publish downstream consequences to other teams.

Her second was assuming a complete relation map mattered more than local maintenance knowledge.

Then Sera’s turn.

Fatima looked at her.

“Useful decision?”

Sera said, “Not going to Block C.”

Kade felt that.

Fatima nodded.

“Why?”

“Because it already had structural operators. Transit did not have enough coordination, and failure there would have blocked ambulances and evacuation.”

“Decision that looked useful from your role?”

Sera thought.

“Staying at transit too long.”

Imani looked at her.

Sera continued.

“Once the supervisor had accurate routes and crowd flow dropped, I stayed because I was already there. I should have checked back with command two minutes earlier.”

Fatima nodded toward the incident log.

“And the courtyard route?”

Sera exhaled.

“Command data said accessible. A resident knew the exercise closure made the north gate inaccessible. I published the wrong route first.”

“What fixed it?”

“She told me.”

Fatima smiled.

“Good.”

Kade frowned.

“You were still useful.”

Sera looked at him.

“That is not the same as being right.”

There it was.

Kade wrote it down.

Sera saw.

“Seriously?”

“Yes.”

“That is disturbing.”

“Growth.”

Fatima closed the debrief.

Then she looked at Sera.

“Vale. Stay.”

Everyone else stood.

Kade did not move fast enough.

Sera looked at him.

“Go.”

He went.

Outside, Imani caught up with him.

“She is good.”

Kade smiled.

“I know.”

Imani looked at him sideways.

He corrected himself.

“She knows.”

Better.

---

Sera came back to the residence an hour later carrying a Kisiwa Response packet.

Kade was on the steps with Mara.

He looked at the packet.

Sera saw.

“No.”

“I did not ask.”

“You were going to.”

“Everyone has become unbearable.”

She sat one step below them.

Mara asked, “What is it?”

Sera looked at the cover.

“Field exchange invitation.”

Kade’s chest tightened before he could stop it.

“For how long?”

“Six weeks next year. Maybe.”

“That’s good.”

Sera looked at him.

He meant it.

Mostly.

The mostly was his problem.

Not hers.

Mara asked, “Do you want it?”

Sera did not answer immediately.

Then:

“Yes.”

Simple.

Kade looked out across the campus.

Students were resetting District Six for another cohort.

A utility crew replaced a damaged training valve.

A response vehicle rolled toward the garage.

The city beyond the campus continued having real problems that did not know Kade Mercer existed.

Sera held the exchange packet in both hands.

Kade understood something unpleasant and useful.

A rescue system that only worked when the same person stayed in the same place was fragile.

Apparently friendships could be too.

He would have to learn how not to make that her problem.

# Chapter 29 — Handoff

Kade’s first interoperable Consensus cell failed because everyone trusted it.

That was new.

The test floor at Vahana’s lunar operations annex had been divided into three independent systems:

A simulated rescue corridor.

A damaged power-transfer lattice.

A navigation relay with intermittent timing error.

Three tasks.

Three teams.

No team could finish the full scenario alone.

That was the point.

Cell One contained Kade, Mara, and Neema.

Cell Two contained Aya, Julian, and Arjun.

Cell Three contained Jonas, Eli, and Sera.

The rule was simple enough to become dangerous:

Each cell could form a shared state for its local task.

Then responsibility had to transfer to the next cell without the first network remaining active as a supervisory layer.

No permanent global Consensus.

No Kade hovering above the process.

No Aya doing it either.

The chapter title had become a threat.

Kade stood at Cell One’s platform.

Mara looked at him.

“You are smiling.”

“I like problems with obvious failure modes.”

Neema shook her head.

“You will regret saying that.”

She was right in forty-three seconds.

---

Cell One’s rescue corridor began with a collapsed structural gate blocking access to a simulated clinic.

Kade held the shared physical boundary.

Mara owned geometry.

Neema owned access priority.

The network formed quickly.

Too quickly.

They had become good at this version.

Mara exposed the safe load path.

Neema selected the route that preserved wheelchair access and emergency egress.

Kade moved existing structural load through the frame without becoming the only place where the geometry lived.

The gate opened.

Task complete.

Now handoff.

Cell Two needed the corridor state because its power-transfer route crossed the same structure.

Kade released Consensus.

Mara published geometry.

Neema published access restrictions.

Arjun acknowledged.

Aya acknowledged.

Julian acknowledged.

Cell Two formed.

For one second, it worked perfectly.

Then Julian asked, “Is east support still safe above sixty percent?”

Mara answered from outside the active cell.

“Yes.”

The test alarm sounded.

**HANDOFF FAILURE — OUTGOING CELL RE-ENTERED DECISION LOOP**

Kade stared.

“We answered a question.”

Dr. Iyer said from observation, “Exactly.”

Mara looked offended.

“The state was incomplete.”

“Then the handoff was incomplete.”

Kade felt the trap.

They had released field control.

They had not released responsibility.

The next team still depended on the old team to interpret its own state.

Rented miracle again.

Different form.

Iyer reset the floor.

“Again.”

---

The second attempt failed more elegantly.

Cell One documented every assumption.

Every threshold.

Every uncertainty.

Every local consequence.

Their handoff packet became beautiful.

Cell Two took four minutes to read it.

The scenario timed out.

Eli laughed from Cell Three.

“Congratulations. You have invented bureaucracy.”

Kade looked toward him.

“You are next.”

“Threat received.”

Iyer displayed the packet on the wall.

Nine pages of state.

No missing information.

No usable priority.

Kade thought of Vahana’s first arrivals exercise.

A handoff was not the biography of a system.

It was what the next operator needed to own first.

Again.

The international circuit had become a machine for making old lessons harder.

Good.

Annoying.

They rebuilt.

This time the packet contained five things:

**CURRENT STATE**

**WHAT CHANGED**

**WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAIN**

**FIRST OWNERSHIP DECISION**

**DO NOT ASSUME**

Kade looked at the last field.

“Important.”

Mara said, “Especially for you.”

“Cruel.”

“Accurate.”

---

Third attempt.

Cell One formed.

Opened rescue corridor.

Released.

Handoff:

**CURRENT: east support stable to 63% modeled load.**

**CHANGED: west brace removed to preserve accessible route.**

**UNCERTAIN: thermal response above 58%.**

**FIRST DECISION: choose power path before adding load.**

**DO NOT ASSUME: old west route remains available.**

Cell Two read it in eleven seconds.

Aya formed shared state with Julian and Arjun.

Kade stood outside.

Actually outside.

Hands down.

No field.

No commentary.

This was harder than expected.

Cell Two routed power.

Julian requested temporary structural priority.

Aya published relationship changes.

Arjun controlled transfer sequence.

They chose an east path at fifty-six percent load.

Inside Cell One’s old model, Kade thought he saw a better route.

He said nothing.

Cell Two succeeded anyway.

Not his route.

Different route.

Also good.

Kade felt something loosen.

Then Cell Two handed to Cell Three.

Their packet was shorter.

Too short.

Cell Three formed around Jonas, Eli, and Sera.

Eli immediately said, “What is the source timing uncertainty?”

Arjun’s hand moved toward his comm.

Stopped.

Handoff rule.

No re-entry.

Cell Three had to work with what it had.

Jonas said, “We know uncertainty exists. Not magnitude.”

Sera asked, “Does mission depend on exact timing?”

Eli looked at the relay model.

“No. We can choose a slower safe acquisition window.”

They did.

Less efficient.

Successful.

The missing detail became cost, not catastrophe.

That was the point.

---

The fourth test introduced participant withdrawal during handoff.

Of course.

Neema withdrew from Cell One after the rescue gate opened.

Her access-priority state had to be reconstructed before handoff.

Mara held secondary.

Approximate.

They published the uncertainty instead of pretending precision.

Cell Two inherited degraded mission state.

Julian said, “We need local access authority before choosing power path.”

A role-player representing the clinic supervisor answered.

Local knowledge.

The next cell repaired what the previous cell could not know.

Kade liked that.

Then Aya withdrew from Cell Two.

The network ended.

Not because she was mandatory.

Because the active cell had only two participants left and their specific shared-state implementation lost relational coherence.

They did not force continuation.

Arjun and Julian completed the remaining physical work conventionally.

Handoff still occurred.

Consensus was not mandatory just because it had started.

Important.

Cell Three took over.

Sera looked at the mission board.

“Good.”

Kade called from outside, “You like failure now?”

She looked at him.

“I like systems that can keep working after the fancy part stops.”

Kade smiled.

Civilian infrastructure had infected everyone.

---

By lunch they had a protocol.

Not a grand theory.

A protocol.

Kade liked that better.

They called it **TRANSFERABLE SHARED-STATE CELL ARCHITECTURE** until Eli refused to continue eating beside the phrase.

“Terrible.”

Aya said, “Accurate.”

“Still terrible.”

Sera said, “Call it handoff.”

Everyone looked at her.

She shrugged.

“That is what it is.”

Handoff Protocol.

Fine.

The protocol required:

- local scope declared before Consensus forms;
- participant consent scoped to that task;
- explicit exit condition;
- no outgoing cell retains hidden supervisory authority;
- next cell receives current state, changes, uncertainties, first ownership decision, and prohibited assumptions;
- outgoing participants may answer historical questions after handoff only if the incoming cell pauses and explicitly requests consultation;
- shared state is optional; conventional continuation must remain possible where feasible.

Julian read the fourth line twice.

“No hidden supervisory authority.”

Kade looked at him.

“Your father would hate it.”

Julian smiled faintly.

“Yes.”

Then he looked at Kade.

“So would the old version of you.”

Kade started to object.

Stopped.

Fair.

---

The afternoon test used four cells.

No Kade in two of them.

That mattered.

Cell One: Kade, Mara, Neema.

Cell Two: Aya, Julian, Arjun.

Cell Three: Jonas, Eli, Sera.

Cell Four: Hana, Nessa, Priya.

The final cell had no Zero candidate.

No Kade.

No Aya.

It was not asked to form full Consensus.

It was asked to inherit the system and finish safely.

Kade loved the design before understanding why.

The sequence began.

Rescue corridor.

Power transfer.

Relay timing.

Final stabilization.

Cell One succeeded.

Released.

Cell Two succeeded.

Released.

Cell Three encountered false telemetry.

Jonas challenged it.

Eli verified physically.

Sera changed mission priority.

They released.

Cell Four inherited a messy system with degraded timing and one load outside preferred margin.

Priya looked at the handoff packet.

“We do not need Consensus for this.”

Nessa agreed.

Hana agreed.

They completed conventionally.

The board displayed:

**MISSION COMPLETE**

**CONSENSUS CELLS USED: 3**

**CONVENTIONAL CELL USED: 1**

**HANDOFF FAILURES: 0**

**OUTGOING RE-ENTRY: 0**

Kade stared at the result.

The system had finished after Consensus stopped.

Not failure.

Success.

That was the piece he had been missing since Book One.

Consensus did not need to become the operating system for every problem.

It could be one mode among several.

A temporary way of sharing state when the task justified the cost.

Then it could end.

The work could continue.

No permanent center.

No permanent network.

No permanent Kade.

He expected the thought to hurt.

It did not.

Not much.

Progress.

---

That evening Dr. Zhou called from the Lunar Far-Side Array.

The first controlled test of the deep-space relay corridor had succeeded.

Local relays aligned.

No endpoint transmission yet.

Leakage remained below detection models.

The next test would extend farther.

Kade looked at the Handoff Protocol on the wall.

Then at the relay diagram.

One task passed to another.

One system becoming visible only long enough to do work.

“What happens if we use cells for the relay?” he asked.

Eli sat up.

Zhou’s expression changed.

“Explain.”

Kade did.

Not one giant Consensus spanning every relay operator.

Task-specific cells.

Array calibration.

Lunar routing.

Orbital relay.

Deep-space transmitter.

Each with its own authority and exit.

Each handing state forward.

If one cell failed, the others did not have to become one catastrophic dependency.

Zhou was quiet.

Then:

“That may be testable.”

Kade smiled.

Good.

Not destiny.

Engineering.

Tomorrow the Handoff Protocol would leave the training floor.

That made it real enough to be afraid of.

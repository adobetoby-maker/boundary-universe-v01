# Chapter 27 — Lunar Far Side

The Moon ruined Kade’s sense of scale before breakfast.

He had expected gray.

He had not expected distance to become visible in every movement.

The Lunar Far-Side Array sat inside a pressure-linked cluster of low structures buried partly under regolith. No towers. No glass. Nothing built to impress anyone who might fall upward if distracted.

Eli loved it immediately.

“This is the best place humans have ever made.”

Sera looked at the ceiling height.

“It has no windows.”

“Exactly.”

Julian rotated one shoulder under lunar gravity.

“You are happy because the building is entirely infrastructure.”

“Yes.”

Mara said, “He has found his religion.”

Kade looked at Eli.

“Terrible theology.”

A woman in a white-gray operations suit met them at the transit lock.

“Dr. Lian Zhou. Signal systems.”

Her badge listed no rank or Standard.

Only:

**ARRAY OPERATIONS — FLIGHT / SIGNAL AUTHORITY**

Vahana influence everywhere.

Zhou looked at Kade’s group.

“Before we begin: nothing you do here changes array pointing without operator approval.”

Kade raised both hands.

“I have learned.”

Mara coughed.

Zhou ignored them.

“The returned coordinates and countdown have been treated publicly as one message. We do not know that they are one function.”

That distinction changed the problem.

---

The countdown was not embedded in the returned signal the way everyone assumed.

The signal contained a timestamp family.

The countdown displayed at Asterion had been created by human software interpreting the sequence as a decrementing time-to-event.

Kade stared at the reconstruction.

“So we made the clock?”

Zhou nodded.

“From alien—or nonhuman—timing data. Probably.”

“Probably is doing work.”

“Always.”

The raw return consisted of repeated timing packets, coordinates, parity structures, and one recurring interval sequence that decremented predictably against atomic time.

Humans turned that into:

**00:364:17:42:08**

Then everyone psychologically converted it into *something happens when this reaches zero.*

Eli said, “What else does a decrementing time sequence mean?”

Zhou changed displays.

“Window opens. Window closes. Transmission arrives. Receiver activates. Orbital geometry aligns. Relay becomes visible. Rendezvous opportunity. Encryption epoch changes.”

Kade looked at the list.

“Or attack.”

“Or attack.”

Good.

Not removed.

Just one hypothesis among several.

Aya, joining remotely from Kiyomizu due to medical restriction from her recent shared-state event, appeared on the side display.

“Does the sequence track Earth time?”

“No,” Zhou said. “That is the useful part.”

She overlaid the countdown against barycentric corrections.

The interval drifted slightly unless corrected for Earth’s changing position relative to the returned coordinates.

Mara leaned forward.

“It is referenced to geometry.”

“Exactly.”

Kade felt the clock become physical.

Not prophecy.

Position.

---

The coordinates pointed beyond the solar system.

Everybody knew that.

What they had not known publicly was that the line of sight passed through a region where three old deep-space probes, two commercial laser relays, and one dormant scientific beacon could form a temporary communication chain if retasked.

Eli stared at the map.

“A relay corridor.”

Zhou nodded.

“Not a good one.”

“Still.”

“Still.”

Mara asked, “When does the geometry become optimal?”

Zhou put the countdown beside the projection.

Zero.

The room went silent.

Kade looked at her.

“That is too clean.”

“Yes.”

“Independent derivation?”

“Three teams. Different ephemeris software. Same window within eleven minutes.”

Mara exhaled.

There.

Rule Six fairness arriving as mathematics.

The countdown was not proven to be an attack timer.

It was strongly correlated with a communication geometry window.

Maybe rendezvous.

Maybe visibility.

Maybe trap.

But now the timer had a physical job.

Kade preferred dangerous things with jobs.

---

The far-side array itself became the next lesson.

Zhou took them into the signal control room.

No dramatic dish visible outside.

Most of the receiving elements were spread across kilometers of regolith and linked by fiber and buried power.

The room displayed their phase relationships.

Kade watched hundreds of elements combine into one instrument.

“Consensus,” Eli whispered.

Kade looked at him.

“No.”

Eli smiled.

“Growth.”

But the analogy was useful.

Each element remained local.

Timing made them one aperture for one task.

No permanent center in the antennas themselves.

The correlator concentrated state temporarily.

Then the processed result moved elsewhere.

Kade felt the architecture rhyme again.

Not same physics.

Same systems problem.

Zhou assigned them a calibration exercise.

Simulated only.

Six array sectors.

Each participant controlled phase correction for one sector.

Goal: maximize coherent signal without hiding local uncertainty.

Kade wanted global phase.

Of course.

He did not get it.

Mara did.

He looked at Zhou.

“She is enjoying this.”

Zhou said, “She scored higher on relevant calibration.”

Mara did not look smug.

That made it worse.

Kade got Sector Four, the noisiest.

Eli got power timing.

Julian got fault-priority rules.

Sera got operator safety/evac constraints, which she called “being assigned the humans again.”

The exercise began.

Kade’s sector produced intermittent phase noise.

His first instinct was to smooth it.

Then he remembered Helix.

Uncertainty itself was state.

“Sector Four correction confidence low,” he said.

Mara kept the uncertainty visible.

The combined signal got worse.

Then better because the model stopped trusting a clean lie.

Zhou nodded once.

Kade felt absurdly proud.

---

The real problem arrived during lunch.

A small solar particle event hit earlier than forecast.

Not dangerous to the buried array.

Dangerous enough to interrupt a maintenance crew on the surface and force a shelter return.

Sera’s entire body changed when the alert sounded.

Not fear.

Work.

Zhou handed response authority to the lunar safety officer.

Not herself.

The safety officer rerouted two maintenance crawlers.

One route crossed a communications trench scheduled for active calibration.

Eli said, “We need to pause sector tests.”

A signal scientist objected.

“Window is seventeen minutes.”

Sera looked at him.

“People first.”

The scientist said, “They have shielding.”

“Enough?”

No one answered quickly.

Sera turned to the safety officer.

“Your call.”

The officer canceled the calibration.

Nobody died.

No equipment failed.

They lost twelve minutes of rare array time.

The scientist was angry.

Also correct that the scientific loss mattered.

Kade liked the Moon less after that.

Which meant it had become real.

---

That evening the team received the full countdown analysis.

Confidence levels attached.

Not headlines.

**82% — zero correlates with maximum relay-corridor viability**

**61% — returned coordinates specify endpoint or relay target**

**34% — sequence may indicate visibility/synchronization window**

**UNRESOLVED — whether source expects human transmission**

**UNRESOLVED — whether opening corridor increases detectability to third parties**

The last line came from Aaron.

Kade stared at it.

The Refuge Node had been hiding from something capable of hearing it.

If the countdown opened a communication window, using it might make humanity louder.

Mara said, “That is a later problem.”

Kade looked at her.

“Unfortunately.”

Sera said, “Everybody is tired.”

They were.

Kade returned to the data.

One question mattered now:

Could humanity test the relay corridor without committing to a full transmission?

Zhou’s answer was careful.

“Yes. We can illuminate local relays, verify path geometry, and stop short of the endpoint.”

Eli leaned forward.

“When?”

“Before zero. Small windows improve over the next months.”

Kade looked at the countdown.

For the first time, the next move was not *wait and see what happens.*

They could practice.

Test handoffs.

Measure leakage.

Learn how visible the system became as it formed.

The countdown had turned from doom into an engineering deadline.

That did not make it safe.

It made it something humans could finally work on.

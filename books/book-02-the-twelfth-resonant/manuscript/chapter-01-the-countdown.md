# Chapter 1 — The Countdown

The countdown had been on every screen at Asterion for ninety-three days, which was long enough for students to start using it to complain about breakfast.

At 07:14:22, Kade Mercer stood in Meridian Commons with a bowl of cereal going soft in front of him while the west wall display showed:

**00:271:09:31:46**

“Two hundred seventy-one days,” Eli Navarro said. “Plenty of time to improve the eggs.”

Sera Vale looked at the tray in front of him.

“You are eating pancakes.”

“I am speaking for the community.”

“The community asked you to stop speaking before seven thirty.”

“Authoritarian.”

Across the room, somebody had taped a paper sign beneath the countdown.

**DAYS UNTIL WHATEVER THIS IS**

Underneath, in different handwriting:

**PLEASE DO NOT SCHEDULE FINALS AFTER**

Kade liked that one.

Mostly because the joke had appeared before administration replaced the display lockouts for the third time.

Asterion had discovered an institutional fact nobody had anticipated when a message from outside the solar system appeared on every campus screen.

Teenagers still had homework.

The countdown had not canceled morning conditioning.

It had not repaired Lab Six.

It had not made the dining hall coffee better.

It had not convinced Professor Amadi that Kade’s notation had become acceptable merely because several governments now referred to him as a strategic coordination asset.

Mostly it had made everything normal louder.

Kade looked from the numbers to the ranking strip beneath them.

His name still sat where it had landed at semester reset.

**MERCER, KADE — #37 — STANDARD IX**

He hated the second line more than the first.

Thirty-seven was a ranking.

Rankings moved.

Rankings could be wrong in understandable ways.

Standard IX had become a noun people used when they wanted him to stop being a person.

Public Nine.

The Ninth Standard.

The boy who linked six minds.

The boy who contacted something beyond the solar system.

That last description was especially irritating because Kade had spent much of the relevant event bleeding from the nose and trying not to fall over.

Mara Vey sat across from him reading a technical brief while eating toast with the efficiency of someone who considered breakfast a solved problem.

Kade looked at the title floating above her tablet.

**INTERNATIONAL WORKING GROUP ON COOPERATIVE RESONANCE SAFETY — REVISION 14**

“You’re reading policy before eight.”

“I’m reading what people intend to do to us before they do it.”

“That is less comforting.”

“It wasn’t meant to be.”

She scrolled.

Kade waited.

Mara looked up.

“What?”

“You made the face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you found something irritating enough to become interesting.”

Her mouth moved slightly.

“They changed ‘consenting operators’ to ‘authorized operators.’”

Kade stopped eating.

“Where?”

Mara turned the tablet.

He read the paragraph.

The change was small.

That was why it mattered.

*International demonstration of cooperative IX-class field behavior should be limited to authorized operators under recognized institutional supervision.*

Kade looked at her.

“Authorized by who?”

“Exactly.”

Eli leaned across the table.

“Technically, grammar is our enemy.”

Sera took Mara’s tablet, read the paragraph once, and handed it back.

“No.”

Kade looked at her.

“That your policy analysis?”

“Yes.”

“Detailed.”

“I’m training at eight.”

That mattered more than the sentence suggested.

Sera’s accelerated Disaster Response assessment had moved from a vague post-siege possibility to three mornings a week with Asterion’s emergency operations faculty and two afternoons at the harbor training complex.

She had started carrying a real responder pack instead of a student one.

Kade had made fun of the number of straps exactly once.

Then Sera made him carry it up nine flights of stairs.

He had revised his position.

Eli had changed too.

His side of Room 814 now contained fewer illegal drones and more parts that came with procurement labels.

Systems Lab had given him a fellowship bench.

His civilian stabilizer prototype had survived Sera’s first stress test and failed the second when she dropped it down a stairwell.

Eli maintained the second test had been emotionally motivated.

Sera called it field realism.

Julian Cross was not at breakfast.

Rank One had started appearing at sponsor events, government panels, and academy functions with the expression of someone being congratulated for circumstances he had not chosen.

Taren Holt sat at the far end of Meridian Commons under supervision.

Two months ago, Kade would have tracked his location without thinking.

Now he noticed only after he had already looked.

Progress, apparently, could be measured in the delay before suspicion.

Taren caught him looking.

He lifted his mug slightly.

Kade nodded once.

Not forgiveness.

Not nothing.

The countdown changed another second.

**00:271:09:31:45**

Mara closed the policy brief.

“You’re due in Containment Lab Two at eight fifteen.”

“I know.”

“You were going to be late.”

“I was considering being precisely on time from a different location.”

“You were eating cereal.”

“Cereal has delayed many important men.”

“You are not an important man.”

“Good.”

Mara stood.

Kade looked at her.

“You coming?”

“No.”

That surprised him enough to show.

Mara saw.

“Kade.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You expected me to come.”

He hated that she was right.

The lab session was a scheduled attempt to reproduce Consensus under controlled conditions.

Kade had assumed Mara would be there because she had been the first person to join him in Sublevel Nine.

Because she understood his field geometry better than anyone except possibly Aaron.

Because when something frightened him, Mara’s presence made the problem feel measurable.

None of those were the same thing as her being obligated to attend.

“What are you doing?” he asked.

“Advanced control practical.”

“Oh.”

“My own training.”

“Right.”

She watched him.

Kade looked back at the cereal.

The mistake was small.

That was why it mattered.

“Have fun,” he said.

“I will not.”

“That sounds correct.”

Mara’s expression softened.

“Your lab will be fine.”

“You don’t know that.”

“No.”

She picked up her tablet.

“That is why you’re testing it.”

Then she left.

Kade stared after her.

Eli said, “You did better than expected.”

“I am going to throw your pancake away.”

“Violence against breakfast is still violence.”

Kade stood.

At the far table, Taren was looking at him.

Not smiling.

Just looking.

Kade remembered the old version of Taren, who would have known his lab schedule before Kade did.

The current version did not ask where he was going.

That restraint felt more complicated than curiosity would have.

---

Containment Lab Two had been designed for the exact kind of experiment everyone claimed they did not know how to conduct.

That was Kade’s first problem with it.

His second was the observation gallery.

Dr. Park stood behind the glass with four neurologists, two field physicists, Director Sen, and three people Kade did not know wearing the sort of clothes governments used when they wanted to appear civilian.

His third problem was the chairs.

Six of them formed a circle around a low instrument platform.

Kade stopped inside the door.

“No.”

Park looked up from her tablet.

“That is not a medical symptom.”

“Why six?”

“Because the first stable event involved six active participants.”

“That is not a reason to recreate the furniture.”

“It is not about furniture.”

“It is becoming about furniture.”

Sen’s voice came through the speaker.

“Kade.”

He looked toward the glass.

She had gotten better at looking tired in public.

The months since the siege had not been kind to her political schedule.

Parliamentary inquiries.

International reviews.

Parents.

The parents had apparently been worse.

“Today is not a demonstration,” Sen said.

“Then why are there strangers watching?”

One of the civilians shifted.

Sen said, “Because telling governments they cannot watch makes them assume we are hiding a weapon.”

“Are we?”

“No.”

“Good. Wanted to establish tone.”

Park pointed toward the chairs.

“Sit.”

Only three other students entered.

Nessa Kim.

Anton Bell.

Priya Shah.

Kade frowned.

“No Mara. No Sera. No Julian. No Taren. No Eli.”

“Correct,” Park said.

“Why?”

“Because if Consensus only works with the exact people from Sublevel Nine, we need to know that.”

That was unfortunately reasonable.

Kade sat.

Nessa took the chair opposite him.

“I was told this was safe.”

Park said, “You were told the risk was controlled.”

Nessa looked at Kade.

“That is a worse sentence.”

“I’ve been saying that for months.”

Priya stretched her wrists.

“What do we do?”

Kade opened his mouth.

Then stopped.

Everyone looked at him.

That was the problem.

At Sublevel Nine, he had asked.

They had chosen.

Here, three people had been scheduled into a laboratory where the entire room already assumed Kade was the event.

“I don’t know,” he said.

One of the civilians in the gallery wrote something down.

Kade disliked him immediately.

Park activated the instrument platform.

A suspended frame rose between the chairs.

Four masses connected by tension cables.

Simple physical problem.

Hold the frame level under variable load.

No one needed Boundary architecture.

That was intentional.

Kade looked at the others.

“Before we start. Are you here because you want to be?”

Anton blinked.

Priya smiled slightly.

Nessa said, “I volunteered.”

Anton nodded. “Same.”

Priya said, “I was promised I could leave if you became annoying.”

“That seems broad.”

“Very.”

Kade breathed.

“Okay.”

He reached for the shared model.

Nothing happened.

He tried again.

Not force.

Not control.

The space where separate choices could become one physical answer.

Nessa’s Perception field lit first.

Anton stabilized his side.

Priya corrected an induced swing.

Kade felt all three effects.

Individually.

That was all.

No joining.

No shared operator.

No shift in the boundary.

Park watched his neurological trace.

“Again.”

They tried again.

Kade asked each person to state what they intended to do.

Nessa: perceive load changes.

Anton: hold structure.

Priya: redirect motion.

Kade: share the model.

Nothing.

Third attempt.

Kade felt something begin around Nessa.

Not a connection.

A possibility.

He reached toward it.

The possibility vanished.

Nessa flinched.

“Stop.”

Kade released immediately.

The lab went silent.

Park leaned toward the glass.

“Symptoms?”

Nessa shook her head.

“No. It just felt like someone was about to finish my sentence.”

Kade’s stomach tightened.

“I’m sorry.”

Nessa looked at him.

“You stopped.”

“That is a low standard.”

“It is still relevant.”

The civilian wrote something else.

Kade stood.

“I’m done.”

Sen said, “We have another forty minutes scheduled.”

“I don’t care.”

One of the government observers finally spoke.

“Mr. Mercer, the purpose of the session is to establish reproducibility.”

Kade looked up.

“Who are you?”

“Dr. Harlan Price. International Resonance Coordination Office.”

“Did you volunteer for this?”

Price frowned.

“I’m not participating.”

“Exactly.”

Sen closed her eyes briefly.

Price said, “No one is being compelled.”

Kade looked at the three students.

Then at the six chairs.

Then the observation gallery.

The entire room had been built around the assumption that his ability was a procedure waiting to be standardized.

Maybe that assumption was enough to break it.

“We’re done,” he said again.

Park checked the students.

Then nodded.

“Session terminated.”

Price started to object.

Sen cut his audio.

Kade liked her for almost four seconds.

---

The failure was public by lunch.

Not officially.

Asterion had improved somewhat at keeping actual medical telemetry private.

But four students had entered Containment Lab Two under guard and left ninety minutes early.

Teenagers could perform arithmetic.

The campus network produced theories.

**IX ONLY WORKS IN CRISIS**

**MERCER NEEDS ORIGINAL TEAM**

**STANDARD NINE = TRAUMA RESPONSE?**

**MAYBE HE JUST GOT LUCKY**

Kade liked the last one least.

Which told him something unpleasant about himself.

He put his tablet facedown.

Mara sat beside him on the sea wall.

Not across.

Beside.

They had developed that habit after the siege.

It remained unlabeled.

Kade preferred it that way.

For now.

“I failed,” he said.

“Yes.”

He looked at her.

“You could soften it.”

“You did not reproduce Consensus.”

“Still.”

Mara watched the water.

“Did you learn anything?”

“Nessa does not like people finishing her sentences.”

“Reasonable.”

“And the room was wrong.”

Mara looked at him.

“How?”

“Everyone was waiting for me.”

“That sounds familiar.”

He frowned.

She continued.

“You still think Consensus is something you do correctly enough that other people can join.”

“That is not what I think.”

Mara waited.

Kade hated when she used silence as evidence.

“Fine,” he said. “Maybe a little.”

“A little is sufficient.”

He looked toward the Spire.

“You would have helped.”

“Yes.”

The answer came immediately.

Kade turned back.

Mara’s expression was steady.

“If you asked.”

There it was.

Not *if you needed me.*

Not *if the lab scheduled me.*

If you asked.

And she could still say no.

Kade leaned back on his hands.

“I’m becoming very tired of the universe making the same point.”

“It may be compensating for your learning speed.”

“That was cruel.”

“Accurate.”

His tablet buzzed again.

Kade ignored it.

Mara glanced at the screen.

“Your mother.”

He checked.

Elena had sent a photograph from Northline.

Darius stood beside the vending machine holding three bags of pretzels.

Caption:

**MACHINE STILL RESPECTS HIM.**

Kade laughed.

The sound felt better than it had all morning.

He typed back:

**GOOD. SOME SYSTEMS REMAIN STABLE.**

Then another message arrived.

This one from Aaron.

**Heard the test didn’t work. That may be good news.**

Kade stared at it.

Mara read his face.

“Aaron?”

“Yes.”

“What did he say?”

Kade showed her.

Mara considered it.

“He may be right.”

“That is becoming an alarming family habit.”

Kade typed:

**EXPLAIN.**

Aaron replied three minutes later.

**If they can make it routine before they understand it, they will mistake repeatability for safety.**

Kade read the sentence twice.

Then:

**Also: you were never the only variable.**

That one irritated him more because it was exactly the thing Mara had just said.

He put the tablet away.

The countdown continued on a tower display across campus.

**00:271:04:06:11**

Five hours gone since breakfast.

Nothing from beyond the solar system had changed.

Everything on Earth apparently had.

---

Kade found Julian Cross outside Training Hall Three ten minutes before the conventional benchmark.

Julian wore a formal Asterion jacket over training clothes and looked offended by both.

“You have a panel?” Kade asked.

“Had.”

“Before lunch?”

“Tokyo was awake.”

Kade looked at the jacket.

“Did the jacket help?”

“Apparently it reassures governments.”

“That fabric exists?”

“Very expensive fabric.”

Julian pulled the jacket off and folded it over one arm.

For a second, Kade saw a public clip from the previous week: Julian Cross, Rank One, seated beneath a headline that read **CONVENTIONAL EXCELLENCE IN AN IX WORLD**.

Kade had sent him the screenshot with the message **YOU ARE A HISTORICAL CONTROL GROUP NOW**.

Julian had not replied for six hours.

Then: **I dislike you.**

Kade nodded toward the training hall.

“Priya is about to beat me.”

“Probably.”

“That confidence is why people love you.”

Julian looked toward the ranking board.

“They asked me this morning whether I felt replaced.”

Kade stopped smiling.

“By me?”

“By the concept of you.”

“That sounds worse.”

“It was.”

Julian’s expression stayed controlled, but Kade had learned where the control sat now.

“What did you say?”

“That you are still bad at conventional defense.”

Kade stared.

Julian smiled.

“I also said Standard Nine is not a ladder rung above me.”

“Less useful for headlines.”

“Much.”

Kade hesitated.

“You okay?”

Julian looked at him.

Kade resisted the urge to make the question smaller.

Julian considered it seriously.

“No,” he said. “But I’m going to training.”

That answer felt more honest than most reassurance.

Kade nodded.

“Same.”

They went inside together.

---

At thirteen thirty, Kade stood in Training Hall Three with Priya Shah trying to hit him.

The fact that she had spent the morning trying to join his allegedly civilization-changing Standard Nine network did not make her slower in ordinary combat.

Unfortunately.

The weekly conventional benchmark was Hale’s idea.

“No Boundary work,” Hale had said. “No unusual transfer. No excuses. I want to know whether you are still learning to fight when nobody cares what your Standard says.”

Kade had pointed toward Priya.

“Why her?”

“Because she enjoys this.”

Priya had smiled.

Hale was correct.

Now she drove Kade backward with a clean three-step combination, waited for the retreat habit he had supposedly fixed months ago, and scored when he gave her half of it back under fatigue.

The training board flashed.

**SHAH 2 — MERCER 1**

Kade reset.

Around the small arena, perhaps forty students watched.

Three months ago they would have been interested because Kade was climbing.

Now they were interested because every fight involving him had become evidence in an argument nobody on campus had agreed to have.

Could Standard Nine beat Standard Six?

Was IX even a combat classification?

Had Kade become stronger after Consensus?

Could he link opponents without consent?

The last rumor had required Sen to publish a statement using the words **structurally impossible and ethically prohibited**.

People had quoted the second half and ignored the first.

Priya settled into stance.

“You’re thinking about the crowd.”

“No.”

“You looked at them.”

“I contain peripheral vision.”

“That is Eli’s phrase.”

“Intellectual property is weak under friendship.”

The signal sounded.

Priya came right.

Kade expected the acceleration burst.

She did not use it.

He moved too early.

She changed levels and took his base.

Kade hit the mat.

Point.

Three-one.

Priya offered a hand.

“Public Nine,” she said.

Kade accepted it.

“I hate you.”

“No, you don’t.”

“Temporarily accurate.”

They reset.

Kade won the next exchange.

Not elegantly.

He watched her hips instead of her field trace and denied the inside route before she committed.

Three-two.

The crowd reacted harder to that point than Priya’s previous two.

Kade heard someone shout, “Use Nine!”

He almost laughed.

Priya did not.

Her eyes hardened.

She attacked.

Kade stayed conventional.

So did she.

The exchange lasted forty seconds.

Kade nearly took the boundary.

Priya recovered, changed direction off the same right foot Kade had learned to read in his first week, and turned his own pressure into the deciding point.

**FINAL — SHAH 4 — MERCER 2**

The arena buzzer sounded.

Kade bent over with both hands on his knees.

Priya remained standing because she was a bad person.

Hale walked onto the mat.

“Assessment?”

Kade looked at the board.

“I lost.”

“Excellent pattern recognition.”

“My conditioning held.”

“Mostly.”

“I stopped chasing her acceleration.”

“Eventually.”

Kade looked at Priya.

She shrugged.

“You still assume the interesting thing is the field.”

Kade straightened.

“That sounded like Mara.”

Priya looked offended.

“I have independent thoughts.”

“Prove it.”

“I beat you.”

“Strong evidence.”

Hale closed the assessment.

On the public board outside the hall, Kade remained #37.

No miracle recalculation.

No Standard adjustment.

He had lost an ordinary benchmark to a student ranked far below him because the benchmark was not the ladder and rank was not prophecy.

That felt healthy.

Annoying.

But healthy.

As Kade left, two younger students were arguing beneath the board.

“See? Nine is useless alone.”

“That’s not what it means.”

“Then what does it mean?”

Neither had an answer.

Kade kept walking.

Neither did he.

Vector Analysis at fourteen hundred had also changed because of the countdown.

Professor Amadi had replaced the normal exercise with an emergency logistics model.

The wall showed Earth, Luna, orbital stations, deep-space arrays, and four hypothetical communication delays.

“Today,” she said, “you are not saving civilization.”

Several students looked disappointed.

“You are allocating bandwidth.”

The disappointment became visible.

Amadi smiled slightly.

“Good. We are learning what heroism looks like in infrastructure.”

Kade liked her more every month.

The problem was simple in the way difficult problems often were.

A hypothetical response packet arrived from outside the solar system.

Four agencies wanted full access.

Three scientific arrays needed uninterrupted telemetry.

A lunar emergency network could not tolerate more than forty milliseconds of additional routing delay.

And the public information system had to remain live enough to prevent panic from becoming its own infrastructure failure.

Kade’s group included Owen Park again, a Vahana exchange applicant named Leena Das, and a quiet Perception student Kade knew only as Arun.

Owen started assigning bandwidth by institutional priority.

Kade stared at the diagram.

“That fails.”

Owen did not look up.

“We have been working together long enough for you to provide more than that.”

“Lunar emergency network.”

“It has reserved capacity.”

“On the same relay you just gave to the science arrays.”

Owen frowned.

Leena leaned over.

“He’s right.”

Kade looked at her.

She pointed to the failover architecture.

“The reserve is logical, not physical. Same backbone.”

Owen muttered something about badly written exercises.

Kade smiled.

“Then the exercise is lying.”

Owen looked at him.

“You have one sentence.”

“It remains good.”

They rebuilt the allocation.

Not around prestige.

Around what failed if delayed.

Emergency communications first.

Public status second—not because public information was more scientifically important, but because eight billion frightened people could create physical consequences faster than a telescope could.

Raw science feeds were buffered.

Government copies were staggered.

The model stabilized.

Their score landed fourth.

Kade stared.

“Fourth?”

Amadi walked past.

“You used too much redundancy.”

“We preserved every critical path.”

“At twice the bandwidth cost of the best team.”

Kade looked toward the first-place model.

Mara’s group.

Of course.

Their network preserved the same critical functions with two fewer mirrored routes.

Mara saw him looking.

She lifted one eyebrow.

Kade mouthed, “Show-off.”

She smiled.

Amadi tapped Kade’s table.

“Mr. Mercer.”

“Yes?”

“Why did I assign this?”

“Because cosmological uncertainty was making the syllabus feel insecure.”

“Try again.”

Kade looked at the countdown in the upper corner of the wall.

The exercise had contained no alien physics.

No Boundary architecture.

No hidden Standard.

Just systems forced to share limited capacity under uncertainty.

“Because whatever the countdown is,” he said, “most of the work before it reaches zero is going to be ordinary.”

Amadi nodded.

“Exactly.”

That bothered Kade in a useful way.

Outside class, Taren stood beside a security officer holding a tablet he was not touching.

Kade slowed.

“You waiting for me?”

“No.”

The officer looked at Taren.

Taren corrected himself.

“I was told to wait here while someone decided whether I am permitted to attend the exchange briefing.”

“That sounds healthy.”

“It is extremely trust-building.”

The officer did not smile.

Taren did.

Kade noticed a notification on the tablet between them.

**DELEGATION CANDIDATE ROUTING — RESTRICTED**

Taren saw Kade see it.

For months, old Taren would have known the list before the people on it did.

Maybe he already did.

Instead he turned the tablet facedown without opening the notification.

Kade looked at him.

Taren shrugged.

“I’m trying something new.”

“What?”

“Finding out information when I’m supposed to.”

“That sounds terrible.”

“It is.”

Kade almost asked whether his own name was on the list.

The question reached his mouth.

Stopped.

If Taren knew, asking would turn restraint into a service Kade expected him to break.

“See you at six,” Kade said instead.

Taren’s expression changed by a fraction.

“If they let me in.”

“Right.”

Kade kept walking.

Trust repair, apparently, could look like two people refusing to make the easy request.

At sixteen hundred, Eli summoned Kade to Systems Lab with the words:

**IT LIVES. BRING SERA.**

Kade arrived to find Sera already there wearing Response training black and an expression of distrust.

Eli stood behind a waist-high metal box attached to a portable field frame.

Prototype Four.

The civilian stabilizer looked less impressive than the Conduits Asterion students wore.

That was the point.

No rare alloys.

No custom neural interface.

No polished housing.

A rescue crew could afford it if Eli got the price down another twenty-two percent.

“What changed?” Kade asked.

“Power routing.”

Sera said, “He means I broke the last one.”

“You dropped it down stairs.”

“Rescue equipment encounters stairs.”

“Not usually from the top.”

“Field realism.”

Eli looked at Kade.

“You see what I endure?”

Kade smiled.

For the next thirty minutes, nobody asked him about Standard IX.

Sera loaded the frame unevenly.

Eli adjusted damping.

Kade held one side physically because his Resonance was irrelevant to the test.

Prototype Four survived.

Eli stared at the readout.

Then sat down hard on the floor.

“It worked.”

Sera looked at the box.

“Once.”

Eli pointed at her.

“Allow joy.”

“Thirty seconds.”

Eli looked at Kade.

“You heard her. Celebrate efficiently.”

Kade laughed.

This was what he had wanted Asterion to remain.

Not safe.

Not simple.

A place where impossible things could coexist with badly labeled bolts and friends arguing over drop tests.

His wrist display vibrated.

A priority summons.

**CENTRAL FORUM — 18:00**

**ALL FIRST-YEAR DELEGATION CANDIDATES**

Kade frowned.

Sera’s display lit too.

Then Eli’s.

Across the lab, three other students checked their wrists.

Eli looked at him.

“Delegation?”

Kade read the message again.

No explanation.

Before he could answer, every wall display in Systems Lab changed.

The countdown disappeared.

For one second, the room held only Asterion’s crest.

Then four unfamiliar academy seals appeared beneath it.

A notification followed.

**EMERGENCY INTERNATIONAL EXCHANGE SESSION — ANNOUNCEMENT 18:00**

Kiyomizu.

Beneath its seal: **PAIRED CONTROL / MUTUAL AUTHORIZATION.**

Kisiwa.

**DISTRIBUTED CIVIC RESPONSE / COMMUNITY LOAD SHARING.**

Vahana.

**COOPERATIVE COMMAND MESH / HANDOFF DOCTRINE.**

Helix.

**MULTI-OPERATOR SHARED-STATE SYSTEMS.**

Kade read the four descriptions twice.

Different schools.

Different countries.

Different public philosophies.

The wording was not the same.

The shape underneath it felt uncomfortably familiar.

Eli stood up from the floor.

“Did everyone independently invent synonyms for ‘people stop being separable’?”

Sera looked at the display.

“Maybe.”

Kade thought of Aaron’s precursor work.

The twelve-person coupled tests.

Asterion’s insistence that its history had been exceptional.

One screen was not evidence.

He had learned that lesson.

Still.

He filed the similarity away.

Sera was already staring at Kisiwa’s seal for a different reason.

Eli had stopped breathing normally at Vahana’s orbital insignia.

Their reactions had nothing to do with him.

Good.

The countdown returned.

**00:270:20:17:03**

The numbers were still falling.

Asterion, apparently, had decided the appropriate response was to make school larger.

Kade could work with that.

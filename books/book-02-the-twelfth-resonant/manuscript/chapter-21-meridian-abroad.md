# Chapter 21 — Meridian Abroad

House Meridian learned how to be homesick in four countries before anyone admitted the word.

The symptoms were inconsistent.

Eli missed Asterion’s terrible potatoes.

Sera missed the sound of the Meridian bell at dinner even though she had once called it “an acoustic hostage situation.”

Julian missed having a room where nobody cared whether he was Rank One.

Mara missed a desk she had organized herself.

Kade missed Room 814.

He did not say that last one.

They were back in transit again, this time between Asterion and a short reciprocal academy conference in Geneva, which Helix insisted was technically not part of the formal circuit and therefore required twice as much paperwork.

Kade sat on the floor of an airport gate with his back against Eli’s equipment case.

“You packed the blue mug,” he said.

Eli looked up from a circuit diagram.

“What blue mug?”

“The Meridian mug.”

“I own several blue objects.”

“The chipped one Taren stole from House Sol after House Trial.”

“He did not steal it.”

“It says SOL on the bottom.”

Eli considered.

“Cultural exchange.”

Kade took the mug out of Eli’s side pocket.

A strip of blue tape still covered the Sol crest badly.

Sera looked over.

“You brought contraband crockery through four security systems.”

“Five,” Eli said.

“Why?”

He shrugged.

“It is ours.”

That ended the argument.

Mara, sitting opposite them, looked at the mug for a moment longer than necessary.

Then she returned to the academy governance packet she had been reading since breakfast.

Kade noticed the packet.

He also noticed she had not turned it toward him.

Progress sometimes looked like leaving a document alone.

Julian arrived carrying six sandwiches.

“Why six?” Kade asked.

“Because you always claim you are not hungry and then take half of someone else’s food.”

“That happened once.”

“Tokyo, Nairobi, Bengaluru, Helix.”

“Different legal jurisdictions.”

Julian handed him a sandwich anyway.

Kade accepted it.

Nobody mentioned Aaron’s model for almost seven minutes.

That was a record.

---

The conference hotel had put the five Asterion students on the same floor but in separate rooms.

This created an immediate problem.

At 22:13, everybody ended up in Eli’s room.

Nobody had agreed to it.

Sera arrived first with tea.

Mara came next because Eli had asked whether his latest stabilizer calculations made sense and she had made the mistake of answering.

Julian brought the leftover sandwiches.

Kade came because his room felt like a room assigned to Kade Mercer, public IX?, rather than a place where he was allowed to be unimportant.

By 22:30 the floor between the beds had become a miniature Meridian Commons.

Shoes by the door.

Food in the middle.

Mara’s tablet balanced on one knee.

Eli arguing from the carpet.

Sera leaning against the wall.

Julian occupying the only actual chair because rank had finally found a legitimate purpose.

Kade used the blue mug.

“This is weird,” he said.

Eli looked around.

“The mug?”

“No. This.”

Sera said, “A room?”

“Everybody being here.”

Mara looked at him.

“You asked us to come.”

“I asked Eli.”

Eli pointed at Sera.

“I asked her.”

Sera pointed at Mara.

“I asked her.”

Julian raised one hand.

“No one asked me.”

Kade smiled.

“Good. System remains decentralized.”

Julian threw a napkin at him.

For twenty minutes they talked about nothing important.

A Kiyomizu student who had challenged Eli to a vending-machine repair contest.

Neema’s refusal to accept Asterion’s definition of emergency drills.

Arjun’s belief that orbital logistics was a personality type.

Jonas’s ongoing campaign to make Kade read a formal proof before criticizing the notation.

Aya’s habit of eating worse the more interesting the research became.

The people they had met were beginning to exist in stories that did not require their schools as labels.

Kade liked that.

It meant the world was becoming populated rather than expanded.

Mara finally closed the governance packet.

Eli noticed.

“Bad?”

“Complicated.”

Kade said, “That word has become threatening.”

Mara handed the packet to Sera instead of him.

“Student disclosure proposal. Sen submitted it after Aaron’s model came out.”

Sera read the first page.

“Opt-out?”

“Partial. Safety telemetry remains mandatory. Research derivatives become visible by category. Strategic access requires separate authorization.”

Julian leaned forward.

“Does it apply retroactively?”

“No.”

Kade felt anger begin.

He let Mara finish.

“She included a historical audit.”

The anger changed shape.

Not gone.

More specific.

“What does that mean?”

“Students can request whether their past data was used in precursor-derived research or strategic profiling.”

Eli sat up.

“Us?”

“Yes.”

Sera put down the packet.

“I want mine.”

Julian said, “Same.”

Kade looked at Mara.

She had already requested hers.

Of course.

He could tell by her face.

“Did you?”

“Yes.”

Kade thought about asking what she expected to find.

Instead he opened his own request form.

Everyone else did too.

Five screens.

Five choices.

No group vote.

The room went quiet in the comfortable way it only did with people who no longer mistook silence for distance.

Kade submitted.

Eli submitted.

Sera submitted.

Julian paused.

Kade noticed and said nothing.

Julian finally closed the form without sending it.

Sera looked at him.

“You not doing it?”

“Not tonight.”

No one pushed.

Good.

---

The next morning Elena called while Kade was walking along the lake before the first conference session.

He answered on video.

She was in the kitchen at home, wearing scrubs and holding a mug that actually belonged to her.

“You look cold.”

“Europe is personally hostile.”

“It is October.”

“Northline taught seasonal flexibility.”

Elena smiled.

Then she looked at him for a second too long.

“What?” Kade asked.

“Aaron called.”

Kade stopped walking.

“When?”

“Yesterday.”

“You talked to him.”

“Yes.”

A short answer.

Not defensive.

Kade stared at the lake.

“How was it?”

Elena breathed out.

“Bad.”

He looked back at the screen.

She almost smiled.

“Also necessary.”

“What did he say?”

“That is between us.”

Kade felt the old reflex.

Need to know.

Family information as shared property.

He recognized it early enough to hate it privately.

“Okay.”

Elena’s face softened.

“He asked if he could call again.”

“And?”

“I said next week.”

Kade nodded.

No reconciliation.

A scheduled next conversation.

That felt more believable.

Elena said, “He asked about you.”

Kade’s stomach tightened.

“What did you say?”

“That you are seventeen and therefore legally required to be difficult.”

“Medical fact?”

“Strong evidence.”

He laughed.

The laugh helped.

They talked about Northline after that.

Darius had been caught fixing the vending machine with a spoon.

Ms. Alvarez wanted Kade to speak to the physics club remotely.

Elena had washed one of his old hoodies and discovered an Asterion transit token in the pocket.

Ordinary information.

Home still existed without waiting for him.

Good.

---

The conference itself was deliberately boring.

That was the point.

Students from five academies sat in mixed groups and compared the parts of school nobody put in promotional videos.

Discipline policy.

Food budgets.

Dorm privacy.

Instructor complaint procedures.

Emergency leave.

Data ownership.

Rank appeal.

Kade’s group included Jonas, Neema, Arjun, Ren, and a Kisiwa second-year named Laila Okafor.

The moderator asked one question:

“What policy at your institution would you steal from another?”

Kade expected jokes.

He got answers.

Ren wanted Helix’s student-data appeal process.

Jonas wanted Kiyomizu’s right to request a partner change without proving misconduct.

Arjun wanted Kisiwa’s rule requiring community stakeholders in emergency-system simulations.

Neema wanted Vahana’s explicit responsibility-handoff notation.

Laila wanted Asterion’s public incident summaries after training injuries.

Everyone looked at Kade.

He thought.

“Kiyomizu’s badges.”

Ren frowned.

“No rank?”

“Not exactly. The idea that an institution does not have to display every true measurement everywhere.”

Jonas nodded.

“Visibility is a design choice.”

“Yes.”

Kade looked at his own badge.

For the conference it read only:

**KADE MERCER — ASTERION**

No #37.

No IX?.

He had known that intellectually.

The body learned slower.

---

That evening the five Meridian students ate at a small restaurant two streets from the hotel because Sera refused to attend another catered reception.

No faculty.

No cameras.

No academy representatives.

Eli ordered something he could not pronounce and immediately regretted it.

Mara helped him without making fun of him for almost thirty seconds.

Julian received a message from his father and turned the screen facedown.

Sera told them Kisiwa had sent preliminary dates for her six-week placement.

The table went quiet.

Then Eli raised the blue mug he had inexplicably brought with him.

“No,” Sera said.

“I am making a toast.”

“With stolen House Sol ceramic?” Julian asked.

“Shared heritage.”

Eli held it up.

“To Sera abandoning us professionally.”

Sera stared.

Kade almost intervened.

Then Sera smiled.

Small.

Real.

“To leaving and coming back,” she said.

Kade felt the sentence land somewhere deeper than the joke deserved.

Everyone raised whatever they had.

Water.

Tea.

The blue mug.

No one promised the group would remain unchanged.

That would have been false.

They celebrated the fact that change did not automatically mean loss.

Later, walking back to the hotel, Kade realized that Meridian had become portable.

Not because the same five people were always together.

Because they had learned how to carry enough of the relationship across distance that separation did not erase it.

Fault tolerance again.

Only this time nobody needed a diagram.

# Chapter 12 — Vahana

Vahana Academy gave every visitor a problem before it gave them a room key.

Kade decided this was becoming an international conspiracy.

The problem arrived on a thin black tablet while the Asterion delegation stood in the arrivals hall of Vahana’s Bengaluru campus, still carrying bags and still pretending the overnight flight had not happened.

The tablet displayed one sentence.

**YOU HAVE CONTROL OF ORBITAL PLATFORM KAPILA-3 FOR THE NEXT NINE MINUTES. PREPARE A HANDOFF.**

Kade looked at Instructor Hale.

“We have not seen Kapila-3.”

Hale looked at his own tablet.

“Neither have I.”

“That seems irresponsible.”

A woman in a dark Vahana jacket approached them.

She was perhaps forty, with a silver thread at one temple and the calm expression of someone who had watched many visiting students become offended by instructions.

“Dr. Meera Iyer,” she said. “Orbital systems faculty.”

Eli straightened immediately.

Kade caught it before Iyer did.

Iyer noticed too.

“Navarro.”

Eli blinked.

“Yes.”

“Vahana requested you first.”

Kade turned to him.

“Congratulations. You are internationally important.”

Eli looked genuinely alarmed.

“Why?”

Iyer said, “Your civilian stabilization work.”

“Oh.”

“That sounded disappointed,” Mara said.

“I was prepared for something more mysterious.”

“Engineering rarely is,” Iyer said.

Eli smiled.

He was done for.

Iyer pointed at the tablets.

“You have nine minutes.”

Kade looked around the arrivals hall.

“Where is the platform data?”

“On the tablet.”

He opened the file.

Kapila-3 was a low-orbit training and research platform used by Vahana’s orbital curriculum.

Current state:

- attitude control nominal;
- thermal loop B running six percent high;
- one external maintenance drone inactive;
- crew sleeping cycle in four hours;
- docking rehearsal scheduled in thirty-two minutes;
- power reserve seventy-eight percent;
- no emergency.

Kade frowned.

“That is it?”

Iyer said, “What were you hoping for?”

“Fire.”

Sera looked at him.

“Do not.”

“I mean informationally.”

Mara was already reading.

Julian had put down his bag.

Aya stood slightly apart, studying the platform dependency map rather than the status list.

Eli whispered, “What counts as handoff?”

Iyer heard him.

“The next team should be able to assume responsibility without calling you.”

Kade looked up.

“That is the whole rule?”

“No.”

“What is the rest?”

“If your handoff causes them to ask the wrong question, you failed.”

That was worse.

The timer began.

---

Kade wrote too much.

He knew this at minute seven.

At minute six he still believed he was being thorough.

His handoff note contained every system dependency he could infer from the platform diagram, three possible explanations for the elevated thermal loop, a priority map for the inactive drone, and a paragraph about docking risk if the thermal anomaly worsened.

Mara’s note was shorter.

Eli’s was a diagram.

Sera wrote four lines.

Julian wrote five.

Aya wrote something that looked like a relationship map with timestamps.

Kade read his own again.

It was excellent.

Probably.

The timer ended.

Iyer collected the tablets.

“Now you inherit one another’s platforms.”

Kade looked at Mara.

“You wrote four lines.”

“Five.”

“That is not enough.”

“You do not know what I wrote.”

“Five lines.”

“Correct.”

The tablets redistributed.

Kade received Eli’s handoff.

It contained a system diagram with three highlighted paths, one circled thermal reading, and a note:

**THERMAL B HIGH BUT STABLE 42 MIN. DRONE 3 DEAD AFTER BATTERY SWAP. DOCKING REHEARSAL OWNS ATTITUDE PRIORITY AT T+23. IF B > +9%, CUT NONESSENTIAL LAB LOAD BEFORE TOUCHING ATTITUDE CONTROL.**

Kade read it once.

Then again.

He knew what mattered.

He also knew what Eli had deliberately not included.

Interesting.

Across the hall Eli was reading Kade’s note.

His face became increasingly concerned.

Kade walked over.

“Problem?”

Eli held up the tablet.

“I do not know what you want me to care about first.”

Kade stopped.

“It is all there.”

“Yes.”

“That is the point.”

“No.”

Iyer appeared beside them.

“What did Mercer hand you?”

Eli looked down.

“Information.”

“And responsibility?”

Eli scrolled.

“Unclear.”

Kade felt personally attacked by typography.

Iyer said, “A handoff is not a biography of the system. It is a declaration of what state you are leaving, what has changed, what is uncertain, and what the next operator must own first.”

Kade looked at Eli’s diagram.

Three highlighted paths.

One uncertainty.

One priority condition.

Visible state.

“Fine,” he said.

Iyer smiled slightly.

“Good. We found the first lesson before housing.”

---

Vahana’s campus did not feel like an orbital academy until Kade looked up.

The buildings were low and broad, built around shaded courtyards and equipment bays rather than towers. Students crossed between labs carrying tool cases, simulation helmets, and parts Kade could not identify.

Then the clouds shifted.

A thin white line moved across the blue above them.

Not an aircraft.

Too high.

Too fast.

Eli stopped walking.

“What is that?”

Their student host looked up.

“Transfer vehicle.”

He was eighteen, tall, with a Vahana logistics band on one wrist and a screwdriver clipped to his collar for reasons Kade did not understand.

“Arjun Rao,” he said. “Second-year orbital systems.”

Eli stared at the sky.

“Crewed?”

“Cargo.”

“Where?”

“Platform cluster east.”

Kade looked at Arjun.

“Do you answer every question with fewer words than necessary?”

Arjun considered.

“No.”

Aya nodded approvingly.

Kade regretted introducing them.

Arjun led them toward residence registration.

Their bands were different from Kiyomizu and Kisiwa.

Vahana displayed role permissions rather than rank.

Kade’s read:

**FIELD SYSTEMS — LIMITED**

**ORBITAL OPERATIONS — OBSERVER**

**BOUNDARY EXPERIMENTS — PROHIBITED WITHOUT FLIGHT DIRECTOR CLEARANCE**

Kade stared at the third line.

“They know me.”

Mara read hers.

“Everybody knows you.”

“That remains invasive.”

Eli’s band was longer.

**POWER / STABILIZATION SYSTEMS — SUPERVISED OPERATOR**

**GROUND TEST HARDWARE — OPERATOR**

**ORBITAL OPERATIONS — TRAINEE**

Kade pointed.

“Navarro outranks me.”

Arjun looked at the bands.

“Of what?”

Julian started laughing.

The joke had survived another country.

---

Vahana’s first classroom looked like a control room someone had cut into twelve pieces.

Each student station had only part of a spacecraft’s state.

No one saw everything.

Iyer stood at the center.

“Orbital systems punish invisible assumptions.”

The wall displayed a simple diagram of a vehicle approaching a station.

“On the ground, bad energy accounting often damages equipment.”

The vehicle moved closer.

“In orbit, the same mistake changes what the entire structure is doing.”

Kade leaned forward.

That sounded relevant.

Iyer assigned six operators.

Eli got power.

Arjun got flight dynamics.

Mara got docking telemetry.

Julian got structural load.

A Vahana student named Leela Nair got thermal control.

Kade got communications.

He stared at the assignment.

“Communications.”

“Yes,” Iyer said.

“I do not know communications.”

“Then you will not improvise with force.”

“Hostile.”

“Preventive.”

Aya watched from the observer station with Sera.

The exercise began.

The docking vehicle approached on a simulated trajectory.

Arjun called state changes.

“Relative velocity point-one-eight.”

Mara answered, “Docking lidar clean.”

Leela said, “Thermal stable.”

Julian reported, “Structure inside envelope.”

Eli said, “Power reserve seventy-one. Guidance load rising.”

Kade’s screen filled with messages from a fictional flight director, crew, and maintenance team.

Too much information.

He tried to forward everything.

The channel clogged.

Arjun looked at him.

“What matters?”

“All of it.”

“No.”

Kade hated Vahana immediately.

A crew message arrived:

**MANUAL ALIGNMENT DRIFT FELT IN PORT FRAME. REQUEST STATUS.**

Kade looked at Julian.

“Crew reports drift sensation.”

Julian checked structural load.

“Nothing on my sensors.”

Mara said, “Lidar still clean.”

Arjun said, “Trajectory nominal.”

Kade looked back at the message.

The easy answer was reassure crew.

Everything said nominal.

Except a person inside the vehicle.

He remembered Kisiwa.

Local knowledge.

Different sensor.

“Kade to crew,” he said. “Describe direction and timing.”

Iyer looked at him.

No approval.

Just listening.

The simulated crew answered.

**RIGHTWARD PULSE AFTER ATTITUDE CORRECTION. THREE TIMES.**

Arjun’s hands moved.

“Attitude correction.”

Eli checked power draw.

“Guidance spike point-four seconds before each report.”

Leela looked at thermal.

“Thruster bank three temperature climbing.”

Mara said, “Lidar clean because position error is being corrected.”

Julian said, “Correction load is entering frame late.”

There.

The vehicle was staying on course by spending more effort than the summary telemetry showed.

Arjun aborted docking.

The simulator marked:

**SAFE HOLD — 00:04 BEFORE THRUSTER LIMIT**

Kade exhaled.

Iyer paused the exercise.

“What found the problem?”

Eli said, “Crew report.”

“Why?”

Mara answered, “Because the summary systems were reporting outcome, not effort.”

Iyer nodded.

“Good.”

Kade looked at his communications screen.

The least technical station had caught the first clue because it contained the only person describing what the system felt like from inside.

That seemed familiar.

---

Lunch at Vahana happened in a long dining hall attached to the engineering block.

Eli sat with Arjun and Leela.

Kade sat beside Mara because every other seat near the Vahana students had filled before he understood what was happening.

Sera was comparing response certification rules with a third-year emergency systems student.

Aya had acquired three diagrams and no food.

Kade pointed at her tray.

“You are becoming worse.”

Aya looked down.

“I have food.”

“You have tea.”

“Tea is food-adjacent.”

Mara said, “Do not encourage her.”

Across the table Arjun and Leela had started arguing.

Not loudly.

Technically.

“Automation should own the routine handoff,” Arjun said.

Leela shook her head.

“Automation should build the packet. It should not decide what uncertainty deserves emphasis.”

“That is sentiment.”

“That is operations.”

Eli looked delighted.

Kade asked, “What are you fighting about?”

Arjun answered, “Human handoff failure.”

Leela answered at the same time, “Automation handoff failure.”

Kade looked at Mara.

“Good school.”

“Complicated school.”

“Better.”

Leela turned to Kade.

“Last year a student team let the system auto-generate a maintenance transfer. Every value was correct.”

“And?”

“It buried a low-confidence sensor inside seventeen pages of nominal state.”

Arjun said, “No incident occurred.”

“Because the next operator read seventeen pages.”

“Which proves human oversight works.”

“Which proves the automation did not hand off responsibility.”

Eli said, “Both.”

They looked at him.

He continued.

“Automation is good at complete state. Humans are better at deciding what the next human is likely to misunderstand.”

Arjun considered.

Leela considered.

Kade said, “Navarro has been internationally promoted.”

Eli ignored him.

The argument continued through dessert.

No one won.

Good.

---

In the afternoon Vahana gave Eli a hardware bay.

Kade went because Eli asked him to.

That distinction mattered now.

The assignment was simple enough to sound fake.

A stabilization module had developed intermittent output noise during thermal cycling.

Eli had four hours to diagnose it with Arjun and a Vahana technician.

Kade sat on a crate and tried not to touch anything.

Eli opened the module.

“Connector.”

Arjun said, “Maybe.”

“You said that too fast.”

“I like connectors.”

“That is not evidence.”

“Neither is enthusiasm.”

They tested.

Connector good.

Eli checked the regulator.

Good.

Thermal sensor.

Good.

Then the module failed only when a separate power-conditioning unit changed load.

Eli stared at the bench.

“Coupled noise.”

Arjun nodded.

“Shared ground.”

They rerouted the test.

The problem disappeared.

Kade looked at Eli.

“You solved it.”

Eli shook his head.

“No. We found the condition.”

“That is solving-adjacent.”

“The module is still wrong.”

They opened the ground path.

A tiny fracture in a mounting trace became visible under magnification.

Eli went quiet.

Kade knew that look.

Not mystery.

Happiness.

A problem with edges.

A thing he could understand.

Arjun said, “You want to rebuild it.”

“Yes.”

“We have spares.”

“I know.”

“That is not a reason.”

“It is to me.”

Kade smiled.

Eli belonged here differently than Sera had belonged at Kisiwa.

Not because Vahana was better for him than Asterion.

Because it asked a part of him to stand at the front instead of behind Kade’s story.

Kade tried not to make that realization about himself.

He was improving.

---

Before dinner, Iyer called the visitors back to the arrivals hall.

Their original handoff notes appeared on the wall.

Kade’s was still too long.

He disliked it on sight.

“Again,” Iyer said.

Same Kapila-3 state.

Same nine minutes.

But now each visitor had to prepare a handoff for a named successor with a specific role.

Kade’s successor was a communications trainee with no structural background.

He deleted half his first note immediately.

Then another quarter.

What did the next operator need to own?

Current state.

One uncertainty.

One threshold.

One scheduled event.

One thing not to mistake for an emergency.

He wrote:

**KAPILA-3 NOMINAL. THERMAL B +6% AND STABLE 42 MIN; WATCH +9% THRESHOLD. DRONE 3 INACTIVE AFTER BATTERY SWAP; NONCRITICAL UNTIL AFTER DOCKING REHEARSAL. DOCKING REHEARSAL T+23 OWNS ATTITUDE PRIORITY. CREW REPORTS NO ANOMALY. IF THERMAL RISES, CUT LAB LOAD BEFORE CHANGING ATTITUDE STATE.**

He read it.

Short.

Incomplete.

Useful.

The timer ended.

Iyer handed his note to Leela.

She read it once.

“What do I own first?” Iyer asked her.

“Docking state until thermal crosses nine.”

“What is uncertain?”

“Why thermal B is high.”

“What can wait?”

“Drone three.”

“What should you not do?”

“Touch attitude control to solve a thermal problem before shedding lab load.”

Iyer looked at Kade.

“Better.”

There.

Visible delta.

Not power.

Communication.

Kade accepted it.

Mostly.

Eli’s second note was even better than his first.

Sera’s remained five lines.

Mara’s became six.

Julian’s became four.

Aya’s was still a map.

Iyer stared at it.

Aya said, “You said prepare a handoff. You did not specify prose.”

Iyer looked at Kade.

“Do not encourage her.”

“I said nothing.”

“That is usually false,” Aya said.

The room laughed.

---

That night the delegation stood on the residence roof.

This roof was not forbidden either.

Kade’s international opinion of roofs continued improving.

The city lights extended farther than he could parse.

Above them, a moving point crossed the sky.

Kapila-3, according to Arjun’s tracker.

Eli stood at the railing.

“Tomorrow we see the orbital training vehicle.”

Kade looked at him.

“You sound twelve.”

“I feel twelve.”

Sera said, “You usually act twelve.”

“Ouch.”

“Also true.”

Eli ignored them.

Kade looked up at the moving point.

On Earth, he had spent months learning that force could move through structures in ways other people did not see.

Tomorrow Vahana wanted to teach him what happened when there was no convenient ground beneath the structure to absorb the consequences.

He suspected he was going to hate it.

That usually meant the lesson was worth having.

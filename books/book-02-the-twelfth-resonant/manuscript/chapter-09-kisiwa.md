# Chapter 9 — Kisiwa

Kisiwa did not send a car to collect them from the airport.

It sent a city bus.

Kade thought this was a mistake until he saw Professor Njeri Kamau waiting beside the rear door with a transit card clipped to her Kisiwa jacket.

Instructor Hale looked at the bus.

Then at Kamau.

“Official transport?”

“Yes.”

Hale looked again.

The bus was carrying twelve ordinary passengers, three Kisiwa students, a woman with two grocery bags, and a man asleep against the window.

Kamau smiled.

“You are visiting a civic-systems school. We considered hiring a private vehicle. It seemed educationally dishonest.”

Sera smiled before anyone else.

Kade noticed.

Eli noticed the charging interface beside the front wheel.

Mara noticed the municipal route map.

Julian noticed a Kisiwa student give up his seat to an older passenger without being asked.

Aya, who had joined the international circuit for the remaining legs after three days of administrative negotiation, noticed all of them noticing different things.

“Good,” she said.

Kade looked at her.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That is usually false.”

Aya had begun using his own phrases against him with increasing confidence.

International exchange was corrupting people.

They boarded.

Nairobi moved past the windows in layers of traffic, construction, glass, concrete, trees, billboards, pedestrians, motorcycles, buses, office towers, apartment blocks, roadside shops, schools, and infrastructure Kade could not read quickly enough to pretend he understood the city.

That helped.

At Asterion, most systems were visible because someone had designed the island to teach them.

Here, the systems had reasons that existed before his arrival.

Nairobi was one of several major Kisiwa teaching hubs across the Union, not a summary of the whole federation. Kamau had said that twice in the exchange briefing. Kade suspected she had met visiting students before.

Kamau stood near the center doors.

“First assignment.”

Kade closed his eyes.

“We have been in the country eleven minutes.”

“Yes.”

“That is becoming an international standard.”

“No Standard jokes here,” Sera said.

Kamau pointed to the route display.

“Tell me what this bus depends on.”

Eli answered immediately.

“Power.”

“Too broad.”

“Charging grid. Battery maintenance. Traffic control. Route scheduling. Driver availability.”

Julian added, “Road condition. Emergency access.”

Mara said, “Passenger information. Fare systems. Network communications.”

Aya looked out the window.

“People knowing the route exists.”

Kamau nodded.

Sera said, “And the other routes. This one matters differently if three alternatives fail.”

Kamau looked at her.

“Yes.”

Sera met the look without changing expression.

Kade knew that face.

She was trying not to look pleased.

He said nothing.

Progress.

Kamau continued.

“At Kisiwa, we do not begin infrastructure training with the Resonant intervention. We begin with the service.”

Kade looked at the passengers.

Not targets.

Not exercise markers.

People going somewhere.

The bus stopped.

A student in a Kisiwa jacket stepped off carrying a toolbox.

Kamau said, “That is David Okello. Third-year civic field systems. He will be one of your hosts.”

Kade watched David cross toward a utility cabinet beside the station.

“Is he fixing something?”

“Probably.”

“You don’t know?”

“He is on municipal rotation. The city tells him what needs work.”

Sera leaned forward.

“Students have live utility access?”

“Under supervision and certification.”

Her voice changed.

Kade heard it.

So did Kamau.

“You are Vale.”

Sera looked at her.

“Yes.”

“Kisiwa requested you first.”

“I know.”

“Good. I dislike surprises that are actually paperwork.”

Eli whispered, “I like her.”

Sera said, “You say that about every competent adult.”

“Not every.”

Hale looked at him.

Eli corrected himself.

“Most.”

---

Kisiwa’s Nairobi campus looked less like an academy than a public-works headquarters that had acquired dormitories by accident.

The main courtyard contained no arena.

Instead it held a section of water pipe large enough for Kade to walk through, a damaged transformer housing, two bridge joints, a collapsed-wall training rig, and three municipal vehicles with Kisiwa student crews around them.

A scoreboard hung above the operations building.

Kade relaxed.

Then he read it.

**WATER RESTORATION — TEAM 4 — 38 MIN**

**TRANSIT RECOVERY — TEAM 2 — 51 MIN**

**CLINIC POWER CONTINUITY — TEAM 7 — 99.3%**

No names.

No individual ranks.

He looked at Sera.

She was already gone.

Not literally.

She had walked twenty meters ahead with Kamau and a Kisiwa student wearing response-orange bands.

Kade followed with the others.

The student turned.

“Imani Mwangi.”

She was eighteen, maybe nineteen, with short braids, a compact responder pack, and the expression of someone who had been told visiting students were coming and had not yet decided whether this was useful.

“Sera Vale,” Sera said.

“I know.”

Imani pointed at the pack on Sera’s shoulders.

“Asterion Response issue?”

“Yes.”

“Too many straps.”

Kade made a sound.

Sera looked at him.

“Do not.”

“I said nothing.”

Imani looked between them.

Kade said, “She made me carry it nine flights.”

“Why?”

“I had opinions.”

Imani nodded.

“Corrective education.”

Sera smiled.

Kade felt betrayed internationally.

Kamau handed out campus bands.

They showed certifications instead of rank.

Kade’s listed his approved field categories and a large red block:

**MUNICIPAL SYSTEM ACCESS — OBSERVER ONLY**

Sera’s read:

**RESPONSE ROTATION — SUPERVISED OPERATOR**

Eli looked over.

“Vale outranks you.”

Kade stared at him.

“Of what?”

Julian started laughing before anyone else.

The joke had traveled.

Kade decided this was success.

---

Their first classroom had no desks.

It had a city map covering the floor.

Not a simplified city.

A real district model with water, power, transport, clinics, schools, drainage, emergency routes, telecom hubs, and population-density layers.

Professor Kamau stood at the edge.

“You have thirty minutes. Improve this district’s disaster resilience.”

Kade looked at the map.

“What disaster?”

“Unknown.”

“That seems unfair.”

“Yes.”

Eli crouched beside the power layer.

“We need redundancy.”

Mara moved toward communications.

Julian studied transport.

Aya looked at service overlap.

Sera did nothing.

Kade noticed.

“What?”

Sera pointed at the population layer.

“Where are the people who know this district?”

Kamau said nothing.

Kade looked around.

No local operators.

No maintenance crews.

No residents.

Just six visiting students and a map.

Sera said, “We cannot improve a real district from a classroom without asking the people who operate it.”

Kamau smiled.

“Correct.”

The floor changed.

**ASSESSMENT COMPLETE**

Kade stared.

“That was the test?”

“Yes.”

“We failed in forty seconds.”

“Vale did not.”

Everyone looked at Sera.

She hated this immediately.

Kamau opened the side doors.

Five adults entered wearing city utility badges.

Water engineer.

Transit planner.

Clinic operations coordinator.

Electrical technician.

Community emergency liaison.

Kamau said, “Now begin.”

The exercise became harder.

And better.

Kade asked the water engineer which lines failed most often.

She showed him two old junctions and one new pipe everyone assumed was reliable but was difficult to isolate.

Mara learned the clinic’s backup generator depended on a fuel route that crossed a flood-prone underpass.

Eli found the power technician had already rejected one elegant redundancy scheme because replacement parts took four months to import.

Julian asked the transit planner which route could absorb evacuation traffic without blocking ambulances.

Aya asked the community liaison which neighborhoods ignored official emergency messages and why.

Sera moved between all five conversations without taking ownership of any of them.

Kade noticed that too.

After thirty minutes, their proposed system looked less impressive than his first imagined solution.

It was also real.

More local shutoff valves.

A community radio fallback.

A different clinic fuel route.

Two low-cost power-transfer points instead of one advanced Resonant installation.

Training for local maintenance crews.

A transit priority plan.

Kamau asked, “Who designed it?”

Kade looked at the group.

Nobody answered.

Good.

---

Lunch happened outside under a long shade roof between the operations building and a student workshop.

Kade had expected a formal welcome meal.

Instead they joined a line with students coming off rotations.

Some wore clean uniforms.

Some did not.

One student had mud dried to both knees.

Another smelled faintly of ozone.

Imani sat with Sera.

David Okello joined them carrying the toolbox Kade had seen on the bus route.

Asha Mbele, a Vahana-bound Kisiwa student from the coastal engineering program, sat beside Eli and immediately asked about Prototype Four.

Eli nearly forgot to eat.

Kade sat with Mara, Aya, Julian, and a second-year named Samwel Rukundo who specialized in structural access.

Samwel looked at Kade’s observer-only band.

“Public Nine.”

Kade sighed.

“Question mark.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

Samwel ate for a moment.

“Can you do the floor thing here?”

Kade stared.

The phrase had survived continents.

“The floor thing.”

“Broken Floor footage.”

“Probably not legally.”

“Good.”

“You sound disappointed.”

“No. Relieved.”

Kade looked at him.

Samwel shrugged.

“We have enough visiting people who see damaged infrastructure and want to demonstrate something.”

“That happens often?”

“Every exchange.”

Kade liked him immediately.

Mara asked, “What do they usually miss?”

Samwel pointed at the workshop.

“Repairability.”

Aya looked over.

“Explain.”

“If your brilliant field solution requires one person with a rare architecture every time the joint shifts, you did not repair the bridge. You rented a miracle.”

Kade stopped eating.

Mara looked at him.

Aya looked at him.

Julian looked at him.

Kade pointed his fork at all three.

“Do not.”

Samwel frowned.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That is usually false,” Aya said.

Samwel laughed.

International corruption continued.

---

The argument started at the next table.

Kade heard the phrase **arena funding** and stopped pretending not to listen.

Two Kisiwa students in combat-gray bands were sitting with a response trainee and a municipal engineering student.

One of the combat students, a tall fourth-year named Peter Kato, tapped the operations scoreboard with his spoon.

“Clinic continuity does not sell broadcast rights.”

The response trainee rolled her eyes.

“People staying alive is a weak marketing strategy?”

“That is not what I said.”

“It is what you meant.”

Peter looked toward Kamau’s building.

“What I mean is the Union uses combat revenue to pay for half these rotations and then acts embarrassed that combat exists.”

Samwel turned in his seat.

“That is not wrong.”

Imani heard him.

“Do not encourage him.”

Peter pointed at her.

“You get municipal funding.”

“For response certification.”

“Yes. Because governments like disaster crews. They do not fund tournament travel.”

Sera watched the argument with obvious interest.

Kade knew the feeling.

Asterion arguments usually assumed combat prestige was the center and everything else had to justify itself around that.

Kisiwa’s argument was different.

Not absence of prestige.

A fight over what prestige should buy.

The second combat student said, “My scholarship requires top-eight regional placement. If civic rotation takes twelve hours a week from training, that matters.”

Imani answered, “And if your field work cannot operate around a live electrical crew, that should matter too.”

Peter looked at Sera.

“You are Asterion.”

Sera frowned.

“Yes.”

“Your school understands competition.”

“That is not automatically praise.”

Kade smiled into his food.

Peter continued.

“Would you cut combat training for municipal rotation?”

Sera thought before answering.

“I would cut anything that makes a responder worse at the job they claim to be training for.”

“That avoids the question.”

“No. It changes it.”

Peter leaned back.

Sera said, “If you are training to compete, train to compete. If you are training to lead field crews during emergencies, then knowing how those crews work is not a distraction.”

Imani looked pleased.

Sera noticed and immediately looked annoyed at being noticed.

Peter considered her answer.

Then nodded once.

“Fair.”

No ideological conversion.

No applause.

The argument moved on to travel budgets.

Kade looked at Samwel.

“So Kisiwa has arena politics.”

“Of course.”

“I was promised a better civilization.”

“Who promised you?”

“No one.”

“Then we are doing well.”

Mara smiled.

Kade looked around the lunch area.

Good school.

Complicated school.

Better.

---

In the afternoon, Kade expected training.

He got maintenance.

Professor Kamau assigned each visitor to a live but low-risk campus system.

Mara went to communications backup testing.

Julian joined structural inspection.

Aya worked with a paired-control water-balancing crew.

Eli disappeared into a microgrid lab with Asha.

Sera left campus with Imani for a supervised response rotation.

Kade received a leaking irrigation valve.

He stared at it.

“This is punishment.”

David Okello crouched beside the valve box.

“This is water.”

“I have manipulated building-scale load paths.”

“Congratulations.”

“The valve is mechanical.”

“Yes.”

“I do not know how to fix it.”

“Yes.”

Kade looked at David.

David smiled.

“Good. We found the lesson.”

The valve supplied one of the campus garden systems. Not critical. Not glamorous. It had been losing water for two days because the replacement seal arrived late.

David opened the housing.

“Show me the failure.”

Kade looked.

“Seal degraded.”

“That is the part.”

“You asked failure.”

“Yes.”

Kade frowned.

He looked again.

The seal had degraded because the stem sat slightly off-axis.

The stem was off-axis because the bracket had loosened.

The bracket had loosened because vibration traveled through a nearby pump line.

“The seal is consequence.”

David nodded.

“Better.”

They fixed the bracket first.

Then replaced the seal.

Kade used no Resonance.

The valve stopped leaking.

David closed the box.

“How long will that last?”

Kade considered.

“If the pump vibration stays inside current range, longer.”

“And if it changes?”

“We inspect the bracket.”

“Can someone other than you do that?”

“Yes.”

“Then maybe you repaired something.”

Kade thought of Samwel.

*You rented a miracle.*

He disliked Kisiwa with increasing respect.

---

Sera returned after dark.

Kade was sitting outside the residence building with Eli and Julian when the response vehicle pulled in.

Sera climbed out first.

Her uniform was dusty.

There was a dark smear across one sleeve.

Kade stood automatically.

She saw him.

“I’m fine.”

He stopped.

Good.

“What happened?” he asked.

Imani came around the vehicle carrying two empty medical cases.

“Warehouse stair collapse. Minor injuries. Mostly access problem.”

Kade looked at Sera.

“You worked it?”

“Yes.”

Her face was different.

Not shaken.

Focused after the fact.

The way she looked after a difficult fight she believed had mattered.

Imani said, “Vale found the secondary entrance.”

Sera shook her head.

“The site manager told us there was one.”

“You asked him.”

“That is not the same as finding it.”

Imani looked at Kade.

“She does this.”

Kade smiled.

“Yes.”

Sera took off her responder pack.

Kade expected her to hand it to him.

She did not.

He was slightly disappointed.

She sat on the low wall.

Eli asked, “How bad?”

“Three injured. One ankle fracture. Two cuts. No trapped workers after the second sweep.”

“Resonance?” Julian asked.

“Minimal. One structural hold while crews cleared a stair landing.”

Kade waited.

Sera looked at him.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You want to ask whether I used the thing you taught me.”

Kade winced.

“I was not going to phrase it that badly.”

Sera’s expression softened.

“I used what Response taught me.”

There it was.

A tiny sentence.

A large separation.

Kade nodded.

“Good.”

He meant it.

The strange part was that it hurt a little anyway.

Not because she was leaving.

Because she was becoming someone in a direction he had not made.

Maybe those were related feelings.

He would have to learn the difference.

---

The next morning, the Kisiwa operations board showed a new line.

**WAREHOUSE ACCESS EVENT — RESPONSE REVIEW 08:30**

No names.

Sera’s contribution existed inside the event without becoming the headline.

She stood beneath the board reading the review notice.

Imani joined her.

“You coming?”

“Yes.”

Kade approached with breakfast in one hand.

Sera looked at him.

“I’ll see you later.”

Not invitation.

Not rejection.

Information.

Kade stopped two meters away.

“Have fun reviewing a stair collapse.”

“That is not fun.”

“Kiyomizu changed my definitions.”

Imani looked between them.

Sera said, “He means useful.”

“Dangerous translation.”

Sera almost smiled.

Then she left with Imani.

Kade watched her go.

Mara arrived beside him.

“You are doing the face again.”

“What face?”

“The one where another person has a life.”

Kade looked at her.

“That is a cruelly broad category.”

“Yes.”

He looked back at the operations board.

Water.

Transit.

Power.

Access.

Clinic continuity.

Lives held together by systems nobody cheered for unless they failed.

Asterion had taught Kade to climb a ladder.

Kiyomizu had taught him to see what happened between people on it.

Kisiwa, after less than a day, seemed determined to ask whether the ladder was attached to anything useful.

Kade took another bite of breakfast.

He suspected the answer was going to be inconvenient.

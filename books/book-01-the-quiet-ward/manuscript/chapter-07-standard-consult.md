# Chapter 7 — Standard Consult

On the eighth morning the fog broke early, which the ward treated as an event. The water came back at 07:10 — gray first, then a color that was almost a color — and the day staff mentioned it to each other in passing the way people mention a returned acquaintance, and bed six's family arrived at nine with a folding cart and a set of logistics questions that the discharge coordinator answered twice, patiently, because good news makes people unable to hear. Sable countersigned the discharge medication reconciliation standing at the nursing station, checked it against Osei's annotations, found the two of them in agreement as usual, and initialed the line. It was ordinary work and she did it at ordinary speed, and the whole time, in the manner of a background process she had stopped pretending she could suspend, she was aware of the folder on her office desk with a photograph in it that she had now failed to classify for two full days.

She had not been idle with it. That was the distinction she kept auditing herself on, because idleness and failure produced the same output and she wanted her chart of herself accurate. In two days she had run the image through every pattern-analysis routine the facility's document system offered, which were built for handwriting recovery and returned nothing useful, as expected. She had checked it against the standard atlas of field-trace morphologies, all four hundred and eleven plates, in order, in one sitting, and against the appendix of known instrument artifacts, and against — this had been the eleven o'clock hour of the second night — a reference text on notation systems, musical and mathematical and stenographic, on the theory that a technician's memory might have stored the thing in a technician's format. Every comparison had come back the same way: not a wrong answer. No answer. The pattern matched nothing, resembled nothing, violated nothing. It sat outside the space her references covered, the way the null sat outside the space her instruments covered, and after two days she was prepared to write the finding she had been circling since the scanner first rendered it: the classification failure was not a deficit of effort. It was a deficit of library. She had reached the edge of what could be done with this object inside her own facility, with her own training, and the correct response to a reached edge was the same as it had ever been. You did not stand at it repeating the acquisition. You found someone whose library was bigger.

Which meant Orlan.

She had been carrying the call for three days — since before the photograph existed, if she was honest, since the night she had lain in the dark itemizing the week ahead and found *one call she had not yet decided to make* sitting in the itinerary like a line item she kept declining to fund. The reluctance was worth examining and she had examined it, and it had turned out not to be fear, which she had checked for first because fear was the diagnosis that most wanted to hide. It was specification. Vey Orlan was at the Northern Pacific Medical Institute; Standard Seven; twenty years in boundary neurology, which meant twenty years of the cases that arrived at his institution precisely because they had defeated institutions like hers. He was not her supervisor and never had been. He was the colleague you called when a case pushed past the edge of your training, and she had called him perhaps five times in eleven years, and each time she had called with a formed question, because that was the covenant of the off-the-record consult: you did not spend a specialist's attention on an unformed problem. For three days she had not known what she would ask him. *What is wrong with my patient* was not a question; it was the case. *Have you seen a null* was closer, but a null was a negative, and you could not hand someone a negative and ask them to recognize it.

The photograph had changed that. Now she had a positive — an object, produced by the patient, reproduced from memory, unclassifiable by her library — and the question had assembled itself the way the week's earlier decision had, without consulting her, overnight. She would describe the null. She would transmit the image. And she would ask him the only question that mattered at an edge: *have you seen either of these before.*

She placed the call at 10:40, from her office, with the door closed, which she noted, because she closed her door perhaps a dozen times a year and each closure was a datum. The institute's switchboard passed her through two extensions and a silence, and then Orlan's voice arrived, unhurried, slightly graveled, unchanged in four years.

"Sable Hren," he said. "It's been a while. Which means you have a case."

"I have a case," she said. "Off the record, if you'll take it that way."

"I generally do, with you. Go ahead."

She gave it to him the way she would have wanted it given to her: in acquisition order, without interpretation. Transfer from Carrow Bay Clinical Institute, nine weeks there, eight days here. Presenting classification acute Resonance dissociation syndrome, assigned at the prior facility, supporting data not in the packet. Patient coherent, cooperative, oriented, unremarkable on physical examination. Field diagnostics: null. Not low, not disorganized, not zero — null. BM-1 twice on her own calibrated array, BM-2 differential, BM-3, BM-4, all no study generated. Empty-room acquisition normal; instruments confirmed functional; manufacturer's technical line consulted, instruments cleared, technician's verbatim comment charted. Three borrowed instruments from three different manufacturers, three different sensing principles: null, null, null. BM-5 coupling series normal on every compound tested — pharmacology unremarkable, the anomaly specific to field instrumentation, not global. Differential of fifteen documented categories: nine eliminated, four open pending longitudinal data, one permanent unknown. And one item — she told him this too, because he would want the shape of her ignorance as well as its contents — one item circled and unpursuable: the patient's Standard Three classification might be a placeholder assigned by default, because if the original classification instruments had returned what her instruments returned, a Standard Three entry was simply what the form produced, and the original classification records were not in the file and she had no authority to request them.

Orlan did not interrupt once. When she finished there was a pause of the working kind, the kind she recognized from her own end of such calls — a specialist walking the data once around before touching it.

"Cross-checked across three manufacturers," he said. "You're certain of the calibration chain on your own array."

"Timestamped before admission. I ran the calibration myself."

"Of course you did." Not flattery; inventory. "Coupling normal. So his boundary layer is present and behaving, and only the classification instruments fail. That's —" he stopped, and she heard him decline to finish the sentence, and noted the declining. "Go on. You said there was an object."

"A patient-generated record." She described the journal: blank paper, the instruction, the reasoning — a failed modality, a changed instrument. She described what had come back on page one, in the terms she had charted: a pattern of lines, not closed, non-geometric, exact in execution, drawn once at consistent weight without correction, from memory, presenting as a fragment of something larger not on the page. "I have it as clinical imaging, document resolution. I'd like to transmit it. Licensed consult channel — I'll log it as telemetric transmission to offsite consult under my BM-6."

"Send it," Orlan said.

She keyed the transmission from the console, watched the file leave, and heard, faintly, an alert land on his end. Then she heard nothing. She had expected a pause — a viewing was a viewing — and had calibrated her expectation to a careful first read, ten seconds, perhaps fifteen. The pause went past fifteen. It went past thirty. No sound of movement on the line, no shift of a chair, none of the small percussion of a man doing something else while he looked; there was the particular silence of complete attention, and it continued, and she did not fill it, and by the time he spoke she had stopped estimating its length, which was itself information she recorded for later.

"Where did this come from," Orlan said.

Not what is it. Where did it come from. She noted the question he had chosen.

"The patient drew it. Sixth day of admission, first page of the journal, overnight, unprompted as to content. He was a Resonance field technician — civilian research contractor. When I asked where the shape came from, he said it was what he had been trying to say in his interview. That he ran out of words and drew it instead. I charted his words verbatim and appended a no-interpretation annotation. What you're seeing is the intake scanner's rendering. The original is in his possession. It's his."

"From memory," Orlan said. "You said reproduced from memory. Reproduced from what?"

"An incident eighteen months ago. In transit, in open space, forty-one seconds during which his personal field monitor logged no reading. Platform diagnostic confirmed the monitor functioning correctly. That number has survived every audit I can run against it."

Silence again — shorter this time, but the same grade of it.

"Vey," she said. "Have you seen anything like it?"

She listened to him weigh the answer. She could hear the weighing; it had duration.

"Not that pattern specifically," he said. "But that quality."

He did not elaborate. She waited a moment to be sure the elaboration was not coming — with Orlan you extended the courtesy of the wait, because he paid out information at his own rate and interrupting the rate got you less, not more — and it was not coming, and she filed the sentence exactly as delivered: *not that pattern specifically, but that quality*, unglossed, source Standard Seven, twenty years. Then she asked the other question, the one the whole call had been built to carry.

"And the null. Have you encountered a null return before. A true null — instruments functional, no study generated, consistent across principles."

This pause was different from the others. The others had been a man examining data. This one was a man examining what he was permitted, or prepared, to say — she could not distinguish which from her end, and she noted that she could not. When he spoke, it was slower than he had spoken at any point in the call, and she understood that the slowness was the message's container, and she took out her pen.

"Sable, I've seen null readings twice in my career. Both times the case was closed on military medical authority within six weeks. I can't tell you more than that. I'm sorry."

Each word had been set down the way the lines on the journal page had been set down: once, at consistent weight, without correction. She wrote the sentence as he said it. He was not frightened — she had heard frightened men on consult lines, and their sentences ran fast and hedged, and this was the opposite grade of speech. This was a man handling information the way she handled a calibrated instrument, with the deliberateness of someone who knew its weight and did not intend to drop it on her carelessly. It occurred to her, while her pen finished, that Orlan had spent twenty years learning to be careful with what he knew for the same reason she had spent eleven learning to be careful with what she measured, and that she was hearing the cost of the learning in the spacing of his words.

"I understand," she said, and did. Then: "The six weeks. Did the patients recover?"

"I don't know. The files were sealed. I only know the cases were closed."

"Closed by whom?"

"Government Medical Authority. Not the local facility. Not the regional board. The government office."

She wrote that down too. *Government Medical Authority. Not local. Not regional.* The distinction had been offered unprompted, in ascending order, which meant he had thought about the distinction before, possibly for years, and considered it the load-bearing fact. She looked at the three lines in her notepad and was aware of the room around her — the closed door, the console with the transmission log still open, the folder with the photograph — the way one is aware of a room when a piece of furniture has just been moved in it.

"Thank you," she said.

"Sable."

She waited.

"I would advise against continuing with this case."

He said it the way he had said everything else in the last two minutes: placed, level, complete. It was not a warning delivered for effect and it was not a request; it was a recommendation from a colleague with more data than he could share, offered in the only form the data's condition permitted, and she received it as that.

"I've committed to a week," she said.

He did not argue. She had known he would not — Orlan had never once in eleven years argued past a stated position, hers or anyone's; he treated other people's decisions as their jurisdiction — but she noted the not-arguing anyway, because there were flavors of it, and this one was not neutrality. This one was a man declining to repeat a recommendation because he knew both what it was worth and what it would cost, and had decided the arithmetic was hers to run.

"I'm sorry I can't tell you more," he said.

"You've told me more than I had."

A small sound came down the line that was not quite a laugh — acknowledgment, she decided, of the accounting. "Be careful," he said.

"I will."

They hung up. She sat for a moment with her hand still on the receiver, in the closed office, in the quiet, and then she opened the notepad flat and wrote the consult up — not in the chart, where a consult of this kind did not belong and had never been going to belong, but in the private file, in the flat voice she kept for entries she expected to reread: *Off-record consult, V. Orlan, NPMI, Standard Seven. Full field data presented; journal p.1 image transmitted, licensed channel, logged. Orlan on the image: recognized a quality, not the pattern. Did not name the quality. Chose "where did it come from" over "what is it." On the null: two prior null cases in his career. Both closed on military medical authority within six weeks. Files sealed. Patient outcomes unknown to him. Closing authority: Government Medical Authority — specified as distinct from local facility and regional board, unprompted. Advised against continuing. Not frightened. Careful.* She read it back and it was accurate, and then she added the last line, because a summary that omitted the operational fact was a chart note, not an analysis: *This case has been seen before. Twice. Both prior instances were removed from clinical hands inside six weeks, and what happened to the patients afterward is recorded nowhere I can reach.*

She sat with that for a while. Deliberately — she gave it the sitting, the way she would have given a difficult film the full lightbox time rather than the glance that confirms what you already decided to see. She was eight days into an admission with a patient whose chart was structurally empty — null instruments, absent records, a classification that might be a placeholder, a prior facility's file gone somewhere she could not follow. Until 10:40 this morning that emptiness had been a diagnostic problem: hers, local, a matter between her library and an object that exceeded it. It was still a diagnostic problem. But it was now a diagnostic problem with a history, and the history had a pattern, and the pattern had a clock in it. Twice before, somewhere, clinicians she would never identify had sat where she was sitting — she assumed the nulls had landed on people who ran the acquisitions twice and called the manufacturers, because that is who nulls land on — and within six weeks the cases had been lifted out of their hands by an office she did not encounter in clinical medicine, and sealed, and the patients had gone wherever the files went. *Did the patients recover. I don't know.* From Orlan, who did not say *I don't know* loosely any more than she did, the sentence was not a gap in his knowledge. It was the finding.

She was not frightened. She checked, with the flat interior audit, and found the reading accurate: pulse unremarkable, judgment unimpaired, no impulse to act faster than the data warranted. What she found instead was a recalibration she recorded in the observer's column: the case had acquired a horizon. Not a deadline — she had no evidence that any office knew or cared about Room Four, and she declined to invent surveillance she could not support — but a horizon, a documented interval inside which cases of this signature had twice stopped being cases and become sealed files. She had committed to a week. The week stood. She had meant it when she said it to Fenn, and auditing herself now with the new information in place, she meant it more, not less, which told her something about herself that she wrote down without comment. Then she squared the notepad on the desk, opened the door, and went back out into the ward, which had continued to exist, and did her job until late afternoon.

She went to Room Four at the end of the day, in the hour when the light off the water came in low and the west corridor was at its quietest. The door stood open a hand's width. He was at the window, and the journal was not on the sill where it lived; it was open on the overbed table, face up, and he stepped back from it as she came in — not away from her; toward the wall, making room, the way a technician steps back from a bench to let a colleague read an instrument directly rather than through his account of it.

She had decided, walking the corridor, not to tell him about the call. She had examined the decision for cowardice and found it was not that. It was that she could not yet state what the information was for him. She did not know whether *this has happened twice before* would land as company or as sentence — whether a man who wanted above all to be believed would receive it as evidence that he was real, or whether a man who had spent eighteen months being processed would hear only the six weeks and the sealed files and the outcomes recorded nowhere. Until she could predict the landing, transmitting was not communication; it was discharge of her own load onto a patient, and she declined it. If that calculus changed she would revisit it. She noted the open item and let it stand.

Then she looked at the page, and stopped thinking about the call.

It was page one. The same page — she confirmed that first, the way she confirmed a patient identifier before reading a film, because the alternative explanations mattered: it was not a second drawing, not a fresh attempt, not a variation. It was the drawing she had scanned two days ago, continued. The pattern had more in it now. She could not say, looking, precisely what the more consisted of — whether the lines had been extended past their old ends, or new lines had been added among them, or whether the directionality she had registered on the first viewing had clarified because there was now more of it to register — and she caught her eye running the comparison against her memory of the scan and failing to localize the difference, which was itself a finding: the additions were not appended to the original. They were continuous with it, laid down at the same weight, with the same exactness, no seam anywhere she could find. The photograph in her office, she understood, standing there, was a crop. It had been a crop when she took it; she simply had not known it yet, because the rest had still been inside the patient.

"I remembered more of it," Ruan said.

Flat. Factual. A technician reporting a recovered file.

She kept her voice at the register they had built between them. "How long has it been since you last saw it?"

"The forty-one seconds," he said.

Meaning: eighteen months. Meaning: he was not drawing from a recurring perception, not from dreams, not from anything ongoing. He was drawing from a single exposure, forty-one seconds long, a year and a half old — and the memory was not degrading, which is what memories did, which is what every model of memory she had ever been taught required them to do. It was resolving. More of it was arriving on the page than had been there two days ago, with no new exposure, the way a long acquisition builds signal the longer the instrument integrates. She observed the thought, marked the last clause as analogy rather than finding — she had evidence only of what the page was doing, not the memory — and struck the mechanism while keeping the observation. The page had more on it. He attributed the more to remembering. His words, as given, would go in the chart; the analogy would not.

"I'd like to photograph it again," she said. "Same as before. Clinical imaging, attributed to you."

"All right," he said. And then, as she picked the journal up, with no change of pressure: "It's not finished."

She looked at him. He was watching the page in her hands, not her, and his face had the expression it had worn when he reported the monitor log — the expression of a man stating an instrument reading he did not expect to be believed and had stopped adjusting for.

"Noted," she said, and wrote it down verbatim, and took the journal to the imaging bay.

The scanner rendered it at document resolution, exact twice over again, and she filed it to the chart as clinical imaging, second acquisition, with an annotation she kept as spare as the first: *field journal p.1, re-imaged day 8. Same page continued by patient — no second drawing. Additions continuous with original, same weight, no visible seam; examiner unable to localize boundary between prior and new material. Patient's words verbatim: "I remembered more of it." Interval since exposure per patient: the original incident. Patient states the record is not finished. No interpretation offered by patient beyond the above. No interpretation applied by this examiner.* She read it back and kept it, and returned the journal to Room Four, to the sill, because it was his.

The building went into its evening configuration while she sat with the day. Bed six was gone — discharged at four, the family and the folding cart and the reorganized household all launched down the coast road in the last of the light — and the census was one lighter, and the repeater above her console showed five channels running slow and healthy and the sixth keeping its station, blank, attended. On the desk, squared to its edge: the notepad, open to the morning's entry, Orlan's sentences in her own hand; beneath it the first photograph, which she now knew to be a crop; in the chart, the second, which she suspected would prove to be one too. She had spent a week believing she was holding an unprecedented problem, and the belief had been wrong in the one way she had not tested: the problem was not unprecedented. It had been held before, twice, by hands she could not identify, and both times an office had come and taken it, and the holders and the held had gone into files that even a Standard Seven with twenty years could not open, and he was sorry, and what he could tell her fit in three sentences and had taken him visible care to say.

Six weeks. She was inside the number now — day eight of it, if the clock ran here at all, and she had no evidence it did, and she declined to promote the assumption above its evidence, and she also declined to pretend she had not done the subtraction. She had one week committed and she intended to complete it: the longitudinal columns filling, the journal integrating, whatever it was integrating, on the sill of a room whose door stood open a hand's width. She took stock in the flat voice, the way she ended every acquisition day. A number that had survived every audit. A page that was getting larger from a source eighteen months closed. A colleague's three sentences, placed like calibration weights. A patient who said *it's not finished* the way other men said the time. None of it classified. All of it attended. She sat for a while in the sound of the ward at evening — trays, the far elevator, the building breathing through its vents — present with a problem that had a history she could not see, and she did not go home until she had read the morning's entry one more time and found that it was still true, and that she was still there, and that both of those facts were, for now, the treatment plan.

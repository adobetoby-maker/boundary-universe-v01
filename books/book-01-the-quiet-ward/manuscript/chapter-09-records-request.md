# Chapter 9 — Records Request

On the tenth morning she took her coffee to the desk instead of the window and laid out, in the flat voice, an inventory of what she did not have. It was a short list with a great deal inside it. She did not have the imaging series from the nine-week admission at Carrow Bay, which she had requested on the first day, by voicemail, from a physician of record who had been off service then and had in eleven days neither returned to it nor arranged for anyone to answer in his place. She did not have a coupling record. She did not have a physician's narrative — not a discharge summary that summarized, not a progress note that progressed, nothing in the transfer packet that constituted one clinician telling another what nine weeks of attention had produced. She had a phrase, *unresolvable field instability*, three words wide with no data behind them, and she had a patient, and she had a chart that was entirely her own work, built from zero, every entry hers, which was either an achievement or a symptom depending on which morning she considered it. For nine days she had worked inside those limits on the theory that the limits were temporary — a slow records office, a busy service, an off-rotation physician. Eleven days without a returned call was no longer a busy service. It was a datum. She had told Fenn one more week and had meant it, and she was inside the week now, and the week deserved to be spent on something other than waiting for a voicemail to age.

So she decided to make the request properly, which is to say in writing, through the channel that existed for exactly this, and she examined the decision before executing it because she examined everything before executing it. It was not an escalation. She checked this the way she checked a dosage. An escalation would have been a call to Carrow Bay's administration, or a complaint to the regional board, or Amroth's department head cc'd in a pointed line; escalation was an instrument and she was not reaching for it. A phone call was a favor between colleagues, deniable, forgettable, dependent on who picked up. A written request through the regional clinical records system was neither of those things. It was a document. It created an obligation with a timestamp, and it created a paper answer — whatever the answer turned out to be — and after nine days of acquiring objects she could not classify, she had developed a certain respect for answers that arrived on paper and could be clipped to something. She was not confronting anyone. She was doing what the situation required, in the form the situation required it in, and the fact that it had taken ten days to become the required form was itself information about the case, which she noted, and then set aside, and then wrote the request.

She wrote it before rounds, in one sitting, and held it to the discipline of a good requisition: correct in form, specific in content, no adjectives. She requested the complete clinical record of the nine-week admission — all diagnostic imaging with acquisition parameters, all field-coupling records, all clinical narratives and progress documentation, all physician and nursing notes, and any ancillary studies performed during the admission period. She gave the patient's full name and date of birth, the admission and transfer dates, the transferring facility and the receiving one, her own name and license enumeration as physician of record. She did not explain why she wanted the records, because the request form did not ask and because the reason — *I am treating a man whose file arrived structured like a redaction* — was not a sentence the form had a field for. She read it once as its writer and once as its recipient, found nothing in it a records clerk could bounce, signed it, and submitted it through the regional clinical records system before the morning meeting. The system issued a receipt with a routing number, which she printed and put in the physical chart, because the case had taught her that things which existed only in one place had a way of coming to exist in none.

Then she went to rounds, because the ward did not know she had filed anything, and would not have cared.

The next two days were, by the standard of this admission, ordinary days, and she attended them the way she attended everything, which meant she noticed that they were good ones. Bed nine stepped down on the eleventh morning — an actual transfer, orderlies and paperwork and a daughter up from the south county thanking every person who passed at a volume the ward absorbed with practiced grace — and the bed was turned over by noon and filled by evening with a routine forty-eight-hour observation case from the main hospital, the kind of admission that resolves itself and asks only to be watched while it does. Bed two, having won a partial adjustment of the fluid restriction earlier in the week, opened negotiations on the salt, and Sable heard the argument in full, declined it in part, and noted that the arguing itself was the best vital sign on that chart. Osei flagged a formulary substitution before it happened instead of after, which was the whole difference between a pharmacist and a dispensary. The fog came in on schedule and went out late, and the west rooms held their pewter light through the middle of both days, and the ward made the sound Ruan had named, or something within tolerance of it.

Room Four had its own kind of ordinary now, and she had learned its parameters the way she learned any baseline. He ate the breakfast completely and the lunch by about half, every day, the proportion so consistent she could have charted it as a ratio; he had told the aide — who told the nurse, who told Sable, the ward's own telemetry — that the kitchen's bread was better than Carrow Bay's and the coffee worse, delivered as calibration data, no complaint attached. He slept in two blocks, by the night log: down about eleven, a waking somewhere in the small hours that he spent, per the night nurse, sitting up with the light off — not distressed, she'd noted, just awake, the way you'd sit up to listen to weather — and then down again until the building's six o'clock sounds. The lumbar pillow stayed where he had specified it. The light-station book was past halfway now, and on the eleventh afternoon he told her, unprompted, that the maintenance histories were mostly records of fog — decades of a keeper logging what the light could not penetrate, which was, he observed, its own kind of instrument reading: a measurement of the medium expressed as the failures of the signal. She wrote that one down too. He was having, if not good days, then days that were level and inhabited, and in a unit like hers that was a clinical status, and she charted it in its proper flat terms and let the rest of what it was remain off the record where it lived.

The journal kept adding. She had stopped expecting it not to. On the eleventh evening she asked to see it and he handed it over the way he always did, and page one had more in it again — the same single unbroken weight, no seam, no correction, and the extensions that had crossed the printed margin on day nine had continued outward and were approaching the cut edge of the paper itself, the physical boundary of the page, as if the printed margin had been merely the first of the tolerances the pattern would drift through. She photographed it on the intake scanner, fourth acquisition, and filed it with the annotation held to its established spareness, and did not ask him whether it was finished, because she knew the answer and he knew she knew it, and there was a particular economy between them now about the things that did not need to be re-asked. The sixth channel on her office repeater kept its station both nights, blank and attended, returning nothing and never alarming, and she read it by eye each morning along with the five that spoke, and Fenn at the noon reviews had arrived at a kind of armed patience about Room Four — he had stopped proposing the referral, she noticed, but he asked *any movement on Four?* each day in a tone that was holding the proposal in reserve, and she answered each day with the truth, which was no, and watched him decide each day to leave it another day. He was waiting for the case to resolve. She had stopped waiting for that some time ago, though she could not have put a date on when, and did not examine the change, having other things in the pile.

The answer to the records request came on the twelfth day, with the afternoon mail distribution, and it did not come from Carrow Bay.

It came in a windowed envelope of ordinary government stock, addressed to her correctly and completely, title and license enumeration and facility, and the return address was an office she had never heard of. The letterhead, when she opened it, said GOVERNMENT MEDICAL AUTHORITY across the top in the restrained type of an institution with no need to persuade, and under that, in smaller letters, *Boundary Medicine Division, Records Administration*. The letter was one page. It was dated two days after her submission, which meant it had been processed the day it arrived in that office, or possibly the same hour. It acknowledged receipt of her request of the stated date, reciting the request's own routing number back to her, correctly. It informed her that the records identified in her request were held under a medical authority classification restricting their release to treating physicians of record within active military medical jurisdiction. It observed that, as the patient's current treating physician of civilian record, she did not fall within that jurisdiction as defined, and that the records therefore could not be released to her under the current request. It thanked her for her inquiry. It advised her, in its final paragraph, that the release channel through which she had submitted was not the appropriate channel for records under this classification, and it provided, for use in any future correspondence regarding this matter, a reference: GMA-BM-2025-0441. There was no signature in the personal sense. There was a signature block that named the office again, and beneath it a line that said *for the Division*, and that was all.

She read it standing at the station where the mail had found her, and then she took it to her office and read it again sitting down, and the second reading confirmed the first: the letter was perfect. She knew the full range of the genre — the evasive, the hostile, the incompetent, the merely slow — and this was none of those. It was polite. It was correctly formatted. Every clause did exactly one job and no clause did more than it had to. It did not say the records existed. It did not say they didn't. It did not tell her what the classification was, or when it had been applied, or by whom, or what *active military medical jurisdiction* contained, or how a treating physician came to be inside it. It answered the question she had asked by describing, precisely, the shape of the door her question had struck, and it left every other question unasked on her behalf. She read it a third time looking for the sentence that overreached, the word that threatened, the tell — there was always a tell, some place where an institution's letter leaned harder than its content required — and there was no tell. Whoever wrote letters for this office wrote a great many of them.

She punched no holes in it. She took the physical chart from the rack, opened the front cover, and clipped the letter to the inside of it, where the transfer summary had ridden since the first day, so that the two documents faced each other — the packet that had arrived saying almost nothing, and the letter that had arrived to explain, courteously, why almost nothing was what she would continue to receive.

Then she sat with it, because that was the relation she had to this case, and by now she knew the posture well enough to take it up deliberately, like a position at an instrument. She did not call Orlan, though the impulse arrived on schedule and she logged it and let it pass; forwarding him this letter would spend his carefulness on her behalf without his consent. She did not call Carrow Bay. She did not go to Colm — not yet, and she examined the *not yet* and found it sound: Colm would process the letter as an administrative outcome, file-and-close, and Sable was not ready for it to be an outcome, because it was not one. It was an acquisition. She turned the desk lamp to the angle she used for reading her own handwriting and opened the private file and set down what the letter had actually told her, which was more than any document yet attached to this case.

The records existed. That was first. The letter had been careful not to say so, and the letter's care did not extend to her inferences: an office does not classify an absence, and a classification does not get a reference number unless there is an object under it. Somewhere, on a server or a shelf inside a jurisdiction she could not enter, nine weeks of imaging and coupling data and clinical narrative about the man in Room Four were being held — *held* was the letter's own verb, and it was the right one. Second: they were held by a government authority whose existence she had learned of this afternoon, an authority with a Boundary Medicine Division, which meant an authority whose remit was broad enough that Boundary Medicine was a subdivision of it. Third: she was outside the jurisdiction, and the letter had defined her position without apology and without malice, the way a chart defines a margin. And fourth — this was the one she circled around longest before writing it — there was a reference number. A reference number meant the classification was documented somewhere. Documented meant there was a system for this. A system meant procedure, precedent, staff, a letter template with the courteous clauses already in place. This had been done before, enough times that an office existed to do it well. And the number carried a year in its body, and the year was last year — which meant the classification had not been applied in response to her request, this week, by someone reacting to her. It had been sitting there, already assigned, since roughly the season Ruan Sela's monitor had logged its forty-one seconds. Her request had not caused anything. It had merely arrived at something, and the something had answered from a form file.

She wrote it into the private notes in the flat voice: *Records request (submitted day 10, routing receipt in chart) returned day 12 — not from Carrow Bay. Response from Government Medical Authority, Boundary Medicine Division, Records Administration. Classification restricts release to treating physicians of record in active military medical jurisdiction; this examiner outside jurisdiction as defined. Letter confirms neither existence nor non-existence of records; existence inferred (see above). Reference number: GMA-BM-2025-0441.* And then she did with the number what she had done with item 8 of the differential, the classification question she could not pursue and would not lose: she circled it, twice, the pen going around the figure slowly, and the circle meant what her circles always meant. Not now. Not never.

She went to Room Four in the early evening, after the dinner trays and before the shift change, having already decided what she would not say. She would not tell him about the letter. The decision had not resolved into a clean answer on the walk down the west corridor — she could not be certain the letter would help him, and could not be certain it would harm him, could not model what it would mean to a man eighteen months into being unexplained to learn that his explanation was being *held*, verb and all — and where she could not be certain she had learned, on this case above all cases, to keep the uncertainty intact rather than spend it. So she held it. But there were questions she could ask from her side of the held thing, and she asked the first one with the tablet closed on her lap, in the tone of a clinician tidying an intake history.

When he was at Carrow Bay, had he signed any releases?

"Many." He said it without needing to consult anything, which told her the signing had been an event with a texture, not a formality that had blurred. He had signed a great deal, he said, and much of it had not been about his care at all — a lot of what he signed concerned the research platform, the platform contractor, the incident investigation. Documents that arrived in stacks, with tabs where his signature went. He did not know what all of it covered. He said this last part plainly, the way he reported the limits of any instrument, including himself.

She asked who had conducted the incident investigation.

He was quiet for a moment — the checking pause, the one she had learned to wait through, his answer running against his logs before release. "They said it was a standard safety review. The platform contractor and a government safety office. I don't remember the exact name of the office."

She asked whether he remembered anything about the documentation he had signed.

"I remember a clause about medical records." He said it carefully, from outside, the way he said everything that touched the incident — a man reading an old entry aloud rather than a man remembering. He did not elaborate. She watched him not elaborate, and recognized the edge of vocabulary when it arrived, and did not push, because pushing was an instrument and it was not the right one, and because she already had what the evening was going to give her. She asked the ordinary closing questions instead, and got the ordinary answers, and the journal lay on the sill under the light-station book, current and reference, gaining what it gained. At the door she said good night, and he thanked her by name, and she was in the corridor before she let herself lay the pieces side by side, because she had not wanted to do the assembly in front of him with her face.

At the time, it had been documented as a safety review. A standard one — his word, or their word passed through him. The platform contractor and a government safety office had conducted it, and among the stacked documents with the signature tabs there had been a clause about medical records, and eighteen months later a government office was holding his medical records under a classification assigned, per its own reference number, in the season of the review. She did not know that the safety office and the Government Medical Authority were the same office, and she was careful with herself about that, the way she was careful about all her inferences; the government had many offices. But she did not believe the government kept its offices strangers to one another, and she found she could not construct an innocent version of the sequence that survived contact with its own dates: an incident reviewed for safety, a clause signed about medical records, a classification applied, a division standing by with a form letter for whichever civilian physician eventually came asking. It was not a coincidence. She held that conclusion at exactly its warranted strength — *not a coincidence* — and no further, because she did not know what it was instead, and the not-knowing was, as always on this case, the accurate position.

The building went into its evening configuration around her. She sat at the desk with the repeater showing five slow healthy channels and the sixth keeping its blank station, and she took the day's stock in the flat voice, and found that the ledger had changed in kind, not just in count. For eleven days everything she had acquired had been an absence with a texture: a null with structure, a packet with a shape like suppression, a silence from Carrow Bay eleven days long. Tonight, for the first time, something had arrived. Not the records — she was precise with herself about this — not one image, not one coupling value, not one sentence of Amroth's nine weeks. What had arrived was the structure she had until now only inferred, confirmed in writing, on letterhead, with a number. There was an authority. It had a division for exactly this. It had classified the records at the time of the incident, not in response to her, and it had been ready for her request before she made it — had been ready, evidently, for years, for anyone's request, with a template and a courteous clause and a reference format. The absence had a return address now. She looked at the physical chart on the desk, her own work entire, every entry hers, and at the front cover with the letter clipped inside it facing the transfer summary — the two most informative documents in the file, and neither of them containing a single clinical fact — and she thought that she had asked the system a question about her patient and the system had answered, fully, in its own language, and the answer was: *we know about this case, and you are not inside it.* She turned off the desk lamp. The ward made its night sound, trays done, the far elevator, the building breathing through its vents, and the sixth channel kept its station, blank and attended, and inside the front cover of the chart the letter kept its station too — polite, correctly formatted, perfectly ordinary, the first thing in twelve days that had answered her at all, and she sat in the dark a while with the shape of what it had answered, which was not the question she had asked, but was, she understood now, the case itself.

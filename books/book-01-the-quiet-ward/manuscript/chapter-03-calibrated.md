# Chapter 3 — Calibrated

The fog was in again at six, which meant the second day of Ruan Sela's admission began the way the first one had, with the water invisible and the ward making its early sound and Sable Hren at the station with coffee she had made too strong on purpose.

She had slept. She noted this the way she noted vitals — factually, because it was information. She had expected to lie awake with the four null returns arranged on the ceiling, and instead she had come up at five with the distinction waiting for her, patient, exactly where she had left it. Zero is a measurement. Null is the refusal of one. It had not developed overnight. It had not degraded either. It was the rare kind of problem that did not require her anxiety to maintain itself.

She reviewed the night notes. Ruan had slept five hours and four minutes, waking at 3:47, which the night nurse had charted without being asked because Osei had flagged the chart and the flag had propagated. He had not called for anything. He had asked the nurse, in the morning, whether the fog was in, and been told it was, and said good, he liked the sound the ward made in fog. The nurse had written this down too. Sable read it twice and charted nothing, because there was no field on the form for a patient who was learning the building.

At 7:40 he ate breakfast. At 8:15 she went to Room Four and started the BM-5 coupling series herself.

She could have delegated it. BM-5 was resident work by any reasonable standard — passive, procedural, four hours of watching a known compound do a known thing. She did not delegate it. She told herself it was because the first coupling data on a null-field patient should be acquired by the physician of record, which was true, and was also not the whole reason, and she declined to itemize the rest.

The protocol was old and it was good. You introduced a compound whose behavior at the cellular boundary was characterized to the fourth decimal — a reference agent, chosen for being boring — at a dose calculated from body mass and nothing else. Then you watched it. Serial draws, timed to the protocol clock. The assay did not look at the patient. That was the thing about BM-5 that she had never had occasion to think about until this morning: the assay looked at the compound. It measured what came back in the samples — how much had crossed, how fast, into which compartments, in what order — and from the compound's itinerary you reconstructed what the boundaries it had crossed were doing. The patient appeared in the data only as geography. As the country the traveler had passed through.

Ruan held his arm still for the line and thanked her, and asked one question, which was whether the draw schedule was on the quarter hour or the protocol's own clock, because if it was the protocol clock he would stop watching the wall clock and stop anticipating. Protocol clock, she said. He nodded and picked a point on the ceiling and became, for the next four hours, the stillest patient she had ever run a series on.

The first draw came back unremarkable.

So did the second. Uptake within reference range. Distribution pattern textbook — she had taught from curves less clean than the ones assembling themselves on her tablet. The reference agent crossed his cellular boundaries at the rate the tables said it should, partitioned where the tables said it would, and began its orderly exit on schedule, and none of it required a single annotation.

She ran the second compound after the washout. Then the third, the shortest of the series, chosen to characterize a different transport pathway. The fog outside the window thinned and re-thickened twice. A cart went by in the corridor at intervals, and each time Ruan's eyes moved slightly toward the sound, tracking the ward, and came back to his point on the ceiling.

By one o'clock she had what she had come for, and it was nothing.

Nothing in the good sense. The sense she had spent twelve years learning to find restful, and could not, today, find restful at all. His pharmacology was unremarkable. His cellular boundaries did with medication exactly what cellular boundaries did — coupled, transported, discriminated, in reference range, on reference time. If she had received the curves blinded, with no name on them, she would have signed them off as a healthy adult male and thought no more about them.

She sat with the tablet for a moment after the last draw, in the chair by the window, while the nurse took the line out.

Every instrument that looked at *what passed through him* saw everything in order. Every instrument that looked at *him* saw nothing at all.

She noticed herself building the sentence. She noticed that it was a good sentence, that it had the compression of a finding, and she took it apart deliberately, because it was not a finding, it was a description of the shape of her ignorance, and she had watched too many clinicians frame the shape of their ignorance well and then mistake the frame for a diagnosis. What she had was narrower than the sentence. The null was not a global failure of his boundary function. His boundaries functioned. The null was specific to the instruments built to read Resonance field state, and she did not know what that specificity meant, and *I don't know* went in the chart in its own vocabulary, later, in the proper field.

"The coupling data is clean so far," she told Ruan, because he was owed the interim the way he had been owed the nulls. "Fully in range. I'll have the composite this afternoon."

"In range," he repeated. He said it the way a man handles a tool he has not been permitted to hold in a while. "That's not a word I've heard about myself recently."

"It applies to your pharmacology."

"I know what it applies to." He said it mildly, without correction in it. "Thank you for saying it anyway."

She made the call at two, from the small consult room off the imaging bay, with the four null studies open on the terminal, the empty-room acquisition beside them, and her calibration log beside that, because she intended to be asked and intended to have already answered.

The manufacturer of the BM-1 array kept a technical line for practitioners. She had used it four times in twelve years: twice for software, once for a boom actuator, once for a reference puck that had drifted out of certification. The line was answered by a technician who gave her name as Maret, and who had, she said when Sable asked — because Sable had learned to ask, it told you what kind of conversation you were about to have — eight years on the clinical support desk.

Eight years was good. Eight years meant she would not be read a script.

Sable laid it out the way she would have presented at rounds. A thirty-four-year-old male, ambulatory, cooperative, no acute distress. BM-1 passive acquisition run twice on an array calibrated that morning, reference puck within a tenth of a percent of certified values, calibration logged and timestamped. Both acquisitions completed cleanly — full run, completion chime, file closed. No study generated. No artifact flag, no out-of-range warning, no anomaly banner. Status line reading *acquisition complete*, result field empty. Then the control: the same array, swung to the unoccupied half of the room, returning the ambient render immediately and normally. Then BM-2 differential on separate hardware — reference lattice read perfectly, no differential result. Then BM-3. Then BM-4. She gave the facility's history, which was that in twelve years it had never had an array failure, and she gave the timestamps, and then she stopped and let the line be quiet.

Maret asked her to hold, not in the tone of someone escalating, in the tone of someone pulling documentation. She was gone for a little under two minutes. When she came back she walked Sable through the diagnostic tree out loud, not as instruction — as verification, each branch stated and closed. Calibration: verified, logged. Acquisition integrity: files closed clean, checksums would confirm, Sable could send them, Sable said she would. Environmental interference: excluded by the ambient acquisition, which had rendered. Software fault: excluded by the same, and by four platforms, and Maret paused there, briefly, on the four platforms, and Sable heard her arrive at the place the tree ran out.

"Dr. Hren, I want to make sure I'm restating this correctly," Maret said. "The instruments completed acquisition and returned no study. Not a dark render. Not a zero-value study. No study."

"Correct."

"And the room reads. The bedding reads. The air reads."

"Everything reads except the patient."

There was a pause that was not a hold. Sable knew the difference. A hold had keys in it, breathing directed elsewhere. This was a person sitting with something.

"Then the instruments are functioning correctly," Maret said. "Everything you've described is the array doing exactly what it's designed to do. Which means —" and Sable heard her choose to finish the sentence, heard the small professional decision get made — "what you're describing is a patient with no measurable field state."

"Yes," Sable said. "That's what I'm describing. What would cause that?"

She asked it the way she would have asked what would cause a fever. It was, as far as she could construct it, exactly that kind of question. It had an exactly-that-kind-of-question shape right up until the moment it was spoken to the person whose job for eight years had been the complete space of things these instruments did, and Sable listened to it land in that space and touch nothing.

"I don't know," Maret said. "We've never seen that before."

She did not say it the way people apologize. She said it the way Sable said it — as a report, delivered from the far edge of what could be honestly claimed, with nothing decorating it. Eight years of this desk. Every failure mode the platform had, cataloged, reproducible, in the documentation. And the documentation had no page, because the instrument had not failed.

"I'd like to send you the acquisition files," Sable said. "For the engineering group. Not because I think they'll find a fault."

"Send them," Maret said. "I'll open a reference case. Dr. Hren — I want to be accurate with you. A reference case means we've recorded that it happened. I don't have a category to put it in."

"No," Sable said. "Neither do I. That's the finding."

She thanked her, and Maret thanked her back, oddly, the way people thank each other at the edge of a map, for company more than for content, and Sable sat a moment after the line closed with the four studies that were not studies, and then went to find Fenn, because there was one more thing rigor demanded and it required a cart.

The main hospital had what the annex did not, which was other people's purchasing decisions. The cardiology wing ran field-state imaging on a platform from the northern manufacturer, an entirely different engineering lineage — different lattice geometry, different acquisition philosophy, a machine that failed loudly and often in ways the technicians there described with real affection. The surgical suite kept a compact differential unit from a third maker, the kind of stripped-down instrument that had four buttons and no opinions. Sable made two calls, said the word *cross-validation*, which was true and which opened doors, and sent Fenn across the corridor connection with a cart and the transfer paperwork.

He came back forty minutes later with both instruments and the particular energy of a man who had enjoyed being asked technical questions by other departments' technicians. He set up the northern manufacturer's imager at the foot of Ruan's bed while Sable verified its field calibration against its own reference standard, and Ruan lay back and put his arms inside the margin without being asked, and said, "Different machine," in the tone of a man noting a new make of transport at a familiar stop.

"Different manufacturer," Sable said. "Different design entirely."

"All right," Ruan said, and looked at the ceiling.

The northern machine ran its acquisition with a sound the BM-1 array did not make, a low interrupted ticking, and completed, and displayed, in its own vocabulary and its own interface and its own engineering accent, nothing. No study. Its error register, which the cardiology technicians had promised was hair-triggered, logged no error.

The compact differential unit read its reference. It read the room. Aimed at Ruan, it returned no differential result, and its four buttons offered nothing further.

Fenn had gone quiet after the northern machine. He ran the third instrument — the surgical unit — himself, at Sable's nod, and she watched him do it properly, no shortcuts, the empty-field control first this time, unprompted, establishing the machine could see the room before asking it to see the man. It saw the room.

It did not see the man.

Fenn stood at the cart with the third result. He did not say anything. Yesterday he had needed to say something — she had watched the pressure build and had read the gauge and had let him vent the tidy explanation, the flat zero, the floor. Today he looked at the small stubborn screen of a machine built by people who had never spoken to the people who built the other machines, and his hand came up and rubbed the back of his neck, and he looked over at Ruan, who was watching the ceiling with his arms inside a margin that no longer had an instrument that wanted it, and Fenn looked back at the screen, and wrote down the timestamp.

"I'll get these back to their departments," he said finally. That was all. He was very careful wheeling the cart out the door.

Ruan turned his head on the pillow.

"Three more," he said. Not a question.

"Three more," Sable said. "Different makers. I wanted to rule out a shared design fault."

"And now you have."

"Now I have."

He nodded slowly, taking it the way he took everything, as information about a procedure. But he held her eye a moment longer than the exchange needed, the way he had once yesterday, and she had the impression — filed, not examined — that being tested by someone who told him afterward what the test had been for was not, in his recent experience, standard practice.

She wrote the chart note at four o'clock, at the station, in the fifteen minutes she protected for charting because notes written on the move were notes written badly.

Yesterday's note had been description. *Null return on all four — no study generated. Distinct from zero-value finding.* Accurate, technical, committed to nothing. Today's note had to do the other job a chart did, which was to say what the findings meant for the care of the patient, and she sat with the cursor in the assessment field for longer than she would have wanted anyone to see.

She had a precise vocabulary for this field and she used all of it in her head before writing. *Negative* meant tested and absent. *Normal* meant tested and in range — his coupling series had earned that word and she gave it, in its own line, without comment. *Nondiagnostic* meant the study was technically inadequate, and it would have been a lie; the studies were technically perfect. What remained was the word for data that was internally consistent and externally inexplicable, the word that meant *the tests functioned and I cannot move from them to a diagnosis in either direction.*

*Field-state assessment: inconclusive across seven acquisitions on six platforms from four manufacturers. Instrument function verified in all cases by ambient control. Manufacturer technical consultation completed; instruments confirmed operating within specification; reference case opened. No field-state finding can be ruled in. No field-state finding can be ruled out.*

She read it back. It was accurate. It was complete. It was the loneliest sentence she had written in twelve years of clinical practice, and she signed it, and noticed that she had thought of it in exactly those terms, and did not chart that either.

Then she went to her office and closed the door.

The office was small. It faced the parking structure, and she had occupied it for nine years and never asked for a better one, because a better one faced the water and she would have looked at the water. She sat down in the chair, not at the desk. The desk was for work and she was not going to pretend this was work.

The telemetry repeater sat on the shelf where it always sat, carrying her six passive channels, the license's full allowance. Five of them were the ward: bed six's pain modulation holding on the schedule she'd rewritten two mornings ago; a post-procedure patient in the east wing settling; three others, quiet. The sixth channel was Room Four. It had been on the list since this morning, because he was her patient and her license monitored her patients, and it displayed what it had displayed all through the coupling series while the assay curves came back clean, what it would display tomorrow.

Not a flat trace. A flat trace was a rendering, a line drawn at zero by an instrument reporting a floor. The channel was simply unpopulated. Five traces and a blank, and the blank was not dark the way an off channel was dark. It was the interface's nothing. The nothing that was not zero.

She sat.

She did not pace, because pacing was for problems that could be walked around, and she had spent two days establishing with instrument-grade thoroughness that this one had no other side to walk to. She did not call anyone; a consult was a question, and she did not have the question yet, only its raw material. She did not draft the theory, because there was no theory, and the vocabulary a theory would need was not on any shelf she had access to, and she had checked the shelves. All of them. That was what the day had been.

The fog had come back up, gray against the parking structure. A car left. Another arrived. The ward's sound came through the door faintly — carts, soft shoes, the station tone.

She held it. That was the whole of what she did, and she did it for a long time. Not despair; she checked, in the flat clinical way she audited herself, and it wasn't. Not resolution either. It was the thing she had never learned from any instructor and had assembled alone out of the wreckage of the one case she did not think about in this chair: the practice of being in a room with something she had no words for, without reaching for the nearest wrong ones, and waiting — however long the waiting ran — for the right ones to exist.

The five traces breathed on the repeater. The sixth channel held its nothing steadily, the way he held everything steadily.

At a quarter to six she stood up, and it was only when she was already in the corridor that she admitted where she was going.

There was no clinical reason. The coupling composite was logged. The cross-platform nulls were logged with their timestamps, and the borrowed instruments were back in their departments, and the chart said *inconclusive* and would go on saying it. She had no test to bring him and no result he had not already been given. What she had was a patient who thanked his transport driver by name, and asked to hear the ward, and arranged his meals around the cleanliness of her data, and called himself a well-run procedure with a satisfaction she had found harder to watch than grief — and a distinction she could not file that was, whatever else it was, *about him*. It seemed to her that a thing that was about a man belonged, in some way no chart had a field for, to the man. And she found she wanted to know whether it troubled him the way it troubled her. She suspected she already knew, and went to see anyway, which was, she reflected, the entire method of her profession reduced to a corridor.

The door of Room Four stood open a hand's width, as it had all day.

He was reading. A physical book, the kind with pages, a worn paperback that must have come out of the soft-sided bag at last — she registered that the bag now stood open on the windowsill, that some small settlement had occurred in the room since morning. The window had gone gray-blue with the last of the light behind the fog. He looked up when she came in, and put a finger in the book, and did not close it.

"The coupling composite is finished," she said. "Everything I tested. Normal coupling, normal uptake, normal distribution. Your body handles medication exactly the way it should. There's nothing remarkable in any of it."

He looked at her for a moment.

"Thank you," he said. "You didn't have to come back to tell me that."

"I didn't," she said.

He held her eye a second longer, and then he nodded, once, the way he nodded at protocol clocks and correct procedure, except that it was not that nod, quite, and neither of them named what it was instead. He went back to his book.

She stopped at the door on her way out and adjusted it to a hand's width exactly, and left it so, and went down the west corridor through the sound of the ward at evening, past the imaging bay, past the station, carrying the nothing that was not zero, and holding it still.

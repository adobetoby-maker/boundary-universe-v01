# Chapter 17 — Colm's Question

The twenty-fourth morning came in from the west like the others, fog first and the light behind it, and Sable Hren took the handoff, read the six telemetry channels by eye — five doing their small honest biological work, the sixth returning its faithful nothing — and went to her office before rounds, which was not her habit, and opened the top drawer of her desk, which had become one.

The extension request was four pages long and it was good. Sable knew it was good the way she knew a clean acquisition when she saw one — not by admiring it but by failing to find the flaw she was looking for, and she had looked for the flaw on four separate mornings now, of which this was the fourth. Day twenty-four. The authorization that had arrived with Ruan Sela — thirty days, pre-approved, unusually clean, Colm's word from the first morning — had six days left on it, and Sable had been counting them the way she counted everything on this case, quietly, in the private ledger, without letting the count leak into her clinical manner. The request sat in the top drawer of her desk in a plain folder, printed, because she had drafted it on the hospital system and then removed it from the hospital system, which was itself a fact about where this case had taken her, and one she had noted without yet deciding what it weighed.

The grounds were real. That was the thing about the request that made it good and also made it dangerous, because a fabricated case for extension would have been flimsy and a real one was load-bearing, and load-bearing documents get read. Unresolved diagnostic presentation: true, and stated in the language the insurance authority's reviewers were trained to receive — a presentation confirmed as non-artifactual across seven instruments and two measurement principles, stable across twenty-three days, without documented precedent in the civilian literature. Active investigation: true. Clinical progress: true, and she had written that section most carefully of all, because progress on this case did not look like progress on any case the reviewers had ever funded. She had not resolved the presentation. What she had done was build a complete negative map of it — what his field state did not do, which was couple with any certified instrument; what it did do, which was persist, organized and consistent, while the man inside it slept better, ate more, and read a book about a light-station keeper at the rate of a man with time; and what the boundary of the map was, which she could now draw with confidence even though she could not name what lay on the other side of the line. Eighteen years of clinical documentation had taught her how to make an honest thing persuasive. The request was both.

And it was still in the drawer, because of the routing block on page four, the one every extension request carried, the one nobody read: copies of extension determinations for cases involving government-classified records to be furnished to the classifying authority. She had read it four times on four mornings. Filed, the request would go to the insurance authority, and a copy of the determination would go to an office with a Boundary Medicine Division and a reference number ending in 0441, and someone in that office would learn — not by investigation, just by the ordinary metabolism of paperwork — that the treating physician at Pacific Meridian was asking for more time. She did not know what that office did with such information. That was the whole problem, stated at its actual size: not that she knew the risk, but that she could not compute it. Two prior cases, closed within six weeks, outcomes unknown. A request for more time was also, read from the other side, a signal that time was being used.

Four days she had sat on it. She was aware — the part of her that audited her own charting was aware — that sitting on it had begun as deliberation and was now something closer to holding her breath, and that six days was still enough time to file and be processed, and that five days was probably enough, and that four was not, and that she was therefore within one day of the decision making itself, which was the one way she had promised herself she would not make it.

She looked at the folder for the length of one unhurried breath, and put it back in the drawer, and closed the drawer, and went to rounds.

---

Rounds were rounds. Bed two's step-down was holding; the new admission in bed nine was responding to a coupling schedule Osei had flagged and adjusted before Sable had finished asking him to; the ward did its morning work with the low competence that never made it into anyone's documentation because competence, sustained, is invisible. She was coming out of the east wing with the tablet under her arm when Administrator Colm fell into step beside her, and Sable knew before a word was said what the subject would be, because Colm did not wander, and because of where they were. Colm's office was a place appointments happened. The corridor was where Colm conducted the conversations she had decided, out of courtesy, not to make into appointments.

"I've been looking at the thirty-day board," Colm said, warmly, at the pace of the corridor. "I was hoping I could catch you for a few minutes. Nothing formal."

"Now is fine," Sable said.

They went to Colm's office, which was small and deliberately so, and had a window with the fog still in it, and two chairs that were the same height, which Sable had noticed years ago and had always taken as the truest available statement of how Colm understood her work. Colm closed the door — not conspiratorially; she closed it the way one closes a door on a conversation that deserves not to compete with a corridor — and sat, and did not put the desk between them.

"Is there anything you can tell me about Room Four?" she said. "I want to understand where the case is. Not for the board — for me." She let that sit for a moment, and then she said, with no change of register at all: "I've seen this pattern before, and I know you know I have."

She did not elaborate. She had never elaborated. On the first morning, before Ruan Sela had ever come through the doors, she had glanced at a tablet she did not consult and said *we've handled those before*, comfortably, and Sable had filed the sentence at the time as institutional reassurance and had spent the weeks since slowly re-weighing it, because Colm's reassurances were never empty; they were drawn on an account, and the account was two decades of this building, and somewhere in that account was a case that had looked like this one — sealed records, a government authority, a bed occupied past its category — and Colm had watched how it was handled. That was everything Sable knew about it, and Colm, sitting across from her now with her hands at rest, was offering the fact's existence and nothing else, the way you lay a single card face up to show the other player what kind of game you understand yourselves to be in.

"I'll tell you what I can," Sable said, "and it will be true, and it won't be everything, and I'd rather say that to you than have you find the seams yourself."

"That's the arrangement I was hoping for," Colm said.

---

So she told her. The diagnostic workup was complete — complete in the working sense, the sense a clinician meant it: every instrument available to this facility had been run, some of them several times, three of them borrowed from other manufacturers to rule out platform artifact, and the picture that emerged was clear in its shape even though the classification remained unresolved. The null was consistent and confirmed. It was not a reading error. Twenty-three days of it, unbroken, across every certified modality, with normal empty-room acquisitions bracketing every run: the instruments worked; the patient's state was simply outside what they were built to characterize. She said all of this in the flat clinical voice, and it cost her nothing, because every word of it was in the chart already and every word of it was true.

What she did not say, she did not say. The letter clipped inside the front cover of the physical chart. The index entry, four sentences, that she had walked backward into through the public classification system. The two suffix codes and the third one that did not exist yet except in her own handwriting. Orlan's telephone voice saying *be careful*. She had rehearsed no version of this conversation in which those things appeared, and none of them appeared, and she watched herself not saying them and found the not-saying clean — not deception, sequence. Colm ran an institution. Facts given to an institution enter its metabolism; that was not a criticism of Colm, it was the definition of her job, and it was precisely why the arrangement Sable had proposed at the door — true, not everything, said plainly — was the only honest one available.

"He is stable," she said. "The presentation is unusual. I'm not ready to discharge him with an unresolved picture."

Colm nodded slowly, taking that in the way she took in staffing numbers, as a real quantity. "And the timeline," she said. "You know it better than I do, I suspect."

"Six days," Sable said. "I've been counting. I have a plan for the extension."

"What kind of plan?"

"I'm preparing the request. I expect to file it this week."

Colm was quiet for a moment, and it was a working quiet — Sable could very nearly see the form the administrator was filling in behind her eyes, because Sable had read the same form. "The insurance authority will want a clinical milestone," Colm said. "They fund motion, not occupancy. Progress, or a specific investigation still pending. Which are you filing it on?"

"I have a specific investigation still pending," Sable said.

It was true. It was true in the way the whole conversation had been true, which was completely and not exhaustively. There was an investigation pending. It had a subject and a method and an open question at its center, and she could not have named it aloud in this office without carrying the rest of the case in behind it — the letter, the index, the sealed files — and so she did not name it, and Colm, who had spent twenty years learning exactly where the boundary of her own need-to-know sat and how to sit comfortably against it, did not ask her to.

"All right," Colm said. "I trust your judgment." She said it simply, the way she said most consequential things, and then she leaned forward slightly, and her voice did not harden — Sable listened for the hardening and it was not there — but it took on the particular clarity Colm reserved for the sentences she considered it her duty to say early rather than late. "I want you to know one thing before it happens rather than after, because that's the only time knowing it is useful. If the extension is denied, we will have to make other arrangements for the bed. Not immediately. But within the week of a denial. That's not me — that's the machinery, and I'll be the one operating it, and I would rather you had time to plan than be surprised by me. I don't like surprising people I trust."

"I understand," Sable said. And then, because it was true and because Colm had earned the saying of it: "Thank you for telling me now."

"That's the job," Colm said, and stood, and the conversation was over, and it had contained no menace anywhere in it, which Sable noted precisely because she had checked. Two people who respected each other had sat in two chairs of the same height and described the same twenty-three days from inside two different problems. Colm's problem had beds and dates in it. Hers had a man in it. Both problems were real. That was the whole of what had happened in this office, and it was not small.

---

The corridor outside Colm's office ran toward the annex, and Sable walked it slower than her ordinary pace, which she noticed and permitted.

What Colm had said, she had said. What Colm had not said was the thing Sable found herself turning over at the junction where the corridor's light changed. *I've seen this pattern before, and I know you know I have.* Fourteen words, laid down and left. Colm had not described the prior case. She had not dated it, or named its physician, or said what other arrangements had eventually meant, that time. She had simply established that the fact existed and that both of them knew it existed — which was, Sable understood, an offer of a kind, or at least the visible edge of one. A door left open a hand's width, if she wanted the figure, and she did not particularly want the figure, but there it was.

She had not asked. She examined the not-asking, walking, because it deserved examination. It was not timidity. It was the same discipline that had kept her from calling the records office, from querying the licensing bureau a second time, from turning any of the case's sealed surfaces into a trail: every question she asked aloud in this building was an event, and events involving this case had a way of being copied to offices she could not see into. A question to Colm about the prior case would be the safest question she had yet considered — Colm was not a records system; Colm was a person, and a careful one — and even so it would change something. It would tell Colm that Sable was investigating backward as well as forward. It would spend Colm's card, the one laid face up, and Sable did not yet know what game the card would be spent into.

But the fact was in the room now. Colm had put it there deliberately, and it would not leave. Somewhere in this building's institutional memory was a case that rhymed with Room Four, and one person who had watched it from start to whatever its finish had been, and that person had today, unprompted, told Sable she remembered. Sable did with the fact what she had done with the reference number and with item eight of the differential and with everything else this case had handed her that she could not yet lift at its true weight: she circled it. Not on paper — she was walking — but the gesture was the same and she felt it complete itself. Not now. Not never.

What the conversation had actually done — she saw this at the annex junction, where the ventilation carried its low institutional note and the day's ordinary traffic moved around her — was make the clock audible again. It had been running the whole time; she had been counting its days in the private ledger every morning; but there is a difference between a clock counted and a clock heard, and for three weeks the case had been allowed to proceed at the case's own tempo, which was the tempo of fog, of a shape emerging by acquisitions, of a man learning to sleep. That allowance had never been official. It had been an artifact of nobody asking. Now somebody had asked — kindly, correctly, with a week's warning attached like a courtesy — and the ward's real arithmetic stood in the corridor with her: beds, dates, a thirty-day board with one line on it that had stopped fitting the board's assumptions twenty-three days ago. She did not resent the arithmetic. She had run wards on it her whole career. She simply noted that from this morning forward the case would be worked inside earshot of it, and that the folder in her drawer had just become the only instrument she had that could buy the tempo back.

She did not go back into the office. She went to the ward, and the morning went on being a morning.

---

At the noon review Fenn came through the material at his usual speed, which was brisk and, these days, almost entirely accurate — bed nine's adjustment holding, bed two ready to step down another level if the afternoon numbers agreed, a supply note about lattice plates that he had already resolved and was reporting only for completeness. Then, at the end, in the tone of a man asking about weather he had asked about every day for three weeks:

"Any movement on Four?"

"There's going to be an extension request," Sable said.

Fenn absorbed this. She watched him absorb it, and she could see the shape of everything he was not saying — the timeline arithmetic he had certainly already done, the question about grounds, the resident's reflexive urge to offer the literature one more time — and she watched him decline all of it, which was a thing he could not have done three weeks ago.

"Okay," he said.

And then, after a beat, in a voice with nothing performed in it: "Is there anything you want me to do?"

"Keep doing exactly what you're doing," she said.

"Okay," Fenn said again, and gathered his tablet, and went to see about bed two, and that was the whole conversation. She sat for a moment after he had gone and noted, in the interior ledger where she kept her accounts of him, that it had been worth more than a longer one would have been — that *is there anything you want me to do* was the sentence of a clinician who had located himself correctly with respect to a case too large for him and had decided to be excellent at the part that was his. She had spent three weeks being harder on Fenn Ash than she should have been, for reasons that were about her own residency and not about him, and she made a note in the same ledger, not for the first time, to tell him one day what she actually thought. Not now. But this one was not filed under *not never*.

---

The afternoon spent itself the way ward afternoons did. Bed two stepped down on schedule; the numbers agreed with Fenn and Fenn, for once, did not double-check their agreement. A discharge went out of the east wing with the particular brief lightness discharges put into a corridor. Osei caught her at the medication station to confirm a substitution she would have approved without being asked and asked anyway, because he was Osei. None of it concerned Room Four, and she let none of it be shadowed by Room Four, because a ward is a working instrument and the case was one channel of six, and keeping it one channel of six was, she had come to understand, part of how this case stayed workable at all.

She went to Room Four in the evening, after the dinner trays, in the hour that had gradually become theirs without either of them designating it.

The door was open a hand's width. Ruan was in the chair by the window with the field journal open on his knee — open, which she registered from the doorway, because the journal's resting state was closed with the pencil squared on the cover, and the pencil was not in his hand. It was on the sill. He was not drawing. He was looking at the page in the manner of a man reading an instrument he had built himself and did not entirely trust yet.

She asked what he was doing with it.

"Not drawing," he said. "Looking at what I've already drawn." He turned the journal a few degrees, not showing her exactly, more adjusting it under his own eye. "I've been thinking about the shape. About what it would take to describe it in a way that would mean something to someone who couldn't see it."

She sat down in the chair she sat in. "I have all five acquisitions," she said.

"I know." He said it without any freight; the scanner was part of their arrangement, and he trusted the acquisition — he had told her so a dozen ways in his own dialect. "But the acquisitions are flat. And the thing I'm looking at isn't flat in the way they are." He paused, and it was one of his verification pauses; she waited through it. "There are dimensions to it that the scanner can't capture. Not because it's too big. Because the scanner takes a picture of a surface, and this doesn't have a surface the way the scanner means."

She thought about that. She thought about it the way she had learned to think in this room, which was slowly and without reaching for the chart, and the analogy that came was small and clinical and she offered it as a question.

"Like a temperature reading," she said.

He considered it with the unhurried literalness he brought to everything, and she watched him check it against the thing on his page the way he checked a torque before trusting it.

"Yes," he said. "Exactly like that. A temperature reading gives you one number where there are actually many things happening."

The fog had come back to the window with the evening, and behind it the water was ending its day of being visible. She did not take out the notebook. It was in her pocket, and it stayed in her pocket, because some readings are acquired and some are held, and she had gotten better, in twenty-four days, at knowing which was which. She held it.

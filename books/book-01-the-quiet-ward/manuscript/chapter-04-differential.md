# Chapter 4 — Differential

The third morning there was no fog, which Sable registered as a small wrongness before she registered it as weather. The water was visible from the station, gray and flat and unremarkable, and the ward made its early sound without the muffling the fog gave it, a degree sharper, a degree closer. She had arrived at six with a notepad.

The notepad was not new. It was the kind she bought in boxes of twelve, narrow-ruled, cardboard-backed, and she had gone through some hundreds of them in twelve years because she did her differentials on paper. Not from sentiment. A differential built on a screen invited the screen's habits — collapsing, sorting, auto-completing, tidying the work before the work was done. Paper held everything at once and in her own hand, including the crossings-out, and the crossings-out were the record of the reasoning. A differential was not a list of possibilities. It was a list of executions, each one dated and signed.

The method was the oldest one in medicine and Boundary Medicine had inherited it whole, because there was nothing to improve. You wrote down everything that could produce the observed finding. Everything, including the embarrassing things, including the things you were sure of, because sureness was a feeling and the page did not record feelings. Then you took each candidate and held it against the data, and you eliminated what the data excluded, and you kept what it could not exclude, and at the end you had not an answer but a smaller country in which the answer lived. She had taught the method to eleven years of residents with the same sentence every year: *the differential is where you find out whether you are practicing medicine or having opinions.*

She sat at her desk with the door open, because closing it before seven signaled something to the ward that she did not want signaled, and she wrote at the top of the first page: *R.S. Finding: null return, field-state instrumentation, 7 acquisitions, all platforms. Coupling normal. Ambient controls normal.* Below that she wrote the numeral one, and began.

*One. Iatrogenic field disruption.* Prior treatment artifact — the possibility that Carrow Bay, nine weeks of it, competent and meticulous, had done something to him that suppressed his field signature as a side effect. It was the first thing any reviewer would ask, and it was the reason she had run the coupling series before starting the differential rather than after. Iatrogenic disruption was not a mystery state; it was pharmacology wearing a costume. Every documented agent that flattened field readout did so by loading the cellular boundary, and a loaded boundary announced itself in coupling data the way a limp announced itself in a gait — altered uptake, skewed partition, a transport signature you could not hide because the compounds did not know they were being watched. His coupling was textbook. She had the curves. There was no pharmacological signature because there was no pharmacology; the admission med list she had built herself contained nothing with a field-coupling profile, and Carrow Bay's discharge list, thin as it was, contained nothing either. She drew a line through *one* and wrote the reason in the margin, dated it, and felt nothing, because the first eliminations were supposed to feel like nothing. They were the brush being cleared.

*Two. Environmental Resonance contamination.* Exposure to damaged or malfunctioning Resonant materials — the industrial presentation, the one that showed up in dockworkers and salvage crews and, occasionally, in researchers whose containment had failed quietly for months. She gave it more time than it deserved, because his occupation invited it: a Resonance field technician handled Resonant materials for a living. But contamination was loud in exactly the places his workup was quiet. It carried a biomarker profile — a specific inflammatory cascade, boundary-membrane stress products, measurable in ordinary blood work, present for months after exposure — and his admission labs were clean, and Carrow Bay's labs, run at intake nine weeks ago, had been clean too; it was one of the few things the transfer packet actually contained. And the history excluded it independently. A civilian research platform ran controlled environments, logged handling, monitored dosimetry, because the insurers required it, and his file held nine weeks of nobody suggesting contamination, which from a meticulous facility meant the labs had been looked at by people who knew what contamination looked like. Also — she wrote this last, because it was the decisive part — contamination degraded readings. It smeared them, shifted them, buried them in artifact. It did not delete them. A contaminated patient read badly. He did not read at all. Line through *two*.

*Three. Standard field suppression, self-induced.* Some Standards with advanced training could reduce their own field signature — temporarily, effortfully, and never completely; the certified suppression tiers topped out at partial attenuation, and the certification existed precisely because the capability was rare enough to license. She pulled his licensing record onto the tablet, the one document in his file that was ordinary, and read it again. Field technician. Standard Three. Certification history: standard technical endorsements, materials-handling ratings, transit clearances. No suppression tier. No advanced field-discipline coursework of any kind. A Standard Three with no suppression training could not attenuate his own signature by a measurable fraction, let alone hold a total suppression continuously for — she checked her own timestamps — fifty-one hours across seven acquisitions, through sleep, through a four-hour coupling series during which he had lain still and watched the ceiling. The capability was not in his file, and the capability at that magnitude was not in anyone's file; it was not in the literature. She lined it out. Then she sat for a moment with her pen off the page, because *three* had brushed against something on the way down, and she wanted to see what it was before it got away. It was the licensing record. *Standard Three.* She left the thought where it was — it belonged further down the list, and a differential worked in order or it did not work — and wrote the numeral four.

*Four* and *five* she wrote together, because they were siblings: acute dissociation syndrome, class I and class II. The trauma-response form, reversible, and the injury-pattern form, which was not. This was his admitting diagnosis, or the neighborhood of it — Carrow Bay had written *acute Resonance dissociation syndrome* on the transfer and provided nothing that supported the words. She could not eliminate either class, and she wrote down why, because *cannot eliminate* required a reason the same as elimination did. Both classes were defined longitudinally. They were diagnoses of trajectory — a documented field baseline, a documented departure from it, a characteristic evolution over weeks. She had no baseline. She had no trajectory. She had seven acquisitions of nothing and no prior field data of any kind, because the records that would contain it had gone somewhere that was not here. Without the longitudinal series the dissociation syndromes could be neither ruled in nor ruled out; they simply floated, unfalsifiable, which was the diagnostic equivalent of unemployed. She marked them both *open — pending longitudinal data* and moved on, and did not let herself resent the missing records, because resentment was also not recorded on the page.

*Six. Field-state artifact* — measurement interference from an external source, some emitter in the building, some resonant structure in the walls, producing a null the way a bright light produced a blind spot. This one the week had already killed, and thoroughly, and she wrote its obituary with something close to satisfaction because it was the elimination she had bought with the most labor. Interference was hardware-specific. Different lattice geometries, different acquisition philosophies, different manufacturers' different opinions about shielding — each platform had its own susceptibilities, documented in its own service literature, and no interference source in the literature touched all of them, because they did not listen the same way. The northern manufacturer's imager and the four-button surgical unit and her own BM-1 array agreed about almost nothing at the engineering level. They agreed about him. Three manufacturers, one null. And the ambient controls sat on top of that like a headstone: every instrument, aimed at the empty half of the same room, in the same building, inside the same hypothetical interference field, had rendered the room without complaint. An artifact that affected every technology identically and only within the volume of one specific patient was not an artifact. It was a finding wearing an artifact's name. Line.

*Seven. Equipment calibration failure* — her own. She eliminated it in four lines, citing the calibration log, the reference puck at a tenth of a percent, the timestamps, the empty-room acquisitions, and Maret's diagnostic tree, closed branch by branch on a recorded technical line. She was the least reliable witness on this item and so she cited nothing that came from her judgment, only from instruments checking instruments. Line.

*Eight,* she wrote. *Diagnostic misclassification.*

Meaning: the file was wrong about him. Meaning: *Standard Three* was an error, a transposition, someone's keystroke, and his actual Standard level was something else, something whose presentation she should be looking up instead of this. It was the dull possibility, the clerical one, the one every resident wanted to skip because it involved no medicine. She did not skip it. She worked it, and the work went somewhere she had not planned for it to go.

Initial Standard classification was not an interview. It was a measurement — done in adolescence, by field-reading equipment, the civilian classification arrays that were cousins to her diagnostic ones, downstream of the same physics. You put the subject in front of an instrument built to read Resonance field state, and the instrument read it, and the reading became the number, and the number followed you through every license and clearance for the rest of your life. That was the procedure. That was the procedure *now*, and it had been the procedure twenty years ago when a fourteen-year-old Ruan Sela would have stood in front of a classification array in whatever regional center served his district.

An instrument built to read Resonance field state.

She put the pen down.

Seven such instruments, this week, from three manufacturers, had aimed themselves at Ruan Sela and returned null. If the classification array — twenty years ago, different hardware, same physics — had also returned null, or returned whatever a classification array returned when it had nothing to classify, then somewhere in the procedure a human being would have had to write a number in a box anyway, because the form did not have a field for nothing, and forms that did not have a field for nothing got filled in with the floor. Standard Three was the modal classification for the general population. It was what you wrote when you were writing *presumed ordinary*. It was, possibly, what a technician in a regional center wrote for a quiet fourteen-year-old whose reading had not come out, so that the boy could have a license and a life, and who would ever check.

She was inferring far past her data and she knew it. The classification could have been perfectly ordinary; the null could date from eighteen months ago and no earlier; nothing on her desk distinguished the possibilities. That was precisely what made it an open item instead of a theory. To close it she would need his original classification records — the raw acquisition, not the resulting number — and those lived with the licensing bureau's archival system, and she had no clinical authority to request raw classification data on a patient, and the one file of his she knew to be restricted had taught her what requests into his history did. They disappeared into repositories she could not see, and possibly they were noticed.

She did not write any of that. She wrote: *8. Misclassification: cannot eliminate. Std III assigned by measurement — same instrument class as current nulls. If classification acquisition also null → assigned value is placeholder, not reading. Requires original classification records. Not currently obtainable.* And then she drew a circle around the numeral, once, the way she flagged the items that were not finished with her, and made herself write the numeral nine, because the list did not stop for its most interesting member. That was the other thing the method was for.

The rest of the morning went to the ward, because the ward did not know she had a list. Rounds at eight. Bed six's pain modulation, holding on the rewritten schedule, the patient sleeping through the morning assessment for the first time in a week, which she charted with more pleasure than the chart conveyed. A new admission in the east wing, straightforward, boundary strain from an occupational exposure with a presentation so textbook it was almost restful — a finding for every instrument, a category for every finding. Fenn presented it competently and slightly too fast. She slowed him down once and let the rest go. At eleven she looked in on Room Four, briefly, in the ordinary way, and found Ruan sitting by the window with the paperback, the fog's absence having given him the water, which he was not reading about but watching, the book open face-down on his knee. He asked whether the tests were continuing today. She said no — today was analysis. He said, "The quiet part," with evident approval, and went back to the water, and she went back to the desk, and the list, and the pen, and did not tell him he was on page two of it, though she supposed he knew.

Nine through thirteen went faster, because the brush had been cleared and the reasons were shorter. *Nine, novel presentation of known suppression disorder* — open, pending the same longitudinal series that held four and five hostage; a novel presentation was by definition a trajectory she had not watched yet. *Ten, malingering* — deliberate concealment of field state — eliminated by mechanics rather than by character, and she was careful to write it that way, because trust was not an argument. The instruments did not take the patient's testimony. A passive acquisition read what the field did, not what its owner wished to display; cooperation could not produce a null any more than holding your breath could produce an absent heartbeat. There was no performance surface. Line. *Eleven, post-Resonant field collapse* — the terminal presentation, documented in end-stage disease, where the field guttered as the body did — eliminated by the whole of him: ambulatory, eating, sleeping five hours and four minutes, coupling normal, vitals unremarkable, a man so far from dying that the category was almost an insult to the data. Line. *Twelve, Resonant material sequestration* — field state localized to implanted material, the odd documented cases where a reading hid in a surgical fixation or a fragment — eliminated by history and imaging both; no implants, no surgical record, and Carrow Bay's structural scans, which the packet did include, showed a man containing nothing but himself. Line. *Thirteen, contact field interference* — the proximity effect, one practitioner's field masking another's — required a second Standard-class individual in sustained close contact, and left a bilateral pattern, and the pattern degenerated over days of separation. He had been in a hospital room alone for nine weeks and then for three days more, and the null had not degraded by any margin any instrument could state, because the instruments could not state anything about it at all. No history, no contact, no pattern, no decay. Line.

*Fourteen,* she wrote, and slowed down again, because fourteen was the other one that was not finished with her.

*Instrument class limitation.* The possibility that the null was not a failure to read but a *correct* reading — that the instruments were answering honestly a question they had not been designed to be asked. Every field-state instrument she had used this week was built and certified against the documented range of human Resonance presentation. Within that range they were exquisite. Outside it, their behavior was not specified, because outside it was not supposed to exist; you did not certify a thermometer against temperatures matter could not reach. If his field state lay outside the certified classification range — not absent, but *elsewhere*, in some part of the space the engineering had never been pointed at — then every instrument would do exactly what every instrument had done: complete its acquisition, find nothing within its design envelope, and report that nothing cleanly, without error, because no error had occurred. She could not eliminate this. She could not even test it, with anything she owned, because every test she owned was built inside the same envelope. She wrote, in full, because the sentence needed to exist somewhere outside her head: *if instruments are operating outside their design classification, null is not a failure. It is an accurate reading of a state outside classification range.* She looked at the sentence. It was the closest thing to a hypothesis the page contained, and it was not a hypothesis, it was a boundary on her instruments' competence, and she left it marked *open* and did not let it become anything more, because the thing it wanted to become had no evidence under it. Only an absence, and she had spent three days learning exactly how much an absence could and could not carry.

*Fifteen. Unknown.* Undocumented presentation. She wrote it because it belonged on every differential ever written, the standing admission at the bottom of the page that the catalog of what medicine had seen was not the catalog of what existed. Most days it was a formality, a genuflection. She wrote it today in the same hand as the others and did not line it out, and was aware, as she wrote it, that on this particular list the last item was doing more work than it had ever done on any list of hers before. She left it there. It did not need a note. It was the note.

By nine that evening she had the tally, boxed at the bottom of the fourth page the way she boxed the assessment at the bottom of a chart note. Nine categories eliminated, each with its reason and its date. Four open pending longitudinal data she did not have and could only get by waiting — the two dissociation classes, the novel suppression presentation, and the instrument-class question, which waiting would not actually resolve but which she had no other column for. One circled: the classification records, live and unpursuable. One permanent: unknown.

It was progress. She made herself say so, internally, in the flat voice she used for auditing her own morale, because it was true: the country had gotten smaller. Three days ago the finding could have been anything. Tonight it could not be contamination, could not be suppression, could not be artifact, could not be equipment, could not be malingering, could not be collapse or sequestration or contact interference or anything iatrogenic — could not be, with documented reasons, most of the things a reasonable physician would reach for first. That was the shape of the progress. Everything she had earned in three days of instrument-grade thoroughness was subtractive. She had built, carefully, on paper, in her own hand, a detailed and defensible map of where the answer was not, and she was aware — the way she was aware of weather — that this was the specific kind of progress that wore clinicians down. Not failure, which at least ended things. The other one. The long correct middle, where every day's work was real and every day's yield was another verified absence, and the discipline consisted of not letting the absences accumulate into a mood.

Fenn knocked on the open door at nine, which was his way — knocking on open doors — and came in with two coffees, which was also his way, and had been since his first year, when she had understood within a month that the coffee was partly kindness and partly reconnaissance and had decided that the mixture was, on balance, also kindness. He put hers down at the edge of the desk, clear of the notepad, which he did not look at directly, which meant he had seen it.

"Anything?" he said.

She capped her pen. "I've found what it isn't."

"Is that progress?"

"Yes."

He was quiet a moment, holding his coffee. "That might be the most depressing sentence I've ever heard in a clinical context," he said, in the tone of a man offering it as humor and hearing, halfway through, that the room was not where he had aimed it.

She did not correct him. She looked at the boxed tally at the bottom of the page, nine lines closed and six still breathing, and said, "It had competition," and drank some of the coffee.

He almost said something else — she watched him decide not to, which was newer than the coffee, and better — and he nodded at the notepad the way people nodded at things they respected and did not understand, and told her bed six had slept through the evening assessment too, and left. He was very quiet with the door, which was still open, because she had never closed it.

She stayed at the desk a while longer. Not working; the list was done, and adding to a finished differential at the end of a long day was how errors got in. She squared the notepad on the blotter. Four pages, narrow-ruled, her own hand deteriorating slightly toward the bottom of each page the way it always did. The circle around the eight had gone over its own line twice, she noticed, which she had not noticed doing. The evening ward sound came through the open door — a cart, far off, the station tone, once, somebody's soft shoes on the west corridor going away. On the shelf the telemetry repeater carried its six channels: five traces breathing at their five separate tempos, bed six's among them, slow now, sleeping. The sixth channel held its nothing. Not dark. Not zero. Unpopulated, steady, the interface's own silence, keeping — she had started thinking of it this way and did not chart that either — keeping station.

She turned the notepad face down, which was not concealment, only the habit of a person who did not leave open questions face up overnight, and put the pen on top of it, and sat for one minute more with the coffee going cold and the small true weight of the day: nine executions, four suspensions, one circle she could not open, and at the bottom of the last page, unlined, undated, patient, the word that was not an answer and was not going anywhere. Then she stood, and put the cup where the night nurse would find it, and went out past the station into the late ward, through the sound the building made without fog, carrying the smaller country with her, and holding it still.

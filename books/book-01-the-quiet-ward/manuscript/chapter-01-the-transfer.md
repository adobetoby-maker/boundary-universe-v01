# Chapter 1 — The Transfer

Sable Hren read wards the way other people read weather.

She came in at six-fifteen, forty minutes before handoff, because a hospital at rest told her things a hospital awake would spend all day concealing. The night shift had the lights in the east wing at quarter-power. Someone had left a supply cart angled across the corridor outside bed nine, which meant bed nine had needed something in a hurry around four in the morning and nobody had needed anything since. The coffee in the staff alcove was fresh, which meant the night charge nurse expected to be asked questions at handoff and wanted to be sharp for them. None of this was Resonance. It was just attention, which in Sable's experience was most of what Resonance turned out to be once you subtracted the equipment.

She walked the east wing first. Twenty-two beds, fourteen occupied, and she let her field sense open the way she'd been opening it for twelve years — not reaching, never reaching, just lowering the threshold of what she was willing to notice. A Standard Six perception at rest was a wide, shallow thing. It did not image tissue. It did not return numbers. What it gave her was the sense of fourteen biological systems negotiating their boundary conditions with the world: heat moving out, glucose moving through, the small ceaseless bookkeeping of bodies that had stopped fighting for the night.

Bed six was sleeping badly — his field state had the granular, interrupted texture of pain being managed one notch below adequately. She made a note to review his BM-2 modulation schedule. Bed eleven, post-surgical, was exactly as boring as she wanted her to be, which was the highest compliment Sable's profession offered. Bed fourteen's metabolic assist had been tapered yesterday and his system was carrying its own load now, a little raggedly, the way a patient walks the first day off crutches.

Forty-four beds in the attachment, two wings, one procedure suite, one imaging bay, and a corridor connecting it to the main hospital that the main hospital's staff called the annex and her staff did not call anything, because when you worked in a place you didn't need a name for it. Pacific Meridian Boundary Medicine Center. The plaque at the entrance was brushed steel and slightly too large, installed by people who had expected the facility to grow.

She was at the nurses' station in the west wing, initialing the overnight telemetry log, when the transfer summary arrived.

It came through the regional intake queue at 7:52 with a priority flag and an arrival time. She noticed the arrival time first, because it was the only unambiguous piece of information in the document.

*Patient arriving 12:00. Ground transport. Accepting physician: Hren, S.*

She had not yet agreed to be the accepting physician. That was not unusual — intake assigned first and asked second, and she had the only open bed in the west wing with full diagnostic fit-out, which made the assignment arithmetic rather than judgment. She pulled the summary onto the station's wide display and read it standing up, which was how she read everything the first time.

*Sela, Ruan. 34. Male. Former occupation: Resonance field technician, civilian contract.* Insurance authorization attached, complete, pre-approved for thirty days of inpatient boundary medicine care — which was generous, and she filed the generosity away without deciding what it meant. *Admitting diagnosis: acute Resonance dissociation syndrome.* Transfer initiated by Carrow Bay Clinical Institute, two hundred miles north, a facility she knew by reputation to be competent and by one prior shared case to be meticulous. *Reason for transfer: unresolvable field instability.*

She read the phrase twice, then went looking for the data underneath it.

There wasn't any.

A transfer summary for a boundary medicine patient had a shape, the way a discharge letter had a shape, the way a death certificate had a shape. You expected the admitting field baseline: the BM-1 imaging series from intake, tissue field-states rendered in the standard false-color that every practitioner on the coast could read faster than text. You expected serial imaging — daily or twice daily for an unstable patient, timestamped, so the receiving physician could see the trajectory and not just the endpoint. You expected the medication field-coupling record, because you could not safely give this man so much as an anti-inflammatory without knowing what his cellular boundaries had been doing to drug uptake. You expected Conduit telemetry, if he had a Conduit. You expected, at absolute minimum, numbers.

The summary contained a medication list with no coupling data. A behavioral log — *patient cooperative, oriented, no agitation* — repeated with minor variations across nine weeks of entries, which told her the nursing staff had liked him and had run out of things to say. A single line under diagnostics: *field imaging performed serially; results inconsistent with equipment parameters.* And the discharge statement, the phrase doing all the load-bearing work: *unresolvable field instability.*

*Instability* was a measurement word. It meant variance. It meant a field state that would not hold still long enough to characterize, and it came with a magnitude, always, because instability without magnitude was not a finding, it was a mood.

There was no magnitude anywhere in the document.

She read the summary a second time, slower, the way she'd been taught to read imaging: not looking for what was there, looking for what the structure of the thing implied should be there and wasn't. The second read confirmed the first and added a detail she had skimmed past. The physician of record at Carrow Bay had signed the transfer, but the signature block was the only place his name appeared. No clinical narrative. No summary of treatment attempted. Nine weeks of inpatient care at a meticulous facility, and the physician of record had contributed one signature.

The third read she did sitting down, because by then she was no longer reading it as a summary. She was reading it as a specimen. A document was a record of decisions, and this one recorded a long series of decisions to not write things down, made by people whose entire professional training ran the other way. Either the data had been removed after the fact, which was a serious allegation she had no grounds for and did not intend to make, or — more likely, she told herself, and noticed herself preferring it — the data had never resolved into anything a chart could hold, and nine weeks of clinicians had each looked at their own entries and quietly declined to be the one on record saying so.

She did not know which it was. She was comfortable saying she did not know, a habit that had cost her something at every stage of her career and that she had kept anyway, on the theory that the alternative cost more and billed later.

What she knew was this: a patient was arriving at noon with a diagnosis that was a category, not a description, from a facility that had spent nine weeks with him and sent her four pages. She had no baseline. She had four hours.

"That's it?"

Fenn Ash had come up behind her left shoulder, which was where he always stood when he read over it, close enough that she could smell that he'd already had two coffees and far enough back that he could claim he wasn't reading over her shoulder. He was twenty-nine, in his last residency year, and he arrived every morning pre-loaded with the day's literature the way some people arrived with umbrellas — usually unnecessary, occasionally decisive.

"That's the complete transfer packet," she said.

"That's a transfer packet the way a napkin is a tablecloth." He leaned in, scrolling with two fingers, and she let him, because he would find the same absences she had and it was worth knowing how fast. "No baseline imaging. No serial series. No coupling record — okay, we can't dose him, that's fun. Where's the physician's narrative?"

"There isn't one."

"Nine weeks." He scrolled back to the top and read the discharge line aloud, giving it a weight it had not earned. "*Unresolvable field instability.* Unresolvable by whom? At what magnitude? Instability's a variance claim, you can't just —" He stopped, visibly restraining himself from delivering the lecture she had delivered to him, twice, in his first year. "There's no data supporting that phrase anywhere in this document."

"No."

"Did you call them?"

"Records opens at nine."

Fenn straightened up and did the thing he did when he was uncomfortable, which was to fill the silence with a conclusion. "You know what this is, right? Nine weeks, no findings they were willing to sign, a diagnosis that's basically Latin for *we give up*, and a transfer to the facility with the region's best diagnostic reputation." He gestured at the screen, generous, indicting. "They ran out of patience. Somebody upstairs looked at the bed-day cost of a patient who wasn't producing chartable results, and *unresolvable field instability* is what running out of patience looks like when it's typed up. We're not receiving a patient, we're receiving a shrug."

Sable looked at the discharge line for a moment longer.

It was a plausible reading. It was, in fact, the most plausible reading, and it had the additional feature of being the reading that made the next thirty days simplest: a tired facility, an inconvenient patient, a diagnostic dead end that would turn out to be an artifact of somebody's miscalibrated array, resolved here inside a week by careful people with good equipment. She could feel the reading's gravity, the way it wanted to be adopted. Nine weeks of clinicians at a meticulous institution had probably felt the same pull toward whatever their version of the simple story had been.

She did not respond to Fenn, and after four seconds he glanced at her sideways, recalibrated, and changed the subject himself, which was the most self-aware thing she had seen him do all month.

"Room Four's open," he said. "West wing. Full fit-out."

"I know. Intake already assigned it. Walk with me."

Room Four was at the quiet end of the west corridor, past the imaging bay, a single with its own washroom and a window that faced the water when the fog permitted, which in the mornings it mostly did not. The room's diagnostic fit-out was built into the wall behind the bed head: a BM-1 passive imaging array on an articulated boom, lattice plates set flush into the wall panels — engineered Resonant material, inert until charged, capable of holding a stabilized field-state sample long enough for the instruments to characterize it — and a telemetry node tied into the station system, so that a patient in this room could be monitored continuously without anyone standing over him. It was the best-instrumented room in the attachment. She had specified most of it herself, six years ago, in the budget cycle when the administration had been feeling expansive.

She had Fenn run the array's self-check while she inventoried the room the old-fashioned way — bed, chair, call button, the drawer of things patients needed and never asked for. The array reported itself healthy across all channels. She had him run it again with the reference standard, a sealed lattice puck of known field-state that lived in the imaging bay for exactly this purpose, and the array read the puck to within a tenth of a percent of its certified values.

"It's fine," Fenn said. "It was fine the first time."

"I know it's fine. I want it documented as fine, timestamped, before he arrives." She initialed the calibration log herself. "If this patient's imaging comes back strange, the first question anyone asks is going to be about my equipment. I'd like to have answered it in advance."

"You think it'll come back strange?"

"I think Carrow Bay's equipment is as good as ours, and something about nine weeks of their imaging was bad enough or odd enough that no one would summarize it." She heard herself add the extra sentence and let it stand: "That's two different problems, and I'd rather find out which one we have before I have an opinion about it."

At nine she called Carrow Bay.

The records office answered on the fourth ring, and the clerk was pleasant and thorough and entirely unhelpful in the specific way of someone with nothing to hide and nothing to give. The transfer packet was complete as transmitted, she said. Everything released for transfer had been sent. Sable asked whether serial imaging existed beyond what was in the packet. The clerk said she could see that imaging studies had been performed — the procedure billing was right there — but that the study files themselves weren't in the release set, and releases were assembled by the clinical team, not by records. Sable asked to speak with the physician of record. The clerk said Dr. Amroth was off service and offered his voicemail, and Sable left a message that she kept to four sentences: her name, her facility, the patient's name, and a request for the imaging series at his earliest convenience. She was professionally polite about it. There was no reason not to be. A clinical team that had assembled a release set without its own imaging had reasons, and the reasons were probably ordinary — an oversight, a backlog, a resident given the task and given no list — and she would probably have the files by the end of the week.

She wrote *imaging series requested, Carrow Bay, 09:07* in her working notes, and under it, after a moment, *packet as sent = packet as intended?* — and then looked at the question mark for a while, decided it was doing honest work, and left it in.

The admission orders took her the better part of an hour, and most of the hour was subtraction.

An ordinary boundary medicine admission came with a coupling record, and the coupling record told you what you could give the patient and what his cellular boundaries would do with it — whether a standard analgesic would cross at standard rates, whether his field state was enhancing uptake or blocking it, whether the sedative that worked on ninety-eight percent of the population would arrive in his bloodstream and then simply stand at the door. Without one, every medication was a small experiment, and Sable did not run experiments on people she had never met. She struck the standing pre-orders one by one and rebuilt the set from the short list of compounds with no meaningful field-coupling profile at all — the pharmacological equivalent of foods that no one had ever been allergic to. It was a thin formulary. It would hold for seventy-two hours, long enough to establish his coupling behavior herself, from zero, with the BM-5 protocols she'd been licensed on for a decade and used maybe six times, because almost nobody arrived without a record.

The pharmacist on days, a careful man named Osei who had never once been rushed by anyone, called her back to confirm the order set because it looked, he said, like an admission from thirty years ago. She said that was roughly the situation. He was quiet for a second and then said he'd flag the chart so no covering physician helpfully modernized it over the weekend, which was the kind of thinking that made her grateful for pharmacists in general and for him in particular, and she told him so, and he said, "Well," and hung up, embarrassed.

Administrator Colm found her in the corridor outside the imaging bay at half past ten, which meant Colm had been looking for her, because Colm did not wander.

"The noon transfer," Colm said. "Intake tells me the authorization is unusually clean. Thirty days pre-approved." She said it the way she said most things, warmly and without hurry, a woman who had spent two decades learning that the institutional voice worked best when it sounded like hospitality. "That's a courtesy we don't often get. I wanted to make sure you had everything you need."

"I have a bed, a calibrated array, and four pages," Sable said. "I've requested the rest from Carrow Bay."

"Good." Colm glanced at the tablet she carried and did not consult. "Acute Resonance dissociation. We've handled those before."

"We've handled ones that were documented before."

"Well," Colm said, comfortably, "now it will be," and patted the doorframe twice in the manner of someone blessing a vessel before a voyage, and moved off down the corridor at the exact speed of a person with six more conversations scheduled before lunch. Sable watched her go and reflected, not for the first time, that Priya Colm was the best possible version of the thing she was, and that the thing she was ran on the assumption that every arriving patient fit a category that already had a workflow. It was an assumption that was correct often enough to build a career on. Most assumptions were.

The rest of the morning was a hospital being a hospital, and Sable let it have her, because the alternative was standing in Room Four staring at an empty bed.

Bed six's pain modulation was, as she'd suspected, sequenced wrong — his overnight BM-2 schedule had been written for the patient he'd been on Tuesday, intercepting nociceptive signal at intervals his healing had already outgrown, so that the modulation kept arriving a half-step behind the pain instead of ahead of it. She rewrote the schedule with the day nurse, shortening the intercept intervals and lowering the redirection depth, because a warning system suppressed too well was its own kind of injury, and a patient who could not feel a wound could reopen one. It took eleven minutes and it was the most straightforwardly satisfying thing she would do all day: a documented problem, a documented mechanism, a documented fix. The whole of boundary medicine in miniature, working exactly as the textbooks promised, on a patient the textbooks had anticipated.

She spent ten minutes at her desk afterward with the door open, which was as close as she came to taking a break. Her office was small and faced the parking structure, and she had never asked for a better one, because the view she cared about was the six-channel telemetry repeater mounted beside the door, and it faced her. Six patients, six field states rendered as slow-moving traces, the licensed ceiling of her BM-6 monitoring privileges. She had chosen the six that morning the way she chose them every morning — not the sickest six, but the six most likely to change — and she watched the traces the way a harbormaster watches water, registering nothing because there was nothing to register, and finding the nothing restful.

Fenn had asked her once, in his first year, why she still did this by eye when the station software would alarm on any excursion. She had told him that software alarmed on what it had been told to expect, and the answer had come out flatter and harder than she'd intended, and he had not asked the follow-up question, which she'd been grateful for, because the follow-up question was *what happened to you*, and the honest answer lived in a licensing file with her name on it and a suspension she had requested herself. She had learned — expensively, on another person's ledger — what it cost to expect the wrong things. Careful was not a temperament. Careful was a procedure she had installed, deliberately, the way the lattice plates were installed in Room Four, and she serviced it every morning by eye.

At eleven-forty she went back to Room Four and stood in it alone.

The array boom was folded against the wall. The lattice plates were dark. The bed was made with the particular tautness of a bed no one had failed to sleep in yet. Through the window the fog was beginning to organize itself into a withdrawal, the water showing in strips, and she stood with her hands in the pockets of her coat and let her field sense open onto the empty room the way she had opened it onto the ward at dawn — not because there was anything to read, but because in thirty minutes there would be, and she wanted the last uncomplicated reading of the space. An empty room at rest. Boundary conditions negotiated between wall and air, air and glass, glass and fog. Nothing that needed her.

She thought about the four pages. She thought, specifically, about the version of events where Fenn was right — where nine weeks of silence was fatigue, and the strangeness would come apart in her hands like wet paper the first time she got a clean baseline. It was still the most likely version. She made herself say so, internally, in words, because a diagnostician who stopped articulating the boring hypothesis was a diagnostician beginning to want the interesting one, and wanting was the first miscalibration, the one that never showed up on any reference standard.

And underneath that, in the place where she kept the things she knew before she could support them, was the shape of the document itself: not tired, not careless. Careful. Four pages assembled by people being careful about what they wrote down.

Those were also two different problems.

At 11:54 the station called her. Ground transport was at the receiving entrance, eight minutes early. She took the west corridor at her ordinary pace, past the imaging bay, past the calibration log with its timestamp, and Fenn fell in beside her at the junction, holding two tablets and the expression of a man who had spent his morning reading the entire published literature on acute Resonance dissociation syndrome and had discovered, around page forty, how short it was.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I pulled every documented dissociation presentation in the regional database going back eleven years. If he matches any of them, I'll know inside a day."

"And if he doesn't match any of them?"

Fenn opened his mouth, and for once nothing came out of it, and the two of them walked the rest of the corridor in a silence he did not try to fill.

Through the glass doors of the receiving bay she could see the transport backing in, its lights turning without sound, and the receiving nurse moving out to meet it with a wheelchair the paperwork had not said he would need and had not said he wouldn't.

Four pages, she thought. Four hours. Both spent.

The transport's rear doors opened.

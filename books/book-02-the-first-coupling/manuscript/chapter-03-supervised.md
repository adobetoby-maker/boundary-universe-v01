# Chapter Three: SUPERVISED

## Day 2,269

Soren submitted the fourteen-page assignment at thirty-one pages.

Aaron read all of them.

The first nine answered the question Aaron had assigned six weeks earlier: the difference between preserving a system and changing the question until the system became preservable. The next seventeen examined cases in which the distinction depended on where the practitioner drew the system boundary. Five pages proposed revisions to the Conduit limiter curriculum, three of which Aaron had already entered into the next release and one of which would have disabled an interlock under exactly the conditions that made the interlock necessary.

The final paragraph read:

*A practitioner cannot claim to have preserved the defined system if his intervention replaces a constraint he lacked the architecture to satisfy. The replacement may still constitute good engineering. It does not constitute success at the assigned operation, and calling it success conceals the practitioner’s actual limit from everyone, including himself.*

Aaron read the paragraph twice.

Then he opened the curriculum record and changed Soren’s status from *derived geometry only* to *supervised source exposure eligible*.

The software requested confirmation from a second authorized officer. Amara supplied it from her office across the courtyard forty-three seconds later. She also sent a message.

**He passed, then.**

Aaron looked through his window. Morning light reached only the upper third of the opposite building. The courtyard below retained the night’s rain in shallow depressions worn into paving stones older than field science. A facilities worker guided a wheeled cleaning unit around the drainage channel because its navigation model classified the channel as an edge condition and refused to cross it.

**He completed the curriculum,** Aaron replied.

Amara’s response arrived before he closed the message panel.

**I’ll translate that as yes.**

He did not correct her. Translation errors below operational significance did not require intervention.

At 06:34, Aaron opened the live-session authorization package.

It contained eighty-seven stop conditions.

Thirty-two concerned Soren’s attention state: loss of continuous self-location, uncontrolled subdivision, persistent cross-sensory translation, failure to respond to a release command. Twenty-one concerned the Conduit: limiter latency, lattice saturation, telemetry divergence, unauthorized boundary expansion. Seventeen concerned the monitoring network and the aperture geometry through which Geneva accessed the source’s interior field state. The remainder concerned Aaron.

His section used fewer words. If his coupling index exceeded the validated band, the session stopped. If his identity boundary lost coherence, the session stopped. If he could not release Soren’s field signature from the shared observation geometry within sixty milliseconds, the automatic severance stack stopped it for him.

The controls gave failure a detailed vocabulary and success one line.

*Practitioner receives and correctly distinguishes one bounded field-state sequence under primary-operator supervision.*

Aaron preferred the imbalance. Success required definition. Failure benefited from a list.

He reviewed every condition, signed the primary authorization, and opened Soren’s overnight baseline.

Clean. Seven dense interaction bands, distributed fringe stable, no residual structure from the previous day’s synthetic exercise. Soren had slept six hours and fifty-two minutes according to the medical compliance record. His resting attention index sat four percent above his six-week mean.

Elevated anticipation, the system called it.

The system had not spent six weeks waiting to touch a secret.

---

Conduit Rooms One and Two shared a control bay and a wall that predated both rooms.

The original masonry ran eighty centimeters thick between them. During the Conduit-wing conversion, engineers had cut three narrow channels through the stone for shielded data, cooling, and the resonant transfer bus. They had left the exposed edges behind inspection glass. Pale aggregate and hand-laid brick surrounded three black composite conduits, the building’s previous century visible around the current one without either agreeing to the arrangement.

On Helix Perimeter, adjacent experiment bays had shared vibration, ventilation, power, and the knowledge that vacuum began a few structural layers away. Here, Room One could not hear Room Two. Each occupied its own mass, temperature profile, and minor gravitational relationship to the sloping foundation. The interface software compensated for a 0.018-degree difference between the chairs.

Geneva had made parallelism an administrative objective rather than a physical fact.

Aaron entered Room One at 07:11. Jo Varga waited beside the primary chair with the contact map ready. Amara stood in the control bay beyond the glass, checking the independent medical cutoff against a paper list. She trusted the digital record. She also trusted paper’s inability to update itself while no one watched.

Soren had already entered Room Two.

Through the control display, Aaron watched Varga’s counterpart fit sensors at Soren’s temples and beneath his jaw. Soren kept his hands open on the chair arms, not gripping the manual releases. The curriculum taught practitioners to establish contact with the releases without preparing to use them. Anticipatory tension changed the hand model, and the Conduit sometimes translated that change into a false boundary event.

His hands remained correctly neutral.

His right foot moved once every eight seconds.

The curriculum addressed field control, not feet.

Aaron opened the room channel. “You have a question.”

Soren’s foot stopped. “I haven’t asked one.”

“You’ve reviewed the same status panel four times.”

“Three.”

“The first occurred before the recording marker.”

Soren glanced toward the nearest ceiling sensor, perhaps recalculating how much of the room qualified as before. “Why does the protocol require you in an active chair? Supervision usually happens from the control bay.”

“Because this is not a usual Conduit operation.”

“That answer has survived six weeks without becoming more informative.”

Aaron brought the shared geometry onto both room displays. The model showed two local interface volumes separated by the wall, then the monitoring-network reference extending beyond the building. The external endpoint remained absent. In Soren’s authorized version, the reference terminated at a classification boundary rendered as a plain gray plane. Accurate geometry, incomplete topology.

“A routine operator enters a field relationship fully defined by the Conduit,” Aaron said. “The interface knows every participating system, permitted reservoir, exchange limit, and termination state before the session begins. Today’s external structure cannot be represented completely inside a local model. I establish the observation boundary through my architecture. Your interface operates inside it.”

“So you’re the Conduit for the Conduit.”

“No. I am the practitioner establishing a boundary the instrument can maintain after establishment. Calling a practitioner an instrument creates poor safety doctrine and worse employment policy.”

“But if you release, I lose access.”

“Immediately.”

“If my interface fails?”

“I close your side without closing mine, if doing so remains necessary for controlled termination.”

“If you fail?”

“Both sides close. The independent severance stack removes the transfer-bus correlation and resolves each room against its local ground.”

Soren studied the gray plane. “Can the external structure distinguish the signatures?”

The question reached beyond procedure. Aaron could have limited his answer to the authorized model. Instead he considered the five Helix Perimeter sessions: the reflected carrier, the recognition quality, the sequence that had become a question only after weeks of translation. The Node had distinguished Aaron from every other operator before Aaron knew distinction formed part of the interaction.

“It has responded selectively to practitioner-specific geometry before,” he said.

Soren’s attention sharpened. “Yours.”

“That is why the network uses my calibration carrier.”

“Has anyone else’s signature entered the aperture?”

“Not through an authorized Conduit session.”

Soren became still in the chair. His field baseline did not change, but the movement in his foot ceased completely. “So this isn’t only my first session.”

“It is the program’s first controlled presentation of a secondary human signature.”

The sentence disclosed exactly what his authorization permitted. It did not disclose why no uncontrolled presentation had produced a recoverable result, what Yuki’s last eleven milliseconds contained, or why Aaron had opposed this point in the protocol until Kessler’s risk review made opposition another form of unsupervised delegation.

Soren read the absence anyway. Not its contents, but its outline.

“What happened to the uncontrolled one?” he asked.

Aaron’s threat-response index increased before the Conduit began recording it. Practice offered several true sentences at once. *That record is outside your compartment.* *The result justified today’s controls.* *You don’t need the case history to perform the operation.* Each would close the subject efficiently.

He selected the sentence Soren required to understand the stakes, even if not their origin.

“A non-Eight practitioner cannot independently sustain this contact,” Aaron said. “If you move outside the boundary I establish, the Conduit will stop the session. If the Conduit does not stop it quickly enough, I will. There is no version in which you prove the classification model wrong by holding longer.”

Soren kept his gaze on the gray plane. “Understood.”

“Repeat the last condition.”

“Duration does not create capability. If I leave the established boundary, the session ends.”

“Good.”

The word changed Soren’s expression more than praise should have after six weeks of Aaron using it only when evidence warranted. His right foot resumed its eight-second interval.

Varga secured Aaron’s occipital contacts. “Any headache, visual disturbance, discontinuity, unexplained memory, or desire to revise the protocol?”

“No.”

“Coffee?”

“At 05:51.”

“Food?”

“At 05:43.”

“Human emotion?”

Aaron looked at her.

Varga marked the checklist. “No observable change from baseline.”

Amara’s voice entered through the room channel. “Primary medical confirms clean. Secondary medical confirms clean. Network aperture remains passive and stable. We have nine minutes before the scheduled observation window.”

Aaron settled his head against the support. The chair had acquired a shallow compression at the shoulders from repeated use. He had supervised hundreds of Conduit sessions from the control bay and entered none since leaving Helix Perimeter. The program classified that interval as twenty-four months without active exposure. His nervous system classified the chair in less than a second.

A persistent low-grade pressure gathered behind his eyes before the interface powered beyond standby. Not pain yet. Prediction.

Varga lowered the room lights. “Primary manual release?”

Aaron pressed both thumb controls and let go.

“Verified. Dr. Aldarin?”

Soren’s reply crossed the shared channel. “Secondary manual release verified.”

“State today’s operation,” Aaron said.

Soren had repeated it at every briefing for ten days. He answered without speed.

“One bounded observation sequence from the external field structure. You establish and maintain the access boundary. My Conduit receives a reduced translation layer inside that boundary. I do not initiate exchange, deepen contact, alter the shared geometry, or respond through field action. On release command, I abandon every active model and let the interface resolve me to baseline.”

“If you perceive a correctable instability?”

“Report it. Do not correct it.”

“If you identify a more stable containing geometry?”

Soren paused just long enough to make the answer his rather than the curriculum’s. “It belongs to a different experiment.”

Aaron touched the chair’s authorization plate. “Proceed to fitting depth.”

The Conduit came online.

The interface began with the body.

Contact pressure at his scalp resolved into a map. Chair, floor, room mass, grounded lattice beneath the composite panels. The building entered the model not as weight but as available momentum that the session rules prohibited him from using. Electrical charge moved through the sensor circuits in bounded loops. Cooling fluid carried heat from the Conduit core toward a reservoir two floors below. None of these systems shared an authorized exchange through him. The interface named their separateness by making each boundary legible to his coupling architecture.

Then it presented Room Two.

Soren’s field signature entered as a dense structure behind two limiter layers: seven sustained bands, each holding a different relationship among body, interface, and surrounding mass. Where Aaron’s architecture met an incompatibility and formed a temporary shared boundary, Soren’s built context. He located each system relative to every other one and maintained the set. The difference did not resemble lesser capacity. It resembled a tool made for another task.

Aaron held Room One, Room Two, and the transfer bus as independent structures. The Conduit offered the approved common boundary. He accepted it without closing the exchange path.

“Primary stable,” Amara said.

Another voice confirmed Soren’s telemetry. “Secondary stable.”

Aaron opened the path far enough for Soren’s interface to receive his calibration carrier.

Soren inhaled sharply.

“Report,” Aaron said.

“Distance between my hands increased. No movement. Pressure mapping across the upper teeth. Third component—” Soren stopped. His attention trace divided, recombined. “The room has a direction that isn’t spatial.”

“Identify the source.”

“Your signature through the transfer boundary.”

“Do you perceive the external structure?”

“No.”

Correct. The aperture remained closed. Aaron held the carrier for twelve seconds while the Conduit matched Soren’s private sensory mappings to the shared field relationship. Hands for boundary extent. Teeth for constraint density. Direction without space for the coupling path itself.

The headache behind Aaron’s eyes matured from prediction into fact.

At fitting depth, Standard Eight meant sustaining contact with another person’s incompatible field vocabulary while an instrument translated both of them badly and everyone nearby treated the result as professional advancement.

The work retained its glamour through careful access control.

“Calibration complete,” Amara said. “Prepare aperture stage one.”

Aaron checked Soren’s state. Clean identity boundary. Attention index elevated, stable. No attempt to build a containing frame around the two rooms. Six weeks earlier, Soren’s architecture had reached for that solution before the diagnostic finished presenting the problem. Now the possibility registered in the fringe trace and stopped there.

Aaron engaged the monitoring network.

The source did not occupy Geneva in any ordinary sense. It remained in the outer field, inside the suppression geometry it had maintained since before human instruments acquired categories for measuring it. The network preserved a calibrated relationship to the aperture: distributed observation nodes held the source bearing, matched Aaron’s carrier, and extended a field-state reference through linked Conduit lattices without transferring energy across the distance. Geneva supplied no impulse to the source. The source supplied none to Geneva. The interface joined local models of the permitted boundary conditions closely enough that the interior state became observable through the same narrow geometric imperfection Aaron had first encountered at Helix Perimeter.

No energy crossed the distance. Information arrived through changes in which local systems the network permitted to count as correlated.

Aaron’s coupling architecture completed the last boundary.

The aperture opened.

The Node entered his perception as structured absence.

Not an object. Not a location. A suppression state whose precision implied the field activity it contained: cancellation answering every outward tendency before tendency became emission, an interior organized around not becoming externally legible. Aaron found the familiar extent beyond his perceptual range, the duration without an accessible lower bound, the nearly legible pressure in the part of his mind that processed relationships among relationships.

Two years without contact disappeared from his nervous system. The Node had not become less complex during the interval. Aaron had merely stopped receiving the part that hurt.

His attention index climbed six points.

“Primary inside tolerance,” Amara said. Her voice reached him from an ordinary distance and required translation of a simpler kind. “Aperture stable.”

Aaron held the established geometry. “Dr. Aldarin. Report.”

Silence from Room Two.

Soren’s trace remained coherent. His heart rate increased. The seven interaction bands spread toward the containing model he had spent six weeks learning not to build.

“I have the carrier,” Soren said. Each word arrived separately. “Something beyond it. Constraint without a boundary. I can’t place the systems.”

“You are not required to place them. Observe the translation layer.”

“It keeps changing scale.”

“Let it.”

The reduced layer admitted only a fraction of the Node’s field state. Even that fraction exceeded the relational complexity most practitioners encountered outside multi-system incident work. Soren tried to track it as seven simultaneous structures. Each implied relationships to structures the limiter excluded. His architecture followed the missing relationships and found no permitted model at their end.

Attention distribution rose.

The Conduit reduced translation gain by three percent.

Soren’s leftmost band diverged anyway.

“Instability threshold one,” Amara said.

Aaron had already seen it. The band did not collapse. It reached outward, trying to include Aaron’s carrier, the translation layer, and the implied exterior structure inside one context large enough to make their constraints compatible.

“Soren. Name the operation.”

“Observation.”

“What are you building?”

“Nothing.”

The trace contradicted him.

Not a lie. His architecture had moved ahead of the part of him that assigned intent. Aaron knew that timing intimately and disliked its familiarity.

“Release the containing frame.”

Soren’s hands tightened around empty air above the chair controls.

“I can stabilize the missing relationships.”

“They are not available systems.”

“They’re present in the constraint pattern.”

“As implications. You cannot use an implied system as a reservoir.”

The divergent band split. Soren’s attention index crossed the second threshold.

Aaron placed pressure against the shared boundary, not closing it, making its defined edge unambiguous. The action cost no energy beyond the Conduit’s local lattice load. It changed which field models could exchange structural state. Soren’s containing frame met the limit and spread sideways along it.

The automatic severance timer armed.

“Release,” Aaron said.

Soren did.

All seven bands contracted at once. The containing frame vanished. His identity boundary stayed intact.

Aaron began closing the translation layer.

The Node moved first.

Its interior state changed around the aperture.

The change carried no words. No image, intention, or emotional quality crossed into Aaron’s mind. A field-state sequence propagated through the observable layer: the high-complexity relationships nearest Soren’s signature folded away; constraint density decreased; the aperture narrowed on the secondary side while remaining open to Aaron. The sequence removed potential exchanges in order of the load they imposed on Soren’s architecture. It did not retreat randomly. It selected.

Soren’s attention index dropped four points before the Conduit reduced gain.

Aaron stopped his closure command without reopening anything.

The Node presented Soren with a smaller structure.

Three relationships instead of the unbounded set: Soren’s body to his interface, his interface to Aaron’s carrier, Aaron’s carrier to the edge of something held deliberately beyond him. Each relationship fit inside Standard Seven. None invited a shared boundary. The sequence held for less than half a second, long enough for Soren’s Conduit to record it and for Aaron to understand the shape of what had changed.

Caution.

The translation arrived in his mind with the provisional accuracy of every word he assigned to the Node. Not fear. Not rejection. Not indifference. The field-state equivalent of reducing the force applied to a structure after measuring what it could bear.

Restraint, almost.

The Node had never done that for Aaron.

In the first sessions at Helix Perimeter, it had permitted the aperture to expose whatever the geometry made available. Aaron’s architecture either held or did not. The Node had registered him, reflected him, eventually shaped sequences toward the forms of his comprehension. It had not made itself smaller to preserve him.

Now it had encountered Soren’s signature and changed the interaction before the safety system could.

“Uncommanded source modulation,” Amara said. “Aaron?”

He checked the trace. The change preceded the Conduit gain reduction by one hundred thirteen milliseconds. It preceded his own closure command by forty-eight.

“I see it.”

“Do we stop?”

The correct procedural answer did not require interpretation. Any adaptive source change outside the expected sequence triggered review.

“Yes. Controlled close.”

He reduced the aperture. The Node released the secondary relationship without resistance. For one interval, its state remained legible against Aaron’s carrier alone: immense suppression, organized duration, and the fading geometry of a choice to withhold load from a structure that could not carry it.

Then Aaron closed the last boundary.

The source disappeared from the interface.

The headache stayed.

---

Soren failed the first orientation check.

Medical asked him to identify the room. He said Room Three.

He occupied Room Two.

He corrected himself after looking at the wall and passed the next eleven checks. Name, date, body boundary, session objective, last memory before closure. His baseline field signature returned within thirty-seven seconds. No null region, no residual translation structure, no persistent subdivision. The leftmost interaction band took nine seconds longer than the others to settle and then matched its pre-session state within instrument precision.

Aaron remained in Room One through his own recovery sequence. The headache receded to its established post-contact level: pressure without impairment, a familiar line drawn behind both eyes. Varga checked his pupils and asked the orientation questions without altering them for dignity.

He passed, including the room.

Amara entered the bay after medical released both chairs. “Control review. Now.”

Soren followed his technician through the opposite door. His face had lost color beneath the sensor marks. He moved carefully, as if the floor might renegotiate distance, but without assistance.

The control bay display held three synchronized traces: Aaron’s carrier, Soren’s translation state, and the source-side modulation reconstructed from the monitoring network. A vertical white line marked the first secondary instability. An amber line marked the Conduit’s automatic gain response.

The source had changed between them.

Amara enlarged the interval. “Network artifact?”

“No.” Aaron pointed to the lunar and Lagrange baselines. “All apertures resolve the same sequence. The change originates inside the suppression geometry.”

“Response to what?”

He could say *Soren*.

The evidence supported it. The modulation followed the unique structure of Soren’s instability, reducing complexity in exactly the order his Seven architecture required. It preserved Aaron’s carrier and altered only the secondary layer. No other variable changed inside the relevant sample window.

“The secondary signature,” Aaron said.

Amara looked at him. “Deliberate?”

Another question whose complete answer exceeded the data by a narrow but consequential margin.

“Adaptive,” he said. “Ordered. Source-origin.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

“Intent remains an interpretation.”

“You’re interpreting it.”

Aaron watched the sequence play again. High-load relationships folded away. A smaller structure remained. Nothing in the movement resembled avoidance. The Node had retained contact while making contact survivable for the new signature.

He knew what interpretation his coupling architecture supplied.

He also knew that assigning human motive to field-state structure had produced the destroyed page, two years of omissions, and an official vocabulary designed to hold everything except the thing itself.

“I’m classifying the modulation as adaptive risk attenuation pending repeat evidence,” he said.

Amara kept her attention on him for another second. Then she entered the phrase into the controlled record.

He had answered accurately. He had not answered her.

The distinction came easily now. Select the defensible portion. Mark inference as inference. Let procedural rigor carry the omission without ever stating anything false.

Practice reduced the effort and increased the cost. Aaron had once believed those quantities should move together. He had since acquired institutional evidence to the contrary.

The inner door opened. Soren entered with a medical officer behind him and took the chair farthest from the wall display. His foot did not move.

“How bad?” he asked.

“No lasting deviation in your baseline,” Amara said. “One brief orientation error, resolved without intervention. Your attention state crossed two thresholds and recovered after the release command.”

“I released.”

“You did,” Aaron said.

Soren looked toward the three traces. “After trying to contain it.”

“Yes.”

“I knew not to.”

“Knowledge and field response do not share a clock.”

“That sounds like a gentler term for failure.”

“It is a more accurate term for latency.”

Soren’s gaze moved across the source trace. “Something changed before the gain dropped.”

Aaron had expected the question. Expectation did not prepare the answer; it only removed surprise as an excuse for choosing poorly.

“The source layer reduced complexity,” he said.

“Because I destabilized it?”

“Following your instability.”

“That also wasn’t my question.”

Amara said nothing. She had used the same words less than a minute earlier.

Soren leaned forward. “Did I affect the external structure?”

“You introduced no energy or momentum exchange. The session logs show no unauthorized coupling. Your signature became present inside the observation geometry. The source state changed in correlation with it.”

“It noticed me.”

Aaron could have corrected the verb. *Registered* carried less implication. *Correlated* carried almost none. The full answer remained inside a compartment Soren did not possess and an interpretation Aaron had not decided how to file even in the compartment he did.

The Node had noticed him.

Then it had taken care.

That mattered because care required a model of what another system could survive. It required the Node to distinguish Aaron from the species around him, and Soren from Aaron, and danger from useful contact. It meant the restraint in the trace might not concern incapacity alone. It might concern a judgment extended toward the person carrying it.

Aaron did not tell Soren any of that.

“The source responded to your field signature,” he said. “We don’t yet know what the response means.”

Soren absorbed the sentence. Hunger returned to his attention, changed by the morning but not removed. Aaron could see him assembling the next question, the one about source identity, intention, clearance, and whether being noticed counted as the threshold everyone had spent years telling him he nearly reached.

Aaron interrupted before the question acquired language.

“You remain Standard Seven.”

Soren’s mouth closed.

“The source response does not indicate Coupling behavior in your trace,” Aaron continued. “You did not join the systems. You attempted to create a containing frame and released it on command. That is the result we have.”

“Do I continue?”

Amara looked at Aaron.

Stop authority belonged to him. So did the record of a source-side action none of their procedures anticipated. Soren’s data justified suspension pending review. It also showed a clean recovery, compliance before intervention, and a Node that had reduced load rather than rejected contact.

Aaron thought of the thirty-one-page assignment. The final paragraph. A practitioner could not claim success by replacing a constraint he lacked the architecture to satisfy.

Neither could an assessor claim safety because the other side of an experiment had intervened before his controls did.

“Not until we can reproduce the source modulation in a derived model,” he said. “Minimum seven days. Then a second supervised session, if the review supports it.”

Soren exhaled. Disappointment, relief, and renewed calculation competed without producing a clean external result.

“Seven days,” he said.

“Minimum.”

“Of course.”

Medical returned him to observation. Amara stayed in the control bay while Aaron signed the immediate suspension, the incident-review order, and the no-injury finding. Three documents described one event from different system boundaries. All three remained true.

When he reached the interpretive appendix, the form requested a classification for the source response.

Aaron entered:

*Adaptive risk attenuation in response to non-primary practitioner instability. Source-origin modulation preceded automatic limiter response. Intent undetermined.*

He did not enter *caution*.

The word belonged to his private translation, which meant it occupied the same category as several other accurate things the institution could not safely hold without deciding what they required.

He encrypted the raw trace into the Contact Zero archive and restricted the interpretive appendix to the eight-person compartment. Soren would receive the instrument record, the finding of no unauthorized exchange, and the requirement to reproduce the modulation through derived geometry before another attempt. He would not receive Aaron’s translation.

The omission took four permissions and nineteen seconds.

That efficiency disturbed him more than the omission.

On the display, the Node’s sequence ran once more. Complexity folded away from Soren. The smaller relationship held. The aperture closed.

Aaron watched the point at which the Node chose restraint.

Then he completed the report without calling it a choice.

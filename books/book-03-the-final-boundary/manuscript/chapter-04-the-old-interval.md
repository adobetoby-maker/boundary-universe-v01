# Chapter 4 — The Old Interval

Aaron brought paper.

Kade distrusted that immediately.

Not because paper was dangerous.

Because Aaron only used paper when the digital record had become too complicated to explain without pointing at something physically.

They sat in a small recovery conference room beneath Asterion Medical with one table, four chairs, and a wall display Park had refused to activate until she finished checking Aaron's vitals.

“Thirty minutes,” she said.

Aaron looked offended.

“You said forty yesterday.”

“Yesterday your pulse recovered faster.”

“I am recovering.”

“You are arguing.”

“Related.”

Park looked at Kade.

“Thirty minutes.”

Kade nodded.

Aaron did not.

Park left anyway.

Kade looked at the paper spread across the table.

Old plots.

Dates.

Handwritten labels.

One column had been copied enough times that the ink shades changed between rows.

“You printed this from an archive.”

“Some of it.”

“Some?”

Aaron touched the oldest page.

“This existed on paper first.”

Kade stared.

“That is historically aggressive.”

Aaron almost smiled.

Then Kade saw the first date.

Seventeen years before Aaron disappeared.

He stopped.

“That is before Contact Zero.”

Aaron nodded.

“Before the event we called Contact Zero.”

Kade looked at him.

Different sentence.

Important.

“Explain carefully.”

“I am going to.”

“That was not a request.”

“I know.”

Kade heard the old clipped rhythm and decided not to fight it yet.

Aaron pulled the first page closer.

The chart showed a noisy timing trace from an early Resonance array.

No Refuge Node.

No Asterion.

No modern Conduits.

A research station Kade did not recognize.

“Chile?”

“Northern Atacama test array. Pre-standardization.”

“What were they testing?”

“Coupled prediction between two remote operator models.”

Kade looked at the date again.

“Human operators?”

“Yes.”

“Resonant?”

“One confirmed. One probable. The old records are bad.”

“And the anomaly?”

Aaron pointed.

A flat interval.

Not flat exactly.

Unresolved.

**0.81 s ± 0.05**

Kade's skin tightened.

“Not point-eight-three.”

“No.”

“Close.”

“Yes.”

“Too close?”

Aaron looked at him.

“That is what I thought twenty years ago.”

Kade sat back.

Good.

A memory.

Not a conclusion.

Aaron continued.

“The second record is from a hospital training project in Kyoto. Two clinician models adjusting treatment priority from each other's predictions.”

Another interval.

**0.86 s ± 0.04**

Kade looked up.

“Kiyomizu precursor?”

“Maybe adjacent. I cannot prove program continuity.”

“Good.”

Aaron smiled faintly.

“You say that more now.”

“Everybody trained me badly.”

“Good badly?”

“Unclear.”

They kept reading.

Industrial control team.

Orbital logistics simulation.

Disaster coordination experiment.

Not many.

Five credible records over thirteen years.

All before official Contact Zero.

All involving systems predicting systems that changed because they were being predicted.

None involved a pulse from space.

None involved the Refuge Node.

None were identical.

The intervals ranged from 0.77 to 0.89 seconds.

Kade tapped the page.

“So the pulse did not create this category.”

Aaron shook his head.

“Careful.”

“Right. We know similar unresolved intervals were recorded before the pulse we just received and before official Contact Zero.”

“Yes.”

“That means whatever this is, Earth had already brushed against the condition.”

Aaron looked at the charts.

“Probably.”

Kade frowned.

“You hate certainty now.”

“I hated it then too. I was just worse at admitting it.”

That sounded more expensive.

Kade let it sit.

---

The Refuge records were worse.

Not because they had less data.

Because they had too much damaged data and not enough trustworthy order.

Aaron activated the wall display.

A legacy suppression log appeared.

Rows of phase corrections.

Signal damping.

Thermal load.

Operator strain.

Then a field Kade had never seen before.

**RELATIONAL COHERENCE EXCEPTION**

He pointed.

“Name from then?”

“No. Modern label.”

“What was it called then?”

Aaron hesitated.

Kade noticed.

“Memory or inference?”

“Memory.”

“Good.”

Aaron looked embarrassed.

“We called it the dead half-second problem.”

Kade stared.

“That is terrible.”

“We were tired.”

“It is also not half a second.”

“We were very tired.”

Kade laughed despite himself.

Then the humor fell away.

The old log contained six instances.

Every one occurred when the Refuge Node's internal suppression system and a human predictive control model both attempted to model the other's next correction.

Not when humans observed it.

Not when the Node transmitted.

When each side tried to predict the predictor.

The first interval measured 0.84 seconds.

The second 0.82.

The third 0.83.

Kade stopped breathing normally.

“You knew.”

Aaron looked at him.

“I knew the interval existed.”

“You knew it was point-eight-three.”

“Yes.”

“And you did not tell us before the new pulse.”

Aaron's face changed.

There.

Old anger waiting.

“I had not seen the number in seventeen years.”

“You remembered enough to be afraid.”

“Yes.”

“Then why not say it?”

“Because when I woke after containment, everyone was trying to turn my fragments into answers.”

Kade stood.

Aaron did not stop him.

“I am not everyone.”

“No.”

“Then stop treating me like I am.”

Aaron's hands tightened on the edge of the paper.

“I was trying not to do the opposite.”

“What opposite?”

“Hand you every old fear I had and let you inherit it as fact.”

Kade looked at him.

That was annoyingly reasonable.

It did not make him less angry.

“Then label it.”

Aaron frowned.

“What?”

“Memory. Inference. Guess. Regret. Put the boundary on it and tell me anyway.”

Aaron went very still.

Kade heard his own voice in the sentence.

Mara's too.

Helix.

Kiyomizu.

Everywhere.

Aaron looked at the old log again.

“Fine.”

Kade almost objected to the word.

Did not.

Aaron pointed to the first three events.

“Memory: these happened during coupled prediction tests.”

Then the fourth.

“Memory: the Node increased internal suppression after the interval.”

The fifth.

“Memory: one human operator reported the system felt farther away afterward.”

The sixth.

Aaron stopped.

Kade waited.

“Inference,” Aaron said. “We thought the interval was the Node rejecting deeper coupling.”

“Now?”

“Inference: it may have been a challenge condition rather than rejection.”

Kade's pulse changed.

“Challenge from the Node?”

“I do not know.”

“From something else?”

“I do not know.”

“Good.”

Aaron looked at him.

“That word is becoming aggressive.”

“Family trait.”

---

Petrov refused to let them run a live test with people.

Kade agreed before she finished explaining why.

That worried him.

The approved test used stored-state systems only.

Three institutions.

Three architectures.

Helix recursive prediction.

Vahana transfer-state simulation.

Kiyomizu relational model.

No active Consensus.

No human interiority.

No signal transmission.

The question was narrower than everyone wanted.

Could a deliberately constructed recursive relationship reproduce the old interval without the external source doing anything first?

Helix went first.

Jonas built two models that predicted each other's prediction error.

At low gain, nothing happened.

At medium gain, oscillation.

At high gain, the system became unstable and shut itself down conventionally.

No missing interval.

“Good,” Kade said.

Jonas looked offended.

“You wanted failure?”

“I wanted not-evidence.”

“Scientifically rude.”

Vahana went second.

Arjun configured a transfer simulation where two autonomous schedulers changed handoff timing based on their predictions of each other's predicted margin.

Again, no interval.

Latency rose.

One scheduler overcorrected.

No 0.83-second gap.

Aya watched from Kiyomizu.

“Maybe the condition requires an external reference.”

Kade said, “Maybe the condition does not exist in our test.”

“Also true.”

Mara looked at him.

He sighed.

“Two things can be true.”

“Unfortunately.”

Kiyomizu went third.

Aya refused to increase gain.

Instead she changed topology.

The two models did not predict each other directly.

Each predicted the relation between them.

Then each changed behavior from the predicted relation.

A third layer compared how the relation prediction changed after each adjustment.

Kade felt his stomach tighten.

Not because he understood every equation.

Because the shape looked familiar.

Not star.

Not pair.

Relationship becoming object.

The system ran for nine seconds.

Ten.

Eleven.

Then every local process continued while the relation layer stopped producing a unique state.

Timer:

**0.79 s**

Then resolution returned.

Nobody spoke.

Aya ended the test immediately.

No repeat.

Good.

Petrov asked, “Did the external source change?”

Lunar answered.

“No.”

Kade exhaled.

Then Nessa said, “Wait.”

The room froze.

A pulse appeared on the avoided-reference monitor.

Not the same as before.

Weaker.

Shorter.

Detected by Lunar first.

Vahana second.

Helix third.

No semantic structure.

Duration:

**0.80 s**

Kade looked at Aya's finished test.

Mara's face went still.

Petrov said, “Stop all recursive tests.”

No one argued.

That mattered.

The source had not reacted to Helix's high gain.

Not to Vahana's transfer loop.

It reacted—if reaction was the right word—only after Kiyomizu created the relational topology that produced the interval.

Still correlation.

Not causation.

But different correlation than yesterday.

Kade felt fear arrive cleanly.

Not because the source had spoken.

Because something about their test might have been legible.

---

Park ended Aaron's day after twenty-six minutes of a thirty-minute allowance.

He complained.

She ignored him.

Kade walked with Aaron as far as the recovery-room door.

Aaron looked tired.

Not dramatically.

Humanly.

Kade almost asked another question.

Stopped.

Aaron saw.

“Ask.”

Kade shook his head.

“Tomorrow.”

Aaron considered that.

Then nodded.

“Tomorrow.”

Good.

A promise neither of them had to complete tonight.

Kade returned to Meridian Commons at 17:40.

The emergency-center proposal from that morning had been revised twice.

The new version added withdrawal tests and local veto checks.

Taren had annotated three pages without using hidden access.

Mara was in class.

Eli had labeled a whiteboard:

**THINGS THAT CONTINUED DURING THE INTERVAL**

Under it:

local clocks
manual authority
physical transfer
isolated receivers
hospital stabilization
paper counts
students being annoying

Kade added:

**ARGUMENTS**

Eli looked at him.

“Those increased.”

“Important resilience metric.”

Kade sat.

The old interval existed before official Contact Zero.

The Refuge Node had encountered it.

Earth could reproduce a close version without the source.

And the avoided direction had produced another pulse only after one specific relational topology formed.

Not enough to name the thing.

Enough to stop pretending the pulse was merely a message.

It behaved like a test that cared about structure.

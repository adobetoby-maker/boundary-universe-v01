# Chapter 2 — Kisiwa Time

Sera heard the part where a defense representative tried to place Kade under protective control through a speaker balanced on the response-vehicle dashboard.

She had a more immediate problem.

An ambulance needed to move through water.

The response vehicle stopped two blocks from East District General because the road had become a brown channel filled with branches, plastic containers, and one abandoned scooter.

Rain struck the roof hard enough to flatten conversation.

Imani Mwangi looked through the windshield.

“Depth?”

The driver checked the curb markers.

“Twenty-eight centimeters. Rising slowly.”

“Current?”

“Low here. Faster at the crossing.”

“Alternative?”

“North route adds eleven minutes.”

The ambulance behind them carried a patient whose transfer notes said eleven minutes mattered.

Sera waited.

She was the visiting trainee.

Imani owned the response call.

That did not make Sera decorative. It made her responsible for speaking when she had something useful and quiet when she did not.

The placement was improving her quiet.

Slowly.

Sera looked at the buildings along the flooded block.

“Service lane behind the pharmacy?”

The driver shook his head.

“Retaining wall came down across it.”

That was the collapse she had heard through the first-contact session.

No injuries reported.

One route removed.

Sera studied the water again.

“Can the ambulance clear this depth?”

“Yes,” the driver said. “If the crossing stays below thirty-five.”

Imani asked, “Can we know that for the next four minutes?”

The driver checked the municipal flow layer.

It had stopped updating.

Of course.

The universe could send an undecodable pulse from outside the solar system. A blocked storm drain could still defeat a road.

Professor Kamau's voice came over the response channel.

“The upstream gauge is live. Local display only.”

Sera looked at Imani.

Imani handed her the portable radio.

Not command.

A task.

Sera contacted the Kisiwa trainee stationed at the gauge.

“Read every twenty seconds. Numbers only unless the trend changes.”

“Current depth thirty-one.”

The vehicle waited.

“Thirty-one.”

Rain.

“Thirty-two.”

The ambulance lights turned the water red, then dark, then red.

“Thirty-two.”

Imani said, “Move.”

The response vehicle entered first.

Water pushed against the tires.

Not much.

Enough that Sera felt every change through the floor.

The driver kept a steady line. The ambulance followed inside the wake.

At the crossing, the current pulled harder.

Sera held the radio.

She did not reach for her field.

Redirecting water around two vehicles without a complete drainage model would move the problem toward a building, a person, or a road edge she could not see.

The vehicle climbed the far side.

The ambulance followed.

“Thirty-three,” the gauge trainee said.

Too late to matter.

Good.

At the hospital entrance, three orderlies took the patient.

No one asked Sera about the end of civilization.

They asked her to help carry an oxygen case.

She did.

---

East District General had not lost power.

It had lost certainty about how much power it could promise.

The municipal feeder remained live. The hospital battery held eighty-one percent charge. Two generators were available.

One had failed its last full-load test.

The other shared fuel storage with the ambulance depot.

Prototype Four sat between the hospital's three stabilization points and tried to keep small transfer faults from becoming large ones.

It was doing exactly what Eli designed it to do.

Locally.

The first unit smoothed the imaging wing load.

The second protected intensive care from feeder noise.

The third watched the battery transition bus.

None had lost state during the pulse.

Sera had told the session that.

It was true.

It was not complete.

A power technician named Daniel Otieno showed her the regional confidence line.

“This number is wrong.”

The display showed sixty-four percent handoff confidence.

“Why?” Sera asked.

“Because Unit Two believes Unit Three has confirmed battery reserve.”

“Has it?”

“Unit Three confirmed its own state.”

“Not the reserve?”

“The reserve estimate comes from Unit One.”

Sera looked at the three traces.

Each local unit was valid.

The shared confidence was built from what each unit predicted the others could carry if its own load changed.

Prototype Four did not join minds.

It did not form Consensus.

It barely deserved the word network beside the systems Helix and Vahana operated.

But each unit modeled the others.

The others changed their reserve behavior from those models.

“When did confidence fall?” Sera asked.

Daniel expanded the trace.

One gap.

Eight hundred thirty milliseconds.

Sera went still.

“Local histories?”

“Complete.”

“Physical transfers?”

“Complete.”

“Any command during the gap?”

“No command.”

“Then why is the current number wrong?”

Daniel pointed at Unit Two.

“After the gap, it rebuilt from the last confirmed relationship state. Unit Three had changed battery reserve during the interval. Unit Two never resolved why.”

“So it reduced confidence.”

“Correct response.”

Sera looked toward the intensive-care corridor.

“And if it had increased confidence?”

Daniel did not answer immediately.

“Then the imaging wing might have received capacity we needed to reserve for intensive care.”

Might.

Not did.

The system had failed safe.

This time.

Imani entered the power room carrying two paper route boards that had gone soft at the edges from rain.

Sera showed her the trace.

Imani read it once.

“Disconnect the confidence layer.”

Daniel nodded.

Sera said, “Wait.”

Imani looked at her.

Sera heard how the word sounded from a visiting trainee to the person who owned the response.

“Reason?” Imani asked.

Good.

Sera pointed at the units.

“If we disconnect before preserving the relationship state, we lose the best civic comparison anyone has.”

“And if we preserve it live?”

“We risk another wrong confidence value.”

Imani waited.

Sera hated when competent people made her finish thinking.

“Freeze the confidence layer,” she said. “Do not let it issue handoff advice. Keep local stabilization active. Duplicate the trace to a physically separate recorder. Daniel owns the power decision.”

Daniel said, “I already own the power decision.”

Sera looked at him.

“Good correction.”

Imani almost smiled.

“Do it.”

The confidence line froze.

The three stabilizers continued working.

Daniel called the imaging supervisor and moved nonurgent scans by twenty minutes.

No Consensus.

No global dashboard.

No elegant prediction.

A person who knew the hospital made a decision with incomplete information.

Intensive care retained its reserve.

---

Petrov's office requested Sera's presence at the technical session four times.

The first request marked her as a Twelve participant.

The second marked her as the Kisiwa liaison.

The third called her a civilization-scale shared-state authority.

Sera showed that one to Imani.

“Congratulations,” Imani said. “You have become exhausting at scale.”

“I had help.”

The fourth request asked for a five-minute factual report when local response allowed.

Better.

Sera accepted that one.

Local response did not allow for another forty-three minutes.

The retaining-wall collapse had broken a water service line. The hospital had pressure, but two nearby blocks did not. A school shelter needed sanitation capacity. The northern ambulance route had begun taking overflow traffic.

Professor Kamau assigned teams.

Municipal water crews owned the pipe.

Traffic staff owned the route.

Kisiwa trainees carried equipment, confirmed building access, and used Resonance only where the crews requested it.

Sera and Imani went to the school shelter.

Children sat in a cafeteria with blankets, juice cartons, and the furious energy of people told to remain still.

A staff member had already made a hand-painted sign for the wash stations.

The digital occupancy system said the cafeteria held eighty-six people.

The paper list said ninety-one.

Sera asked which number controlled food distribution.

“Paper,” the shelter lead said.

“Good.”

“Digital says it is current.”

“Digital is wrong.”

The shelter lead looked at her.

“I like you.”

They counted again.

Ninety-two.

One child had been in the washroom during both earlier counts.

The world remained difficult without assistance from anything beyond the solar system.

Sera carried water containers.

Imani negotiated a delivery route.

A Kisiwa first-year named Laila found a gravity-feed option that reduced pump demand.

The shelter lead found six more clean containers in a locked kitchen store.

No one became the center.

The problem became smaller.

That was enough.

---

Sera joined the technical session from the school loading bay.

She had five minutes.

Petrov gave her six.

Kade, Mara, Aya, Eli, Arjun, Jonas, and two people Sera did not recognize occupied the display.

Eli saw her expression.

“Prototype Four is not broken.”

“I know.”

“I wanted that established before your tone injured me.”

“Your machine made a correct local decision.”

Eli relaxed slightly.

“Its confidence layer lost the same interval,” Sera said.

He stopped relaxing.

She explained the three units.

Local state complete.

Physical transfer complete.

Relationship resolution absent for 0.83 seconds.

Failed-safe confidence reduction afterward.

Imaging delayed.

Intensive-care reserve protected.

Human authority retained.

Jonas asked, “Was the confidence layer recursively predictive?”

“Each unit estimated the others' reserve and changed its own behavior from their estimates.”

“Then yes.”

Kade said, “It was not in shared state.”

“No.”

“No Resonant operators?”

“No operators connected to it.”

Mara looked at the trace.

“So this is not specific to Consensus.”

“It is not specific to people,” Sera said.

Silence.

That landed harder than she expected.

Aya recovered first.

“What continued?”

Sera liked the question.

“Every local stabilizer. The hospital feeder. The battery bus. Manual authority. Voice communication. Paper routing. A gravity-fed wash station.”

One of the officials said, “The wash station is not technically relevant.”

Sera looked into the camera.

“It is to the ninety-two people using it.”

Petrov said, “Keep it in the record.”

Good.

Eli asked, “Can you send me the trace?”

Sera looked at Daniel, who stood outside the camera view.

He nodded once.

“The hospital will send it under its own provenance,” she said.

Not hers.

Not Eli's.

Not Asterion's.

The hospital's.

Eli understood.

“Thank you.”

Petrov asked, “Your conclusion?”

Sera looked at the loading bay.

Rain had weakened to a steady tapping on the metal roof. Imani was rechecking the next route. Laila carried empty containers back toward the kitchen. The shelter lead had crossed out ninety-one and written ninety-two in thick marker.

“The missing interval can reach ordinary coordination systems,” Sera said. “It does not need Consensus. It does not need a Zero. It does not need an elite network.”

Kade's face tightened at the word ordinary.

He was already understanding the danger.

Sera continued.

“But the local systems kept working because they were allowed to be local. The hospital did not need the confidence layer to authorize Daniel. The shelter did not need the occupancy system to count people. We lost prediction. We did not lose the work.”

Aya said, “Lower coherence reduced the failure boundary.”

“Maybe,” Sera said. “Or the people here already knew not to confuse advice with authority.”

Professor Kamau would probably approve.

Then correct her wording.

Petrov used the final minute to change two decisions.

All civilian stabilization pilots would preserve local authority during recursive-layer review.

No research suspension order could disable a live civic system without approval from the service that depended on it.

Not a complete answer.

A better boundary.

The call ended.

Sera went back to work.

---

They returned to the Nairobi campus after dark.

The response vehicle smelled like wet fabric, mud, and the food Imani had made everyone stop to buy because competence did not replace dinner.

Professor Kamau waited in the operations room.

She reviewed the hospital trace, the shelter report, and the ambulance crossing.

“Why did you not use Resonance on the flooded road?” she asked Sera.

“I could not see where redirected water would resolve.”

“Why freeze Prototype Four's confidence layer instead of disconnecting all three units?”

“Local stabilization was helping. The relationship model was uncertain, not every component.”

“Why did you tell the technical session about the wash station?”

Sera looked at her.

“Because they were starting to discuss civilization as if it were a network diagram.”

Kamau nodded.

No praise.

Good.

“And what did you miss?”

There it was.

Sera looked again at the ambulance record.

Imani answered before she could.

“The crossing decision depended on one trainee reading an upstream gauge by radio.”

Sera saw it.

“One person became mandatory.”

“For four minutes,” Imani said. “With no replacement.”

“The ambulance still crossed.”

Kamau looked at Sera.

“Success is when hidden dependencies become hardest to notice.”

Sera wrote it down.

Not because it was profound.

Because tomorrow they would assign a second gauge reader and a fallback marker at the crossing.

The pulse from the avoided direction remained important.

So did the route.

Kade sent her a message while she cleaned mud from her boots.

**YOU WERE RIGHT TO STAY ON THE SHIFT.**

Sera answered.

**I KNOW.**

Then, because he was learning:

**YOU WERE RIGHT TO ASK WHAT KEPT WORKING.**

She considered that.

**MARA ASKED FIRST.**

His reply arrived immediately.

**I AM AWARE.**

Sera smiled.

Across the equipment room, Prototype Four's local units showed green.

Its regional confidence layer remained frozen.

Not broken.

Not trusted beyond what it knew.

Outside, rainwater moved through drains, gutters, soil, pipes, streets, and the temporary channels people had made for it.

No single route carried everything.

The city continued.

# Chapter 5 — Low Coherence

The first visibility trial used less power than a cafeteria microwave.

Kade found that reassuring for approximately eleven seconds.

Then the source ignored it.

Helix had built the test around three systems.

System One transmitted a strong, simple calibration pattern with no recursive modeling.

System Two used one tenth the power but kept a persistent model of three external operators and updated itself from their predicted reactions.

System Three used almost no external transmission at all. It ran internally, maintaining a continuously refreshed model of six agents modeling one another.

No Consensus.

No people sharing interior state.

No live human decision authority.

Just architectures.

Jonas stood beside the control rack in Helix's main systems hall.

“Power levels verified.”

Mara checked the independent meter.

“Verified.”

Aya checked Kiyomizu's mirror instrumentation.

“Topology states verified.”

Kade stood behind the observer line because nobody had given him an operational job.

He hated how healthy that felt.

Petrov's voice came through the room speaker.

“Proceed System One.”

The high-power calibration pulse transmitted.

Lunar watched the avoided direction.

Nothing.

Ten seconds.

Thirty.

One minute.

No response.

System One stopped.

System Two activated.

Lower power.

Persistent prediction.

The model adjusted itself every two hundred milliseconds based on predicted operator reactions.

At twelve seconds, nothing.

At nineteen, Lunar reported a weak timing disturbance.

Not a pulse.

Not yet.

At twenty-four seconds, the disturbance sharpened.

Duration:

**0.82 s**

System Two stopped automatically.

The disturbance vanished.

Kade's mouth went dry.

Jonas said, “Power was eleven percent of System One.”

Mara answered, “So power is not sufficient.”

Aya said, “Not sufficient is not irrelevant.”

Good.

Nobody overclaimed.

System Three activated.

No external transmission beyond ordinary synchronization traffic.

Its six-agent model maintained continuous mutual prediction.

Eight seconds.

Nine.

The avoided monitor spiked.

A pulse.

Weak.

But real.

Lunar confirmed direction.

Vahana confirmed relay integrity.

Helix confirmed local instruments had not generated it.

Duration:

**0.84 s**

Petrov said, “Stop.”

System Three dissolved.

The pulse ended three hundred milliseconds later.

Nobody spoke.

Kade looked at the power meter.

System Three had used less external transmission than his room terminal during a video call.

The source had cared more.

If care was the word.

It probably was not.

---

The Kisiwa comparison happened six hours later.

Not in a lab.

Sera refused that framing.

Kisiwa used three live civic systems already doing useful work.

A hospital load-sharing mesh.

A flood-routing coordination board.

A transit dispatch model.

All three had predictive elements.

None maintained persistent high-coherence mutual models.

Sera stood in East District General beside Daniel Otieno while Helix and Asterion watched remotely.

“You are not changing hospital operations to make your experiment clean,” she said.

Jonas looked mildly offended through the screen.

“We know.”

“Good.”

The hospital mesh predicted local demand and neighboring reserve.

It did not predict the predicting process itself.

The flood-routing board predicted road state and pump capacity.

It did not maintain a model of how operators would alter routes because they knew they were being modeled.

Transit dispatch predicted congestion.

Drivers still ignored it when street conditions disagreed.

Low coherence.

Messy.

Useful.

Petrov authorized observation only.

No induced load.

No forced recursion.

They watched for forty minutes.

Nothing from the avoided direction.

A storm cell moved over Nairobi.

The hospital shifted imaging load.

Traffic rerouted around standing water.

Two buses fell behind schedule.

No pulse.

Sera looked at Kade's remote window.

“Still want to centralize everything because it is cleaner?”

Kade frowned.

“That was not my plan.”

“You liked the plan.”

“I understood the plan.”

“That is politician language.”

“Cruel.”

“Accurate.”

Daniel looked between them.

“You two do this during civilization-scale research?”

“Yes,” Sera said.

“No,” Kade said.

Daniel nodded.

“Helpful.”

The avoided monitor remained quiet.

That quiet had become data.

---

Governments reacted exactly as badly as everyone expected.

Some saw the Helix result and demanded immediate suspension of all recursive shared-state research.

Others argued the opposite: if the source reacted to high-coherence models, Earth needed one centralized model large enough to predict and counter it.

Kade read that proposal twice.

Then once more because he disliked how much he understood it.

Julian joined him in Asterion's west study hall.

“What are you reading?”

Kade turned the tablet.

Julian read the summary.

**PLANETARY PREDICTIVE DEFENSE CORE**

He stopped at the title.

“No.”

“That was fast.”

“Yes.”

“It would model source behavior across every detector.”

“Yes.”

“Could predict timing changes.”

“Maybe.”

“Could coordinate local response.”

“So can local response.”

Kade leaned back.

“You sound like Sera.”

“Terrifying.”

Julian kept reading.

The design was competent.

That was the problem.

Minimum-state feeds.

Distributed sensors.

Redundant operators.

A permanent predictive core integrating all of it.

Not command authority.

Prediction authority.

Kade could see how that distinction would disappear during fear.

Julian said, “What happens if it is wrong?”

“Local systems could override.”

“Would they?”

Kade looked at him.

Julian continued.

“If the global model has better data, more compute, more history, and emergency authority to publish recommendations, how long before refusing it becomes negligence?”

Kade hated the question.

Because it was not about evil people.

It was about incentives.

The center could remain technically advisory and become practically mandatory.

Mara entered with two books under one arm.

She saw the title.

“No.”

Kade sighed.

“Everyone is decisive today.”

Mara sat.

“Show me the withdrawal test.”

“There isn't one yet.”

“Then it is not a safety architecture.”

Julian nodded.

Kade looked at both of them.

“You coordinated this?”

“No,” Mara said.

“Yes,” Julian said.

Mara looked at him.

Julian smiled.

Kade felt better.

Normal disagreement still existed.

---

The second Helix trial changed only persistence.

Same models.

Same power.

Same prediction quality target.

System Two would form for five seconds, dissolve completely for five, then reform from local state.

No memory carried across the separation except signed external records.

Aya designed the release condition.

Mara audited it.

Jonas complained that Kiyomizu had made his beautiful model intentionally forgetful.

Aya said, “Correct.”

The test began.

Five seconds coherent.

Release.

Nothing from the source.

Five seconds local.

Reform.

Nothing.

Release.

Third cycle.

A faint timing disturbance appeared at four-point-eight seconds.

The system dissolved.

The disturbance vanished.

No full pulse.

Fourth cycle.

Same.

Fifth.

Nothing.

Jonas stared at the trace.

Kade asked, “What changed?”

“Prediction accuracy stayed within two percent of the persistent run.”

“Power?”

“Same.”

“External traffic?”

“Same.”

Aya said, “Persistence changed.”

Mara added, “And the system proved separation repeatedly.”

Kade looked at the avoided monitor.

The source had reacted strongly to a continuous internal model with almost no transmission.

Weakly to a lower-power predictive system.

Barely to a system that repeatedly became coherent and then genuinely stopped being one system.

Not proof of motive.

Proof of discrimination.

It cared about structure more than strength.

Or it measured structure more easily than strength.

Either way, power was the wrong axis.

Again.

The universe seemed committed to humiliating ladders.

---

Kade and Mara argued after dinner.

Not about whether the result mattered.

About what to do with it.

Kade wanted a seventy-two-hour pause on all high-coherence research.

Mara wanted only persistent architectures paused, with temporary-cell testing continuing under strict limits.

“You want to keep poking it,” Kade said.

“I want to know whether release is protective before governments ban the only thing that might be.”

“And if the testing itself increases attention?”

“Then the stop conditions catch it.”

“Maybe.”

“Yes.”

“You sound comfortable with maybe.”

“I am comfortable saying maybe when maybe is true.”

Kade looked toward the Commons window.

Thirty days had become twenty-nine.

The countdown continued.

“I hate that the safest-looking action might be the thing that makes us easier to classify.”

Mara's expression softened by one degree.

“Me too.”

“That was almost agreement.”

“No.”

“Recovered.”

They sat in silence for a minute.

Not resolved.

Still connected.

Good.

Mara finally said, “Your pause should include the planetary defense core.”

Kade looked at her.

“Obviously.”

“Put it in writing.”

“Why?”

“Because if you only restrict research and leave emergency centralization exempt, governments will build the dangerous topology under a different label.”

There.

The same lesson as the legacy map.

Names did not matter enough.

Shape did.

Kade opened the draft protocol.

He added:

**PERSISTENT HIGH-COHERENCE ARCHITECTURES ARE PAUSED REGARDLESS OF WHETHER THEIR STATED PURPOSE IS RESEARCH, DEFENSE, GOVERNANCE, OR CIVIC COORDINATION.**

Mara read it.

“Better.”

“Praise?”

“Do not make it unpleasant.”

---

At 23:40, Kisiwa sent the final comparison packet.

Forty minutes of hospital, flood, and transit data.

No pulse.

No unresolved interval.

No evidence of source attention.

The systems were not primitive.

They were not weak.

They simply did not insist on remaining mutually predictive at all times.

Sera attached one note.

**LOW COHERENCE IS NOT LOW CAPABILITY.**

Kade read it twice.

That sentence belonged in the book of things humans would later pretend had been obvious.

He forwarded it to Petrov.

Then to Julian.

Then to Eli.

Then stopped himself before sending it to everyone he knew.

Mara would accuse him of turning insight into infrastructure.

Correctly.

The avoided direction remained quiet.

For now.

The strongest transmission had been ignored.

The weakest high-coherence model had been noticed.

And the systems most willing to become separate again had drawn the least response.

Whatever waited in the wrong direction was not measuring how loudly Earth spoke.

It was measuring how difficult Earth was becoming to separate.

# Chapter 13 — Cross Examination

Julian Cross beat Kade eleven times before breakfast.

The first took nineteen seconds.

The second took twenty-three.

The third took fifteen because Kade became irritated by the first two and attempted something ambitious.

By the fifth, he understood why Rank One was Rank One.

Julian did not look fast.

He removed decisions.

Every step closed an option. Every feint moved Kade toward a worse answer. Every small pressure forced a response Julian had already planned for. By the time the final strike arrived, losing had happened several seconds earlier.

It was deeply annoying.

The side room was only twelve meters square. A blue line divided the mat, and a low padded column stood near each corner to make straight retreat impossible. Kade had assumed the columns created options.

Julian used them to take options away.

On the fourth reset, Kade circled toward the nearest column and waited for Julian to close. The space narrowed exactly as Kade wanted. One lane left, one likely entry, no room for Julian's longer reach to matter.

Julian stepped into the lane.

Kade turned off the column and drove his shoulder toward Julian's center.

Julian was no longer there.

His hand caught Kade's forearm. A small Redirection pulse changed the angle of Kade's own momentum—not enough to trip a safety limit, only enough to make the planted foot arrive crooked. Julian's heel blocked the recovery step.

The mat struck Kade's back.

“You gave me the wall,” Julian said.

Kade stared up at him. “I used the wall.”

“You used it first. Then you left it to me.”

The distinction remained irritating through reset five.

On reset six, Kade tried waiting.

Julian waited better.

On reset seven, Kade attacked first.

Julian used the attack to decide where Kade would be standing three exchanges later.

On reset eight, Kade attempted to become unpredictable.

Julian swept him in fourteen seconds.

“You looked pleased,” Kade said from the floor.

“I enjoy when opponents announce strategy with facial expressions.”

“I did not.”

“You did.”

“Traitorous face.”

On reset nine, Kade lasted thirty-one seconds.

Ten lasted forty-two.

Eleven ended when Julian placed Kade exactly where Kade had spent the entire exchange trying not to go.

On the twelfth reset, Kade remained on the mat.

Julian offered a hand.

Kade looked at it.

“Is this part of the strategy?”

“Yes. I exhaust opponents through courtesy.”

Kade laughed and took it.

They were alone in a side training room before first period. No spectators. No ladder consequences. The safety system recorded only injury-relevant telemetry.

Julian had invited Kade after the Broken Floor video escaped.

Kade had expected questions.

What did you feel?

What can you do?

Was the crack real?

Instead, Julian had watched one ordinary match and said, “Your footwork is improving, but you still solve the problem you are currently in instead of the next one.”

Then he had hit Kade eleven times.

“Again?” Julian asked.

“No.”

“Good.”

Kade blinked.

“You are supposed to say perseverance builds character.”

“Perseverance while exhausted builds bad habits.”

“That sounds annoyingly reasonable.”

“It often is.”

Julian sat against the wall.

Kade joined him.

The training room smelled faintly of mat cleaner and warm composite. Outside the glass, morning light had barely reached the courtyard.

For a minute, neither spoke.

Kade liked silence more around Julian than he expected. Julian did not seem to treat it as an invitation to ask personal questions.

Then Julian said, “You’re trying to understand why I’m helping.”

Kade looked over.

“I was being subtle.”

“You weren’t.”

“Tragic.”

Julian drank water.

“I saw the Meridian Night thing with Reid.”

“The dumpling rescue.”

“I prefer that version.”

“You still have not answered.”

Julian looked across the empty mat.

“Because everyone here needs someone who can beat them.”

Kade waited.

“That sounds like rank-one philosophy.”

“It means if nobody exposes your weaknesses, you start protecting them instead of fixing them.”

Kade thought about Northline.

Ms. Alvarez.

Sera telling him his feet were bad.

Mara tapping his ankle every time he planted early.

Park listing deficiencies like groceries.

“Who beats you?” he asked.

Julian smiled.

“Upper-years. Frequently.”

“That is comforting.”

“It should be.”

“Do you enjoy it?”

“No.”

That answer came immediately.

Kade looked at him.

Julian shrugged.

“I enjoy learning. Losing is often how the lesson arrives. I do not have to enjoy delivery.”

There.

Competitive.

Not saintly.

Much better.

“Why me?” Kade asked.

Julian’s expression changed slightly.

“Because your improvement curve is steep.”

“That sounds flattering.”

“It is also inconvenient.”

Kade smiled.

Julian continued.

“If you become good, I want to understand you before I have to fight you in a bracket.”

“Finally. Selfishness.”

“Did that make you more comfortable?”

“Considerably.”

Julian laughed.

“One more thing,” he said.

Kade looked at the mat.

“You said no more.”

“No more fighting.”

Julian opened the wall display.

All eleven losses appeared in a grid.

Kade considered leaving anyway.

Julian selected the first recording. Two small figures took their marks inside a top-down diagram of the room. Kade's path glowed orange. Julian's glowed white.

The replay ended after nineteen seconds.

“When did you lose?” Julian asked.

“When you hit me.”

“Earlier.”

Kade dragged the replay backward to Julian's final entry.

“Here.”

“Earlier.”

He moved back another two seconds. His orange line bent toward the north corner. Julian's white line shortened the angle.

“Here?”

Julian shook his head.

Kade returned the recording to the start.

For five seconds, nothing looked decisive. They traded position. Kade tested the left lane. Julian gave up a fraction of center.

Kade paused.

“You wanted me to take that.”

“Why?”

“Because the column narrowed my exit.”

“And?”

Kade advanced one frame at a time. His lead foot turned toward the gap. Julian had not yet moved to close it.

“I committed before you did.”

“That is where you lost.”

The timestamp read six point four seconds.

Kade stared at the remaining thirteen seconds of the match.

“Then why not finish sooner?”

“You still had choices. None of them could give you the position you wanted, but some could make me pay more for taking it.”

Julian played the second loss.

“Again.”

Kade found the commitment at eight seconds.

The third was humiliating. He had lost at four point one, the instant he launched the ambitious attack.

The fourth took longer to diagnose. On the fifth, Kade blamed the corner again and Julian made him keep searching until he found the moment he had sacrificed distance to protect against a strike Julian never intended to throw.

By the eighth recording, Kade stopped tracking attacks.

He tracked promises.

Every planted foot promised a direction. Every raised guard promised which opening mattered most. Every retreat toward one lane promised the next retreat could still exist.

Julian did not win by predicting the future.

He collected promises and arranged for Kade to break the wrong one.

They reached reset nine.

The longest match so far.

Kade paused at twenty-six seconds.

“There.”

Julian leaned closer.

“Why?”

“You stepped wide.”

“I stepped wide four times.”

“Not like that.”

Kade enlarged Julian's route. At twenty-six seconds, the white line moved farther from center than it had in every prior exchange.

“I made you protect the boundary.”

Julian's eyes narrowed at the image.

“Continue.”

Kade played three seconds. Julian recovered center. The opening vanished.

“You still won,” Kade said. “But that was the first time you had to spend position to answer me.”

Julian replayed the three seconds twice.

The room became quiet except for the soft squeak of students' shoes in the corridor outside.

“I should have changed levels,” Kade said.

“Probably.”

“You didn't expect me to find that.”

Julian glanced over.

“I expected you to find where you lost.”

“That was not where I lost.”

“No.”

Kade pointed at the white line.

“That was where you almost did.”

Something competitive and delighted sharpened Julian's face.

There it was.

The reason for the invitation.

Julian duplicated the frame and sent it to Kade's tablet.

“Bring me an answer next time.”

“To your footwork?”

“To those three seconds.”

Kade stood.

His legs protested.

“What do I get if it works?”

“Three more seconds.”

“Generous.”

“At your current rate, extremely.”

Kade collected his bag.

At the door, he turned.

Julian still had the ninth recording open. He was studying his own wide step now, not Kade's mistake.

Rank One had invited Kade because everyone needed someone who could beat them.

Kade could not do that yet.

But Julian had found one question Kade could make him answer.

That was enough to begin.

They trained twice more that week.

Julian never asked about Zero Protocol.

Never asked about the floor.

Never asked what Kade could do.

He asked why Kade stepped left under pressure.

Why he held his breath before counters.

Why his first plan in almost every exchange lasted less than two seconds.

Why he interpreted structure as a trap.

The last question bothered Kade.

They were working through a simple scenario: Julian held center; Kade had to create an opening without leaving the legal engagement zone.

Kade improvised three different entries.

Julian stopped all three.

“Again,” Julian said.

Kade reset.

“I need a different entry.”

“No.”

“You stopped that one.”

“Yes.”

“So—”

“Use it again.”

“That seems stupid.”

“Use it differently.”

Kade frowned.

They started.

Same first step.

Julian responded the same way.

Kade changed the second step.

Julian adjusted and stopped him.

Again.

Same first step.

Same initial response.

Kade changed the third choice.

This time he gained half a meter before Julian turned him out.

“Again,” Julian said.

By the sixth repetition, Kade was angry.

“Plans break.”

Julian did not move from his starting position.

“So?”

“So improvisation doesn’t.”

Julian stared at him.

“That may be the most incorrect confident statement I have heard this month.”

Kade smiled.

Julian pointed at him.

“That.”

“What?”

“You do that when you don’t want to answer.”

The smile disappeared.

Julian stepped out of stance.

“You are good at adapting because you have spent years assuming the environment will become hostile.”

Kade’s shoulders tightened.

“You a therapist now?”

“No. I fight people.”

“Comforting distinction.”

“But you plan like trusting the plan makes you vulnerable.”

Kade looked away.

Julian waited.

No follow-up question.

No forced insight.

That was somehow worse.

Kade looked back at the engagement zone.

“At school, plans were usually somebody else deciding what I was supposed to do.”

Julian nodded once.

“That explains disliking other people’s plans.”

“Thank you.”

“It does not explain refusing your own.”

Kade stared at him.

Julian lifted his hands.

“Again.”

This time Kade used the same opening seven times.

On the eighth, Julian anticipated the adjustment Kade had used on six.

Kade did not make it.

He stayed with the original plan one beat longer.

Julian’s weight shifted toward an answer Kade never gave him.

For the first time, Julian had to change his feet instead of Kade changing his.

Kade entered.

Julian blocked, rotated, and still put him on the mat.

But it took two minutes.

Kade lay there breathing hard.

Julian stood over him.

Then nodded once.

Kade had learned enough Asterion language to recognize praise when it tried not to look like praise.

“You almost had me,” Kade said.

Julian laughed.

“Never say that after losing.”

“I’m experimenting with confidence.”

“Needs calibration.”

Julian walked to the wall display and drew three white marks in a row.

“Tell me your next opening.”

Kade pushed himself upright. “That ruins the opening.”

“Only if it depends on surprise.”

“Surprise is useful.”

“So are floors. You should not need either one to cooperate.”

Kade looked at the three marks.

“Step outside your lead foot. Force the guard high. Enter under the right arm.”

Julian added a branch beneath the second mark.

“If I keep the guard low?”

“Change level and turn you toward the boundary.”

Another branch.

“If I yield the center?”

“Take it.”

Julian capped the stylus.

“That is a plan.”

“That is a list of things you now know.”

“Correct. Use it anyway.”

They reset.

Kade made the first step.

Julian's guard rose before Kade touched him. The prepared answer was waiting so visibly it looked like a dare.

Kade almost abandoned the entry.

Then he remembered Julian moving the same way seven times and changing the eighth.

The plan was not a tunnel. It was a position from which several roads remained available.

Kade forced the guard higher, entered under the right arm, and took center.

Julian yielded it.

Kade followed too fast.

Julian turned him and drove him toward the blue line.

The third branch.

Kade stopped fighting for center. He changed levels, caught Julian's hip, and used the turn Julian had begun.

For one bright second, Rank One had both feet outside the engagement zone.

Then Julian twisted in the air, touched one hand to the mat, and landed with a single foot inside blue.

The safety tone stayed silent.

Kade had no time to admire it. Julian came up behind him and locked the match.

“Out,” Kade said.

“One foot in.”

“Spiritually out.”

Julian released him.

They checked the replay.

One foot in by three centimeters.

Kade enlarged the final exchange. His own route across the mat appeared as an orange line. It bent twice but never broke. Julian's path had changed four times.

“You changed more than I did,” Kade said.

“Yes.”

“Because I told you the plan.”

“Because you kept it long enough to make me answer it.”

Kade replayed the turn.

The victory was still Julian's. The exchange belonged to Kade in a way the earlier losses had not.

Not because he had become unpredictable.

Because he had become legible and dangerous anyway.

Instructor Grae began Tactical Systems by removing the highest-ranked student from every team.

The class objected.

Grae waited beside the simulation table while twenty first-years explained that this was unfair, unrealistic, and destructive to the balance of the exercise. He wore the dark gray of Asterion tactical faculty and the patient expression of a man who had arranged all three outcomes on purpose.

“The scenario does not care about your rankings,” he said when the room quieted. “Neither will a collapsed bridge, a breached habitat, or an opponent who bothered to study your roster.”

Five practice fields appeared across the table. Each showed a different version of the same problem: move six civilian markers from an unstable platform to a protected zone before the platform failed.

No combat scoring.

No points for control.

One success condition.

All six across.

Julian stood from Kade's table.

“Where are you going?” Kade asked.

“Highest ranked.”

“Convenient time to become obedient.”

Grae pointed Julian toward an observation chair along the wall.

Kade's team consisted of Priya Shah, two students from House Faraday, a quiet Perception user from Kepler, and a Storage specialist who immediately began calculating whether the platform could be held up instead of evacuated.

Across the room, Mara had been removed from her team and looked offended by the architecture of reality.

Grae started the clock.

The simulated platform shuddered.

Priya crossed first, a clean acceleration carrying one civilian marker to safety in under four seconds.

“Again,” Kade said.

The west support failed.

The platform tilted seven degrees.

The Storage student spread both hands over the table controls. His field caught part of the change and bled amber across the load display.

“I can stabilize,” he said.

“For how long?”

“Forty seconds.”

The display revised as a second support cracked.

“Twenty-six.”

One of the Faraday students seized a civilian marker and ran for the north bridge.

The bridge folded halfway across.

Marker lost.

The simulation reset it at the starting point and removed twelve seconds from the clock.

“That makes no sense,” the student said. “We didn't know the bridge would go.”

“You knew the platform would,” Kade said.

“Not the same thing.”

The argument cost six more seconds.

Kade began issuing instructions.

Priya would run the east bridge. The Storage student would stabilize west. Kepler would call support failures. The Faraday students would carry two at a time.

Everyone moved.

For nine seconds, it worked.

Then east failed early.

Priya doubled back. One Faraday student tried to compensate and blocked the Storage student's line to the west support. The Kepler student called three changes at once. Kade changed Priya's route, changed the carrying order, sent the Storage student to the center brace, then changed him back when the north edge began to shear.

The clock reached zero with two civilian markers still on the platform.

Their field vanished.

Julian said nothing from the wall.

That was considerate of him.

Grae walked between the tables while each failed simulation displayed its final frame.

Only one team had extracted all six.

Sera's.

Her highest-ranked student stood at the wall beside Mara. Sera had assigned no fixed bridge, no fixed carrier, and no single person to call every change. The simulation replay showed her team moving slowly at first, pairing carriers and shifting routes whenever the Perception user called a color instead of a full diagnosis.

Green meant continue.

Amber meant finish the current crossing and stop.

Red meant turn back.

The last marker reached safety with less than a second remaining.

Grae froze the image.

“Why did this team succeed?”

“Better communication,” someone said.

“Partly.”

“Vale planned for failures.”

“Every team planned for failures.”

Kade examined the five final frames.

His own team clustered around the center where he had kept changing assignments. Sera's spread in pairs across three routes.

“Her plan could lose pieces without losing its shape,” he said.

Grae turned toward him.

“Explain.”

“My plan assigned people to solutions. When a solution failed, I had to assign everybody again.”

He pointed to Sera's field.

“She assigned them to conditions. They already knew what to do when the condition changed.”

Sera glanced over from the next table.

Grae nodded.

“A plan is not proof that the future will obey you. It is an agreement about what you will do when it doesn't.”

Kade looked toward Julian.

Julian had the decency not to look pleased.

His face remained extremely pleased anyway.

Mara raised one hand from the observation wall.

Grae nodded to her.

“Vale's plan also left capacity unused,” Mara said. “Her Redirection operator finished with thirty percent reserve.”

Sera turned.

“We moved everyone.”

“You could have moved them faster.”

“The condition was not fastest.”

Mara's attention shifted to the frozen clock: eight tenths of a second.

“It nearly became relevant.”

“But it didn't.”

The two stared across the room.

Kade understood both arguments. Mara wanted every resource spent against the measured limit. Sera wanted enough reserve for the failure the simulation had not announced yet.

Grae addressed the class.

“A success condition tells you when you may stop. It does not tell you how much margin tomorrow will demand.”

He looked between Mara and Sera.

“Vey is correct that unused capacity can become wasted capacity. Vale is correct that a result with no remaining options is fragile. Your work is to know which risk you are buying.”

Sera sat back.

Mara nodded once.

Neither looked convinced.

Good class, Kade thought.

Grae restored all five fields.

“Again. Highest ranks remain out.”

This time Kade did not begin by giving orders.

He asked the Storage student for the first visible signs of overload. He asked Priya how much distance she needed to stop safely with a full marker. He asked the Kepler student which calls she could make while moving. He asked the Faraday pair whether they preferred carrying together or separately.

They built three conditions.

If a support crossed amber, the Storage student would release it rather than spend his full reserve trying to save the platform.

If Priya lost a bridge, the nearest carrier would complete the route while she cleared the next one.

If two routes failed together, everyone would abandon the outer edges and move through center in pairs.

The simulation began.

Kade carried the first marker himself.

At fifteen seconds, west crossed amber.

The Storage student let it go.

The platform lurched hard enough to send two markers sliding, but his reserve remained nearly full.

Priya lost east.

Nobody waited for Kade.

The nearest Faraday student completed her crossing. Priya cleared north. The Kepler student called red and ran with the final pair.

Two routes failed together.

Everyone moved through center.

Kade reached for a new instruction.

There was nothing to add.

He carried.

The last marker crossed with four seconds remaining.

Their team did not cheer. The Storage student bent over the table with both hands braced against it. Priya was breathing like the simulation had asked her actual body to sprint. The Faraday pair bumped shoulders once.

The Kepler student looked at Kade.

“You talked less.”

“Cruel but accurate.”

Grae ended the exercise.

As the class filed out, Julian fell into step beside Kade.

“How does it feel?” he asked.

“How does what feel?”

“Having used the same plan twice.”

“The first plan failed.”

“The first version failed.”

Kade stopped in the corridor.

Students divided around them.

“You arranged this.”

Julian looked genuinely puzzled.

“The class?”

“Being removed. Grae's exercise. The lesson.”

“I have Tactical Systems at the same time every seventh day.”

“Suspicious.”

“I also do not control faculty curricula.”

“That is what someone who controlled faculty curricula would say.”

Julian started walking again.

“You solved the next problem.”

Kade caught up.

“We moved six dots across a table.”

“You stopped treating every failure as a reason to become the entire plan.”

The sentence caught under Kade's ribs.

Julian kept his attention on the corridor ahead.

No personal question.

No invitation to explain.

Just the observation.

Kade disliked him slightly less.

It was becoming a problem.

The following morning, Julian ran an open clinic on Field Three.

Kade had assumed an open clinic meant a crowd watching Rank One perform.

Instead it meant Julian spending twenty minutes with a second-year ranked below six thousand who kept crossing her feet during lateral defense.

The field's small bleachers held twelve students. Three had come to train. The rest had come to collect usable footage or stand near fame.

Julian ignored the cameras.

“Again,” he told the second-year.

She reset, moved left, and crossed her trailing foot behind the lead.

Julian tapped her shoulder before she could uncross.

“Dead.”

Her face tightened.

“I know.”

“Then why are you doing it?”

“Because you keep driving me left.”

“I will keep doing whatever works.”

She reset harder than necessary.

On the next exchange she overcorrected, widened her stance, and froze.

Julian tapped her again.

“Dead.”

The student pulled off one glove.

“This is pointless.”

She walked toward the gate while two people in the bleachers pretended not to record her leaving.

Julian did not call her back.

Kade stood from the rail.

“You removed too many decisions.”

Julian looked at him.

“That is usually the objective.”

“For an opponent.”

The student slowed near the gate.

Kade stepped onto the field.

“You told her what not to do. Then you kept creating the exact problem that makes her do it.”

Julian's expression cooled by a degree.

“Do you have a suggestion?”

“Yes.”

Kade pointed at the student.

“Attack me.”

She looked between them.

“Which one of you?”

“Me. He has had enough joy today.”

Julian stepped aside.

The student pulled her glove back on.

Kade took the center. She moved well going right. Going left, her rear foot crossed as soon as Kade pressed inside her shoulder.

He stopped.

“Again. But you're not defending the whole field.”

He indicated one square of mat between two seam lines.

“Keep me out of that.”

“That's smaller.”

“Very observant.”

She reset.

Kade drove left.

Her feet started to cross. Then she surrendered the outer lane, planted wide, and kept him out of the square.

“Again,” Kade said.

Three attempts later, the step held.

He widened the defended space.

The fourth attempt broke.

They narrowed it again.

By the tenth, she could give ground across half the engagement zone without crossing her feet.

Julian stood with his arms folded.

The student tried once against him.

He drove left.

She yielded the lane, held the square, and stayed upright.

Julian stopped before the finishing strike.

“Good,” he said.

She looked exhausted and furious and pleased.

“Can I go now?”

“Yes.”

She left with her head up. The cameras followed her for a different reason.

Julian waited until the gate shut.

“You were right.”

Kade inspected him for signs of medical distress.

“Could you repeat that near a recording device?”

“No.”

“Expected.”

Julian picked up his water.

“I was solving the error instead of the person making it.”

“Apparently everybody needs someone who can beat them.”

Julian's mouth shifted.

“You did not beat me.”

“I improved your clinic. Social victory.”

“Not recognized by the ladder.”

“Weak ladder.”

Julian laughed despite himself.

Then he tossed Kade a practice band.

“Your turn.”

Kade caught it.

“I already trained.”

“You crossed your feet twice while demonstrating.”

“That is slander.”

Julian took center.

Kade fastened the band.

The students in the bleachers leaned forward.

For once, he did not mind being examined.

The third training session ended with Julian beating Kade nine times instead of eleven.

Progress.

Afterward they walked toward breakfast together.

Students noticed.

Kade could feel it in the glances. Rank one and Zero Mercer crossing the courtyard made a social equation people immediately wanted to solve.

“You enjoy this?” Kade asked.

Julian looked around. “People watching?”

“Being known.”

Julian considered the question.

“No.”

Kade looked at him.

“Really?”

“I enjoy winning. Fame is a tax on it.”

That answer sounded too practiced.

Kade filed it away.

At the dining hall entrance, a first-year stopped Julian to ask about a challenge recording. Julian answered seriously, then corrected the student’s stance for thirty seconds before continuing.

“You do that a lot,” Kade said.

“What?”

“Help people who might eventually compete with you.”

Julian shrugged.

“A weak ladder makes the top meaningless.”

“That is the most rank-one thing you have said.”

“It is still true.”

They entered Meridian Commons together.

That alone produced another wave of looks.

Taren was sitting with Sera and Eli. He saw Julian, then Kade, then visibly revised whatever sentence he had been about to say.

“Interesting,” Taren said.

Kade set down his tray.

“No.”

“I have said nothing.”

“You were going to.”

Julian sat at the end of the table.

Sera looked at him.

“You know there are nineteen other Houses.”

“I was invited.”

Kade stared.

“I did not invite you.”

Julian took an orange from the bowl.

“Your roommate did.”

Eli looked pleased.

“I thought cross-House tactical exchange would be healthy.”

Sera looked at him.

“You wanted to ask Cross about his acceleration model.”

“Also healthy.”

Breakfast became unexpectedly normal.

Eli waited until Julian had peeled half the orange before placing his tablet beside the tray.

“Your public acceleration data has a discontinuity.”

Julian continued peeling.

“Good morning to you too.”

“Between initial commitment and the second correction. The model says you change direction before the opponent changes load.”

“Then your model is wrong.”

Eli looked wounded.

“That is one possibility.”

“What is the other?” Sera asked.

“Cross violates causality.”

“Rank One privilege,” Kade said.

Eli expanded two graphs. One showed Julian's acceleration. The other tracked an opponent's center of mass. A narrow blue band marked the gap between them.

“There. Ninety milliseconds.”

Julian finally looked.

“You're measuring the wrong decision.”

Eli leaned closer.

“Movement is the decision.”

“Movement is evidence that a decision already happened.”

Julian tapped the opponent's line two hundred milliseconds earlier, at a change so small Kade could barely distinguish it from noise.

“His rear heel lifts here. He can still move either direction, but he can only generate useful force in one.”

Eli shifted the model.

The discontinuity disappeared.

He looked personally betrayed by the graph.

“So public telemetry can model decision quality,” Kade said.

“No,” Julian replied. “It can model some of the physical commitments that follow decisions.”

“That sounds like decision quality with defensive phrasing.”

“The distinction matters. If Navarro trains a model on outcomes, it will call every move that preceded a win intelligent.”

Eli paused over his tablet.

Then he began writing.

Sera stole one of Julian's orange segments.

“The ladder does that already.”

Julian looked at the missing segment, then at her.

“The ladder does not measure intelligence.”

“It measures what the academy values and then pretends the number describes the whole person.”

“It measures competitive performance.”

“Exactly.”

Sera pointed across the Commons toward a wall display cycling through the week's ranking changes.

“Put those same students in a smoke-filled residence hall with an unconscious civilian and half of them lose a thousand ranks to the nearest person who knows how to carry somebody down stairs.”

“That is a different ranking.”

“There isn't one.”

Julian considered that.

He did not produce a quick answer.

Kade marked the pause.

Taren reached across the table and took a second orange segment while Julian was distracted.

“Cross once won a junior regional after throwing up behind the officials' table.”

Julian turned.

“Food poisoning.”

“Nerves,” Taren said.

“Food poisoning.”

“My source was present.”

“Your source was eight and lost in the first round.”

Taren smiled.

“So you remember him.”

“He told everyone in the medical bay that I was afraid of the final.”

“Were you?” Kade asked.

Julian looked at him as if this were obvious.

“Of course.”

The table quieted for half a beat.

Rank One said it without shame.

Taren recovered first.

“There is also the Marseille podium incident.”

“False.”

“And the mascot.”

“Technically accurate but maliciously framed.”

Eli looked up from the telemetry.

“What happened to the mascot?”

Julian ate the last orange segment before anyone else could steal it.

“Nothing you can prove.”

Kade mostly listened.

It was the first time he had seen Julian as somebody inside a group rather than a number on a board.

At 07:42, Julian’s wrist display lit.

He looked at it.

His posture changed.

Barely.

Kade noticed because he had begun studying what people did before they moved.

Julian dismissed the notification.

Taren said, “Family?”

Julian looked at him.

“How do you know?”

Taren lifted both hands.

“You make a face.”

Julian’s expression flattened.

“I do not.”

Kade nearly laughed.

Taren smiled.

“Everyone has one.”

Julian stood.

“I have a call.”

He left.

Kade looked at Taren.

“That was precise.”

“I notice things.”

“So do I.”

Taren’s smile stayed easy.

“Then we’ll both be annoying.”

Kade filed that away with the other things Taren knew slightly too easily.

---

Later that week, Kade learned what Julian’s face meant.

He was returning borrowed wraps to the side training room when he heard voices through the half-open door.

Julian stood by the wall display.

A man occupied the screen.

Older version of Julian’s face. Sharper. Expensive suit. The kind of posture that looked tailored too.

“You lost thirty-one seconds to Voss,” the man said.

“It was a training session.”

“You gave him the inside line twice.”

“I know.”

“Then why did it happen?”

Julian’s jaw tightened.

“Because he is good.”

Silence.

The man said, “That is not an answer Crosses give.”

Kade stopped outside the door.

He should leave.

Instead he froze, because people were most revealing when they believed nobody was listening.

Julian’s voice went colder.

“It is the answer I am giving.”

“You are number one.”

“Today.”

“You have been given every advantage. Do not develop humility now.”

The call ended.

Kade stepped backward too late.

Julian came through the door and saw him.

For one second, neither spoke.

Kade held up the wraps.

“Terrible hiding place.”

Julian stared.

“Very.”

“I was returning these.”

“I believe you.”

That somehow made it worse.

Kade looked toward the blank screen behind him.

“Your father seems cheerful.”

Julian laughed once.

No humor.

“He thinks rank is a moral category.”

“Sounds exhausting.”

“It is.”

Kade waited.

He remembered Mara saying something similar about her father.

Asterion’s elite, apparently, came with family systems as complicated as everyone else’s.

Julian said, “Do not make this into a reason to go easy on me.”

Kade blinked.

“That is what you think I would do?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“I think you might mistake understanding for pity.”

Kade looked at him.

Julian’s expression was steady again.

“Fair.”

They stood in the corridor.

Then Kade handed him the wraps.

“Thursday?”

Julian looked almost relieved.

“Thursday.”

---

On Thursday, Julian beat Kade seven times.

The eighth bout lasted four minutes.

Kade went in with an actual plan.

Not a clever opening.

A plan.

He intended to give Julian center, draw the pressure toward Kade’s weak left retreat, then refuse the retreat and force a second decision at the boundary.

It worked for forty seconds.

Then Julian recognized it.

Kade stayed with it anyway.

Changed details.

Kept the structure.

At two minutes, Julian stopped smiling.

At three, Kade scored his first clean positional point.

At three fifty-one, Julian trapped him against the edge and ended it.

Kade sat on the floor breathing hard.

Julian offered a hand.

“Better.”

“There is that word again.”

“You made me abandon the first plan.”

Kade took his hand.

“Only the first?”

“Do not become unbearable.”

“Too late.”

Julian smiled.

They reset one more time.

Kade lost again.

He enjoyed that one.

Not the losing.

The fact that he knew why.

---

His tablet updated as he left training.

A message from Mara.

**You trained with Cross.**

Kade replied:

**Your surveillance network is impressive.**

Three dots appeared.

**He is very good. Learn everything.**

Kade stopped walking.

No jealousy.

No rivalry theater.

Just advice.

Julian glanced at him.

“Problem?”

“Mara Vey told me to learn everything from you.”

Julian’s eyebrows rose.

“That is almost a compliment.”

“Apparently everyone here communicates through technical hostility.”

“It saves time.”

Kade typed:

**Already getting punched efficiently.**

Mara replied immediately.

**Progress.**

Kade smiled.

Not the defensive one.

The other kind was becoming easier.

He put the tablet away.

Julian opened the dining-hall door.

“Same time Saturday?”

Kade looked at him.

“You want another seven wins?”

“I want to see whether you make me change the plan sooner.”

There it was again.

Not charity.

Challenge.

Kade stepped through the door.

“Fine.”

Julian smiled.

“Good.”

For the first time in Kade’s life, somebody better than him was not asking him to become less difficult.

Julian was asking him to become difficult in a more disciplined way.

That felt like a form of respect Kade understood.

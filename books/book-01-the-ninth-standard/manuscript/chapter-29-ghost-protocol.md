# Chapter 29 — Ghost Protocol

Eli was alive.

Kade repeated the sentence three times before it felt like language.

The meeting happened in a sealed maintenance room beneath Meridian because apparently being alive was now classified.

That sentence would have been funny yesterday.

Today Kade could not decide whether to laugh or start breaking furniture.

Mara came with him.

She had read the NULL-channel messages twice and said only, “We verify before we believe.”

Kade had wanted to tell her verification was an unreasonable standard when the messages used *panic geometry* correctly.

Instead he had said, “Fine.”

Growth remained irritating.

They descended through a service elevator that required Sen’s temporary key. The display skipped M-1 and M-2 entirely, then stopped at M-3 with a mechanical click older than most of Asterion.

Sera arrived two minutes later after receiving a message containing only:

**COME TO SUBLEVEL M-3. DO NOT HIT ANYONE UNTIL YOU SEE WHO DESERVES IT.**

She walked in prepared to ignore the second sentence.

Eli stood beside a maintenance console wearing borrowed academy clothes, one sock that did not match the other, and a medical patch on his neck.

He looked exhausted.

Also smug.

For half a second Kade’s brain rejected the image.

The face was wrong because it moved.

The hands were wrong because they were attached to a living person.

The empty bed upstairs had trained Kade’s expectations too quickly.

Sera stopped.

The expression on her face changed so fast Kade could not follow it.

Relief.

Anger.

Relief again.

Then pure, focused violence.

Eli raised both hands.

“Before anyone reacts—”

Sera crossed the room and hit him in the shoulder.

Harder than Kade had.

Eli winced.

“Reasonable.”

She hit him again.

“Also reasonable.”

Then she hugged him.

Eli froze.

Sera held on for three seconds.

Then shoved him away.

“If you ever fake your death again, I will make the paperwork accurate.”

“Understood.”

Mara stood completely still.

Eli looked at her.

“Hi.”

Mara crossed the distance and put both hands on his shoulders.

Kade expected another hit.

Instead she checked his face, neck patch, pupils, and stance like she was conducting her own medical intake.

“You’re actually alive.”

“Strong opening.”

Mara hugged him once.

Briefly.

Then stepped back.

“I am furious with you.”

“Getting that theme.”

Kade laughed.

It came out too close to crying, so he stopped.

Then he stepped forward and hugged Eli too.

That surprised both of them even though it should not have anymore.

Eli went still.

Then hugged him back.

“Okay,” Eli said into Kade’s shoulder. “This is significantly worse than being hit.”

Kade did not let go immediately.

That was new too.

When he finally stepped back, he looked at Eli’s face.

“You heard the memorial.”

Eli’s expression changed.

“Some of it.”

“How?”

“Maintenance audio relay.”

Kade stared.

“You listened to your own memorial.”

“Not by choice.”

“That is deeply strange.”

“Yes.”

“What did you hear?”

Eli looked down.

“Enough.”

Kade remembered standing behind the lectern.

*Eli was worth it.*

Embarrassment tried to arrive.

Then Kade became angry at himself for being embarrassed.

“Good,” he said.

Eli looked up.

“Good?”

“Get used to it.”

A small smile returned.

“Terrible threat.”

Dr. Sen stood near the door.

Kade had been deliberately not looking at her.

Now he did.

Anger returned immediately.

It had to share space with relief, but it was still there.

“Start with why I gave a memorial speech for a living person.”

Sen did not flinch.

“I will.”

Eli raised a hand.

“I should start.”

Kade looked at him.

“Fine.”

Eli opened a network map.

“Six weeks ago I found maintenance traffic in Lab Six that did not match academy monitoring. Tiny packets. Regular enough to be deliberate, hidden inside coolant diagnostics.”

“We know this part.”

“You know the part I told you before the explosion. You do not know what I did before that.”

Kade folded his arms.

Eli enlarged the traffic map.

“The packets went nowhere inside Asterion. They jumped through a dead maintenance relay and disappeared into an encrypted external route.”

“NULL-7?” Mara asked.

“No. NULL-7 was mine. That is why I noticed the difference.”

He highlighted two pathways.

One green.

One red.

“The green one is the abandoned maintenance route I use for test packets. It exists locally and should terminate locally. The red one was pretending to be the same kind of traffic but tunneling out.”

Sera leaned closer.

“So whoever did this knew enough about old infrastructure to hide beside a real dead channel.”

“Yes.”

Taren’s knowledge.

Or his handlers’.

Or someone older.

Kade stared at the red route.

Eli continued.

“After the archive lockout, packet frequency tripled. Then one Lab Six pressure monitor reported a test signal from a controller that did not exist.”

“You knew the attack was coming,” Kade said.

“I knew something was about to happen. Not what.”

“Then why were you still there?”

Eli looked uncomfortable.

“Because the coolant controller was exposed through the same maintenance layer. If I left without isolating it, whoever had access could vent the chamber upward.”

Kade remembered the pressure door.

*If it vents into the upper lab, it takes the whole level.*

“You went back on purpose.”

“Yes.”

“That was incredibly stupid.”

“Yes.”

“You could have died.”

“Yes.”

Kade ran out of words.

Eli’s expression lost the last trace of smugness.

“I know.”

The room stayed quiet.

Kade looked at him.

“You were scared.”

Eli blinked.

“Yes.”

“I did not ask if you knew it was dangerous.”

“I know.”

Kade waited.

Eli looked toward the floor.

“I was terrified.”

There.

Not heroic certainty.

A seventeen-year-old deciding while afraid.

“That matters,” Kade said.

“Why?”

“Because if you start telling the story like you knew you were going to survive, I will hit you.”

Eli considered.

“Reasonable.”

Sen stepped closer.

“Eli warned me about the external surveillance fourteen hours before the archive breach.”

Kade looked at her.

“And you did what?”

“Prepared Ghost Protocol.”

A document appeared on the wall.

Old.

Pre-Asterion.

The interface used the same archaic block lettering as the Zero Protocol files.

**GHOST PROTOCOL — COMPROMISED OBSERVATION COUNTERMEASURE**

Below it:

**Premise: if observation cannot be trusted, remove the protected person from the observer’s model.**

Kade read the sentence twice.

“That is your entire institution in one paragraph.”

Sen accepted the criticism.

“Yes.”

The protocol steps appeared.

False casualty.

False device loss.

False recovery failure.

Physical relocation through infrastructure absent from normal campus telemetry.

Adversary-facing data trail kept consistent with the casualty state.

Kade looked at Eli.

“The unresolved device identifier.”

Eli nodded.

“Portable medical isolation pod.”

Kade stared.

“The investigators told me they could not identify it.”

“They could not in the normal device registry. Ghost hardware uses predecessor identifiers.”

The clue moved into place.

Not magic.

Not a hidden body being somehow missed.

A device deliberately built to disappear from modern Asterion systems.

“How did you get from Lab Six into that?” Mara asked.

Eli brought up a floor plan.

“When I closed the local coolant isolation, the controller triggered an old containment emergency route. There is a service hatch under the south console.”

Kade looked at Sen.

“You knew it was there.”

“Yes.”

“You designed this?”

“No. Aaron did.”

The room changed.

Kade’s attention snapped back to the diagram.

“My father.”

Sen nodded.

“Ghost Protocol was originally built for precursor subjects whose identities had been compromised.”

Aaron.

The missing twelfth name.

Kade’s stomach tightened.

“So he used it.”

“More than once.”

“Did it save him?”

Sen was silent.

Kade laughed once.

“Of course.”

“Kade.”

“No. Later.”

He forced himself back to Eli.

“How did you know to use the hatch?”

“I didn’t.”

Eli pointed at the floor plan.

“The console opened it automatically after I isolated the vent. I thought I had broken something else.”

“That sounds like you.”

“Thank you.”

“What happened?” Sera asked.

“I crawled into the service shaft because the lab was filling with coolant. A medical pod activated about thirty meters in. Sen’s people found me there six minutes later.”

“You were injured?” Mara asked.

“Coolant exposure. Mild field burn. Concussion.”

Kade looked at the patch on his neck.

“Mild?”

“Park used the word moderate and then glared at me for an hour.”

“That sounds more plausible.”

Eli sat on a metal workbench.

“The plan was twelve hours.”

Kade turned on him.

“How long has it been?”

“Thirty-six.”

Sera made a sound.

Eli raised both hands.

“In fairness, several things became complicated.”

“What things?”

Sen answered.

“The surveillance remained active after the attack. Whoever was watching checked Eli’s casualty status repeatedly. They also queried recovery-team logs, housing status, and family notification records.”

Kade felt cold.

“They were verifying the kill.”

“We do not know if killing Eli was the primary objective.”

“Sen.”

She held his gaze.

“They were verifying the outcome.”

“That is the same thing if you are Eli.”

“Yes.”

Eli looked less comfortable now.

Good.

Kade did not want the danger becoming interesting to him.

Sen continued.

“If Eli reappeared too soon, they would know the deception and change tactics. We kept the visible system consistent.”

“So you let the whole academy believe it.”

“Yes.”

“You let his parents believe it?”

Eli answered quickly.

“No. My mother was told privately after the first eight hours.”

Kade looked at him.

“Eight?”

“She threatened to fly here and expose the entire operation herself.”

Sera said, “I like her.”

“You would.”

“Why eight hours?” Mara asked Sen.

“Because family communications were among the channels being watched. We had to establish a secure physical contact route first.”

Eli rubbed both hands over his face.

“My mother did not appreciate operational security.”

“What did she say?” Kade asked.

Eli looked at him.

“I am not repeating it under the family-clean rule.”

Sera almost laughed.

The tension cracked for one second.

Then Kade looked back at Sen.

“You let me think he was dead.”

Sen’s face tightened.

“Yes.”

“You watched me stand in the Hall of Names.”

“Yes.”

“You heard what I said.”

“Yes.”

Kade stepped toward her.

“You keep deciding what people can survive knowing.”

Sen did not move.

“You are right.”

The answer stopped him.

“I am not asking you to forgive the decision,” Sen said. “I am telling you that I made it.”

“That is not enough.”

“No.”

Again.

No defense.

Mara stood beside Kade.

“You could have told him something,” she said. “Not Eli’s status. Enough to prevent the memorial from becoming part of the deception.”

Sen looked at her.

“I considered it.”

“And?”

“I did not know whether Kade’s communications were compromised. Or yours. Or Sera’s. Or mine.”

Everyone looked toward the empty space where Taren would normally have stood.

The betrayal returned.

Different now.

More complicated.

Taren had lied.

Taren had also burned his Directorate identity to get them out.

Truth and betrayal occupied the same person.

Kade was beginning to resent reality for refusing clean categories.

Eli said quietly, “I agreed.”

Kade looked at him.

“To the twelve hours.”

“You said.”

“I also agreed to extend after Sen explained the surveillance.”

Sera’s eyes narrowed.

“You agreed while concussed.”

“Park cleared capacity.”

“That sounds like something Sen would put in a report.”

“It is literally in the report.”

Kade stared at Eli.

“Why?”

Eli shrugged badly.

“Because somebody tried to turn my status into a sensor. I did not want to tell them they were wrong yet.”

That was Eli too.

Angry at being reduced to data.

Willing to use the system’s assumption against it.

Kade understood the impulse enough to dislike it less.

“I am still angry.”

“I would be concerned if you weren’t.”

“Good.”

“Good.”

They stood there.

Alive and furious.

Kade realized grief did not reverse simply because the person returned.

The two days still existed.

The speech still existed.

The empty bed still existed in his memory.

Relief did not erase any of it.

It added itself.

That was almost harder.

---

Sen activated a second document.

**SUPPRESSION ORDER — FULL TEXT**

Kade read.

The leaked version had focused on training restrictions.

The actual order was broader.

**Conceal rate of anomalous progression.**

**Restrict unsupervised Boundary events due neurologic risk.**

**Prevent outside telemetry replication.**

**Compartmentalize historical Zero references.**

**Preserve subject autonomy where operational security permits.**

Kade stopped at the last line.

“Subject.”

Sen’s expression changed.

“The language predates you.”

“Still there.”

“Yes.”

He read the order again.

“You weren’t suppressing me.”

“I was suppressing information about you.”

“Same word.”

“Different target.”

“And you thought that justified letting me believe everyone wanted me weak.”

“No.”

Kade looked at her.

Sen continued.

“I thought the misunderstanding was safer than explaining what I was protecting you from.”

“Which was wrong.”

“Yes.”

Mara’s eyes sharpened.

Kade almost laughed.

He had spent months wanting Sen to admit she was wrong.

It was much less satisfying when the consequences were real.

Sera pointed at one clause.

“Where operational security permits.”

“Yes.”

“That language lets whoever controls security redefine autonomy whenever convenient.”

Sen looked at her.

“Yes.”

“Then it is a bad rule.”

“Yes.”

Sera blinked.

Kade looked between them.

“Everyone agreeing that the secret policy is bad is making me uncomfortable.”

Sen folded her arms.

“It was written by frightened people who assumed temporary emergency authority would remain temporary.”

“Did it?” Eli asked.

“No.”

Another clue to the world they had inherited.

Not villains designing evil.

People making exceptions and then living inside them for thirty-one years.

---

Eli ate two sandwiches while they talked.

Kade noticed because watching a presumed-dead person complain about mustard was strangely therapeutic.

“This is terrible mustard.”

“You have been hidden underground for thirty-six hours.”

“That does not improve mustard.”

Sera handed him hers.

Eli stared.

“You are being nice to me.”

“Recovery nutrition.”

“Ah.”

“Do not misinterpret it.”

“Recovered.”

Mara sat beside him.

“What happens to your fellowship?”

Eli stopped chewing.

“I missed the panel.”

Kade felt anger again on his behalf.

Sen said, “Professor Kim postponed it.”

Eli looked at her.

“He did?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“He was told you were medically unavailable.”

“You did not tell him I was alive.”

“No.”

Eli considered.

“That is acceptable.”

Kade stared.

“You are disturbingly calm.”

“My fellowship still exists.”

“Priorities.”

“Exactly.”

That small future reappearing mattered more than Kade expected.

Eli was not only alive.

He had tomorrow again.

---

Then Eli cleared his throat.

“There is more.”

“Of course there is,” Kade said.

Eli opened a map of Asterion.

The island appeared in layers.

Public campus.

Utilities.

Emergency service routes.

Then an older map beneath all of them.

Precursor infrastructure.

Red markers moved through sealed corridors below the Spire.

“Those are people?” Sera asked.

“Personnel tags with academy identifiers disabled.”

“How many?”

“Twenty-three confirmed. Maybe more.”

Sen’s expression hardened.

“The Directorate?” Kade asked.

“We believe so.”

“Believe?”

Sen looked at him.

“We are about to find out.”

Eli changed the display.

One route ended near a label Kade had never seen.

**SUBLEVEL NINE — LEGACY CONTAINMENT**

His skin went cold.

“Nine,” he said.

Mara looked at him.

No one needed to explain why the number mattered.

Kade enlarged the old map.

Sublevel Nine sat directly under the oldest part of the Spire.

Not under the arena.

Not under research.

Under administration.

The place where Asterion made decisions had literally been built on top of whatever it was hiding.

“Is that poetic on purpose?” Kade asked.

Sen did not smile.

“No.”

“Worse.”

Sera traced emergency routes.

“If twenty-three unauthorized people are moving under this island, Response protocols say staged evacuation now.”

Sen nodded.

“Already initiated under storm-preparedness cover.”

Sera’s eyes narrowed with reluctant approval.

“Meridian?”

“First-year Houses move to upper safe zones in twelve minutes.”

“My people are going to ask why.”

“Tell them the storm flooded a lower utility spine.”

Sera did not like the lie.

Kade could see it.

Then she looked at the map and understood why Sen was using it.

Temporary secrecy.

Again.

The same dangerous tool.

Sometimes still necessary.

Julian’s voice came through Sen’s tablet from a secure channel.

“Director, House Sol is reporting access failures on lower transit.”

Sen answered, “Begin student shelter protocol. Quietly.”

“Understood.”

Julian paused.

“Is Mercer with you?”

Sen looked at Kade.

“Yes.”

“Tell him his timing remains terrible.”

Kade leaned toward the tablet.

“Your concern is touching.”

Julian’s voice softened by half a degree.

“Good. You are both alive.”

Then the channel closed.

Kade stared at the map.

Eli was alive.

The Directorate was inside Asterion.

Sublevel Nine existed beneath them.

And the quiet war Sen had described was no longer quiet.

Kade looked at Eli.

“You stay here.”

Eli stared back.

“No.”

“You just came back from the dead.”

“Technically never left.”

“Eli.”

“I know those legacy routes better than anyone here except maybe Sen.”

Sen said, “He is right.”

Kade looked at her.

“Stop helping him.”

“I am helping the island.”

Sera said, “He is medically cleared for walking, not combat.”

“I can avoid combat.”

Everyone looked at Eli.

He sighed.

“I can attempt to avoid combat.”

Mara said, “Better.”

Kade wanted to order him to stay.

The impulse was immediate.

Strong.

Protective.

And completely at odds with everything Kade claimed to believe.

Eli had made the choice at Lab Six.

He had made the choice to extend Ghost Protocol.

He could make this one too.

Kade hated that.

“Fine,” he said.

Eli blinked.

“Fine?”

“Do not make me regret respecting your agency.”

“That is a very threatening sentence.”

“Good.”

For the first time since Northline, Kade wished the problem were only whether he belonged at school.

Then the floor beneath them vibrated.

Not an explosion.

A heavy mechanical movement from somewhere far below.

On the map, the outer door to Sublevel Nine changed from green to amber.

Sen went still.

“They found it,” she said.

Eli’s sandwich hit the table.

The quiet war was over.

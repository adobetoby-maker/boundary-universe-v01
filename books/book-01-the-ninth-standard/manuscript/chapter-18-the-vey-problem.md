# Chapter 18 — The Vey Problem

Mara Vey’s father had a talent for making questions sound like conclusions.

“How is Mercer adapting?” he asked.

Mara looked up from the coupled-field paper on her tablet.

Her father’s face occupied the wall display in her dorm room, sharp despite the slight transmission delay. Director Alaric Vey wore a dark civilian jacket rather than the formal uniform he preferred for public appearances. Somehow that made him look more official.

“Which Mercer?” Mara asked.

His expression did not change.

She enjoyed that more than she should have.

“Kade Mercer.”

“I know.”

“Mara.”

“Father.”

Three seconds of silence.

He knew she was being difficult.

She knew he knew.

That was most of their relationship condensed efficiently enough to please them both.

“He’s adapting,” she said.

“To Asterion?”

“To being observed.”

That earned the smallest movement around his eyes.

Interesting.

Alaric Vey had spent Mara’s childhood insisting that attention was information. Cameras, rankings, interviews, opponents, teachers. Every person who watched you was measuring something, whether they admitted it or not.

Mara had learned the lesson too well.

She had also learned that her father rarely asked questions for only one reason.

Their weekly call was supposed to be about her.

That was the agreement.

Training load.

Vector Analysis.

Her rank.

House Trial.

Whether she was sleeping enough.

Alaric had spent the first eight minutes asking about all of those things with the focused efficiency of someone reviewing a quarterly report.

He had also remembered the date of her Mechanics assessment without checking.

That was the difficulty with dismissing her father as cold.

Cold people did not keep a private calendar of every assessment their children pretended not to care about. They did not watch a House Trial replay twice and send a four-line message at two in the morning identifying the exact exchange where she had stopped protecting the team plan and started protecting her own rank.

Alaric loved her with terrifying attention.

He simply trusted analysis more than comfort.

“You changed the east-platform assignment after Mercer went out,” he had said at the beginning of the call.

“The original distribution assumed he could keep Pike moving.”

“Most captains would have tried to preserve their plan.”

“Then most captains would have lost.”

He had inclined his head.

From Alaric, that was praise without packaging.

Mara had wanted it anyway.

Then he asked about Kade.

Mara closed the coupled-field paper.

“What do you actually want to know?”

Her father’s expression remained neutral.

“I asked a question.”

“You asked four questions about my semester and one about Mercer. The Mercer question is the only one you prepared for.”

A faint sigh.

“You are doing well.”

“I know.”

“Your House Trial performance was excellent.”

“I know.”

“You are still overextending your right shoulder in long exchanges.”

Mara’s grip tightened around the stylus.

“I know.”

Alaric paused.

“There.”

“What?”

“You dislike praise if you think it is being used to soften the next subject.”

“You taught me to notice framing.”

“I occasionally regret my parenting.”

“That makes two of us.”

His mouth almost smiled.

Then he asked, “Has Mercer repeated the structural event?”

Mara’s stylus stopped.

The room changed.

Not physically.

In the way rooms changed when one sentence contained knowledge it should not.

“What structural event?”

Another pause.

Too short for most people to notice.

Not for Mara.

“The arena incident,” he said.

“The Mercer Fault?”

“I dislike that name.”

“So does he.”

“Good.”

Mara leaned back.

The broken-floor telemetry had been restricted within minutes. The public academy notice described a composite-floor failure coinciding with an anomalous combat reading. Students knew that was incomplete because students were remarkably efficient at discovering whatever institutions labeled confidential.

But her father did not work at Asterion.

He should not know the event involved anything beyond a structural fault.

“Why do you care?” Mara asked.

“Professional curiosity.”

“About a first-year ranked nineteen hundred?”

“About an unusual case.”

“An unusual case whose restricted telemetry you have somehow read.”

Alaric’s face gave her nothing.

Mara shifted the call display to the side and opened the public incident summary between them.

“This says composite failure. The faculty report says anomalous combat reading. Neither report says structural event.”

“The floor broke.”

“That is a description, not a classification.”

“Does the distinction matter?”

“It mattered enough for you to use the classified one.”

The transmission delay placed a fraction of false space between them. Mara knew its rhythm. His answer should have arrived.

It did not.

She remembered being nine and visiting the Vey Foundation archive while Alaric met with its directors. He had found her studying two folders whose index numbers skipped from forty-one to forty-three.

*What is missing?* he had asked.

She had pointed to forty-two.

*No. Forty-two is absent. Missing means someone expected it to be here.*

He had made her find the accession ledger before he accepted the answer.

Now Mara looked at her father through a screen and applied the lesson correctly.

“Who expected you to know?” she asked.

His eyes narrowed.

Not an answer.

But no longer nothing.

“You sound like Director Sen.”

“Amara and I have disagreed about what deserves attention for years.”

There it was again.

Not the answer.

The shape around the answer.

Kade did that constantly. He noticed what people had to step around to avoid saying the thing they meant.

Mara had begun finding the habit irritatingly useful.

“Did you know his father?” she asked.

Alaric Vey went still.

Not much.

Enough.

Mara sat forward.

“Interesting.”

“Mara.”

“You did.”

“I did not say that.”

“You didn’t need to.”

His voice sharpened. “Do not investigate Kade Mercer’s family.”

That was the moment Mara knew she absolutely would.

She kept her face neutral.

“Why?”

“Because there are histories that do not improve through curiosity.”

“That may be the least convincing sentence you’ve ever said to me.”

“Mara.”

He rarely repeated her name twice in one call.

She filed that away.

“Does Director Sen know you’re asking me about him?”

Silence.

There.

Enough.

“I have to go,” her father said.

“No. You have to avoid answering.”

His expression softened by a fraction.

“I love you.”

“That is unfair.”

“It remains true.”

The call ended.

Mara sat alone with her reflection in the dark display.

She had spent most of her life being told discipline meant resisting impulses.

Sometimes discipline meant choosing which impulse deserved to win.

---

Mara did not investigate immediately.

That annoyed her.

She had training in twenty minutes.

Asterion did not care that her father had just turned Kade Mercer’s dead parent into a classified question.

The academy remained stubbornly committed to the timetable.

Mara changed into training black and crossed Meridian’s lower bridge toward Combat Hall Two.

Kade was on the far side of the room with Julian Cross, losing.

Again.

Julian moved him toward the edge with three quiet decisions. Kade recognized the trap one beat earlier than he had the previous week, changed his feet, and still lost.

He stayed on the mat for half a second.

Then laughed.

Julian offered a hand.

Kade took it.

Mara looked away before either of them noticed she had watched.

Her own session was worse than usual.

Not physically.

Her body performed exactly as trained.

Her attention did not.

Combat Hall Two had been divided into four lanes. Each lane contained a start plate, two pivot marks, and three waist-high barriers whose positions changed when the runner crossed the first gate. The exercise rewarded prediction. Commit before the barrier moved, and the route opened. Wait to see it, and the next gate closed.

Instructor Chen sent Mara through three acceleration sequences.

She cleared the first in 8.4 seconds.

Too slow for her.

On the second, she chose the center pivot before the first barrier completed its turn. Correct. Her right foot found the plate, her shoulder cleared the closing field, and the finish display flashed 7.9.

She should have reset.

Instead she looked across the hall.

Kade was standing again. Julian had him tracing the final exchange in the air, one hand marking where Kade’s attention had fixed on the edge instead of the opponent.

Aaron Mercer.

The third start tone sounded.

Mara launched late, recovered before the first gate, and predicted a left rotation from the barrier’s opening angle.

The barrier went right.

She clipped the edge with one shoulder.

The safety field caught her before she hit the wall.

For half a second she hung sideways in the blue restraint lattice. Then the field lowered her to the mat.

Chen stopped the drill.

The other two students in her lane stepped off their start plates. Nobody stared openly.

At Asterion, even concern was competitive.

“You are somewhere else.”

“No.”

“Mara.”

Apparently everyone at Asterion had learned to use her name as an accusation.

She exhaled.

“Yes.”

“Medical?”

“No.”

“Family?”

Mara looked at him.

Chen lifted both hands.

“Not asking details. Asking whether you are safe to continue.”

She considered lying.

Then thought about Kade admitting fear after the broken floor.

Irritating influence.

“Yes,” she said. “But reduce speed.”

Chen nodded.

No lecture.

No demand to explain.

He reset the lane to sixty percent and blanked the timer.

Mara looked at the empty display.

“I did not ask you to remove scoring.”

“I know.”

“Then restore it.”

“No.”

Mara folded her arms.

Chen walked to the first pivot mark and tapped it with his shoe.

“What caused the miss?”

“Divided attention.”

“That describes you. What caused the miss?”

She replayed the approach.

Opening angle. Rotation rate. Weight distribution in the barrier arm.

All consistent with left until the actuator reversed.

“The motion cue was false.”

“Every third sequence now includes one. The course updated yesterday.”

Mara’s jaw tightened.

“You changed the failure rule without announcing it.”

“The briefing says not all initial conditions remain reliable.”

She had read the briefing.

She had converted that sentence into ordinary variance because the old exercise rewarded early commitment.

Chen pointed to the blank finish display.

“Your problem is not speed today. It is that you promoted a likely answer to a fact because acting on it usually works.”

The sentence arrived with her father’s silence still attached to it.

Mara disliked instructors who became relevant by accident.

“Run it again,” she said.

“New condition. Call the decision at each gate.”

“Speaking will slow me down.”

“The timer is off.”

Exactly the problem.

The next run was slower.

Mara hated it.

At the first gate, the barrier arm opened left.

“Likely left,” she called.

It held. She took the pivot.

At the second, both lower actuators loaded equally.

“Unresolved.”

Mara shortened her stride instead of selecting a route. The barrier feinted right, reversed left, and left the center lane open for less than a second.

She crossed without touching it.

The final gate presented a clean right rotation.

“Right.”

This time the cue remained true.

She finished four full seconds below her normal speed.

No score appeared.

Chen looked toward the lane record, then back at her.

“Again?”

Mara reset at the start plate.

“Again.”

By the fourth reduced-speed run, she no longer treated *unresolved* as an admission of failure. It was a condition with its own footwork.

At full speed the distinction would cost fractions of a second.

It might also keep her out of a wall.

At the end of training, her rank remained seven.

The number had been part of her identity for months.

Today it seemed small beside a name buried in an archive.

Chen sent her the run record before she left the hall.

The four reduced-speed attempts appeared without scores. Under assessment, he had written one word.

**CALIBRATING.**

Not impaired.

Not distracted.

Not failed.

Mara looked across the room. Kade was arguing with Julian about whether recognizing a trap one step earlier counted as improvement if he still entered it.

Normally she would have joined the argument.

Today she saved Chen’s run record beside her coupled-field notes.

Uncertainty did not require inaction.

It required a different kind of movement.

Then she left Kade to his training until she had evidence he could actually use.

---

She ate lunch with Sera rather than the usual first-year table.

This was partly because Sera asked no questions she did not intend to hear answered.

Sera looked at Mara’s tray.

“You are not eating enough.”

“That is Kade’s line now?”

“No. It is mine. He stole it through exposure.”

Mara took another bite.

Sera watched her.

“What?” Mara asked.

“You’re angry.”

“No.”

Sera raised one eyebrow.

Mara sighed.

“Family.”

“Ah.”

That was all.

Mara looked at her.

“You are not going to ask?”

“If you wanted to tell me, you would.”

Mara understood suddenly why Kade trusted Sera.

It was not because Sera always pushed.

It was because she knew when not to.

Mara ate in silence for another minute.

Sera used the time to complete a form on her tablet.

Not homework.

The header read **STUDENT INCIDENT RESPONSE — OBSERVATION ROTATION**.

“You applied?” Mara asked.

Sera angled the screen away by reflex, then stopped and set it flat again.

“I am applying.”

“The rotation is for second-years.”

“The policy says applicants must complete two medical labs. It does not say they must be second-years.”

“The selection notes do.”

“The selection notes are not policy.”

Mara looked at her.

Sera took another bite.

“Kade is not the only person at this table who reads around the answer.”

Her form showed two completed clinic shifts, a faculty reference request, and one empty box labeled *experience under live conditions*.

Sera had left it empty rather than turning the broken floor or suppression alarm into a credential.

Mara respected that.

“Chen would sign the reference,” Mara said.

“I asked Park.”

“Harder.”

“More relevant.”

Distinct competence.

Distinct goal.

Not a person waiting to help with Mara’s problem.

Then Mara said, “My father knows something about Kade he should not know.”

Sera stopped chewing.

“That sounds bad.”

“Yes.”

“Does Kade know?”

“Not yet.”

Sera thought about that.

“Are you protecting him or protecting your investigation?”

Mara looked at her.

Sera went back to eating.

“Those can look the same from inside your own head.”

Mara disliked how useful that was.

“I need evidence.”

“For which claim?”

“That my father’s knowledge is not incidental.”

“That is your question.” Sera tapped one finger against the table. “Kade’s question will be what the knowledge says about his father.”

Mara considered the distinction.

“I cannot answer the second without investigating the first.”

“Probably not.”

“And telling him now gives him an accusation with no direction.”

“Also true.”

Sera did not make the decision for her.

That would have been easier.

Instead she asked, “What will count as enough?”

Mara looked down at her tray.

Her father had taught her to define success conditions before entering any contest. She had begun this one without doing it.

“An independent link between Aaron Mercer and Asterion,” she said. “Not my father’s reaction. Not a public death record. Something neither of us supplies.”

“Then stop when you have that.”

“Why?”

“Because after that, it becomes his investigation too.”

“Then find the link.”

“And if it is dangerous?”

“Decide what dangerous means after you know what you found.”

That sounded like something Kade would say.

Mara was beginning to suspect Meridian was contaminating everyone.

---

She reopened Kade Mercer’s public file that afternoon.

Most of it was aggressively ordinary.

Northline Alternative.

Three prior suspensions.

Two school transfers.

A semester of academic failure inconsistent with aptitude records.

Unremarkable medical history.

Mother: Elena Mercer.

Father: deceased.

Aaron Mercer.

Industrial accident.

Mara created a blank evidence table beside the file.

Three columns.

**CLAIM. SOURCE. ALTERNATIVE.**

Her first entry was her father’s use of *structural event*.

Source: direct conversation.

Alternative: Alaric had received a private summary through a legitimate academy contact.

The alternative did not explain why he concealed the contact. It still had to remain possible.

The second entry was his reaction to Aaron’s name.

Source: expression and warning.

Alternative: he knew the public accident or knew someone else involved.

Weak evidence.

Mara disliked weak evidence most when it supported a conclusion she wanted.

She opened the request panel attached to Kade’s public file. Disciplinary summaries required a House-faculty sponsor. Medical records required a documented welfare incident. Either request would be logged to faculty.

She closed the panel.

If the answer required entering a false justification, it was not hers to retrieve.

Public first.

Institutional only where her scholar clearance already applied.

No asking Eli to find a side door.

No using Kade’s trust as permission he had not given.

Rules did not make the investigation harmless.

They made its ownership visible.

Mara stared at the name.

Then the date.

Seventeen years earlier.

Close enough to Kade’s birth that the two events almost touched.

She searched public archives.

Employment records.

Tax filings.

A death certificate.

A short local-news item about an industrial containment accident outside the city.

The article carried no byline. The photograph showed a warehouse behind a perimeter fence, smoke lifting from a roof vent, and two emergency vehicles positioned for the camera rather than the building.

Mara checked the image metadata.

Uploaded six hours after the reported accident.

Captured eleven months earlier.

That did not prove the accident was false. News archives reused file photographs constantly.

She entered it in the table anyway.

Claim: the public record had been assembled without event-specific imagery.

Alternative: ordinary local reporting shortcuts.

Still weak.

The death certificate was stronger in the wrong direction. Physician signature. Municipal seal. Registered time. No obvious amendment.

Then Mara opened the issuing physician’s public registration history.

The license number belonged to a doctor who had worked two hundred kilometers away.

Possible during a federal incident.

Not ordinary.

She wrote both.

No Asterion connection.

No Resonance registration.

No reason her father should have reacted.

She opened an International Resonance Authority research index available to high-clearance student scholars.

No Aaron Mercer.

That was more interesting.

People disappeared from public history all the time.

People did not disappear from bureaucracies.

Bureaucracies loved records more reliably than people.

She searched variants.

A. Mercer.

A. J. Mercer.

Mercer, Aaron.

Nothing.

She switched from the searchable database to the archived identifier index.

Old systems were ugly, slow, and wonderfully literal.

One result appeared.

**MERCER, A. — RESEARCH CLEARANCE RESTRICTED**

No details.

No date.

No sponsoring agency.

Just a dead link and a classification marker older than Asterion Academy’s current charter.

Mara felt her pulse change.

A second search produced a fragment associated with the same identifier.

**RELATED FACILITY: ASTERION PRECURSOR PROGRAM**

She read it twice.

Asterion had not existed under that name thirty-one years ago.

But something had existed on this site.

And Aaron Mercer had been connected to it.

Mara opened a second archive window and searched the precursor-program designation itself.

Results appeared in fragments.

Construction permits for an offshore research platform.

A federal environmental waiver.

An obsolete budget line routed through the International Resonance Authority before the Authority publicly existed under that name.

Then nothing for four years.

No photographs.

No project description.

No personnel lists.

An absence too clean to be accidental.

She searched her father’s name.

No result.

Amara Sen.

One publication citation from twenty-nine years earlier, then a missing record block.

Mara stared at it.

Her father and Sen had “disagreed for years.”

About what?

A soft knock sounded at her door.

Mara closed the archive instantly.

Kade leaned through the doorway holding two nutrition bars.

“You studying?”

“Yes.”

“That sounded guilty.”

“It wasn’t.”

“Now it sounds more guilty.”

He stepped inside without waiting for permission, which should have irritated her more than it did.

“One is allegedly chocolate,” he said. “The other admits defeat and calls itself protein.”

“Why are you here?”

“Eli said you skipped dinner.”

“So Eli sent you?”

“No. Eli proposed a drone delivery. I’m preventing property damage.”

Mara almost smiled.

Almost.

Kade placed both bars on her desk.

His gaze moved to the blank wall display.

“You really were studying.”

“Yes.”

“What?”

“Things that do not improve through curiosity.”

Kade stared at her.

“That is an extremely suspicious sentence.”

“I know.”

He waited.

Mara considered telling him everything.

Then she imagined him walking straight into Sen’s office before she had enough evidence to know which question to ask.

Kade had many strengths.

Patience remained theoretical.

“Vector theory,” she said.

He made a face.

“You’re terrible at lying.”

“I am excellent at lying.”

“No. You’re excellent at not answering.”

Mara froze.

Kade noticed.

His expression changed.

Not joking now.

“Did I say something?”

“No.”

Another lie.

Worse.

He leaned against the doorframe.

“Mara.”

She disliked people using her name like that.

Mostly because it worked.

“I found something strange,” she said.

Kade’s humor disappeared completely.

“What?”

“Not enough yet.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the answer you’re getting.”

He stared at her.

She stared back.

A month ago he would have pushed until one of them became angry.

Instead he looked at the closed archive window, then at her.

“Okay.”

That surprised her.

“You’re not going to argue?”

“I’m growing.”

She recognized the phrase from his Darius stories.

Kade picked up the protein bar and tossed it to her.

“Tell me when it becomes enough.”

He turned to leave.

“Kade.”

He looked back.

“Do you know anything about your father’s work?”

His face became completely unreadable.

“No.”

“Nothing?”

“He died when I was a baby.”

Mara nodded.

Kade watched her for another second.

Then he left.

The door closed.

Mara sat very still.

Sera’s question returned.

*Are you protecting him or protecting your investigation?*

She did not like the answer.

Both.

That meant she needed to move faster.

---

Mara went to the physical archive the next morning.

Not the restricted archive beneath old administration.

The public historical collection in the Spire.

Paper had an advantage digital systems hated.

Deletion was visible.

A librarian named Mr. Chen brought her three bound volumes covering Asterion’s institutional history before the academy charter.

“You’re researching architecture?” he asked.

“Precursor facilities.”

“Class?”

“Independent.”

That earned a look.

Elite students doing independent research was normal.

Elite students asking about pre-charter offshore facilities apparently was not.

Chen did not object.

He brought a fourth volume.

“This one is mostly photographs.”

Mara spent two hours turning pages.

Construction crews.

Early seawalls.

Aerial images of the original platform.

Scientists whose names were printed in captions.

She photographed every personnel list and wrote the volume, page, and caption date beside each image. The archive terminal offered automatic transcription. She disabled it after the system turned *containment pier* into *commencement pier* and assigned one engineer three different surnames.
Paper required patience.

It also required looking at what surrounded the answer.

The first volume described a weather station and materials laboratory built on the platform before any permanent residences. The second described emergency-energy research. The third used *academy planning site* nine years before the academy charter.

Mara added each phrase to her evidence table.

None named Aaron.

Near the end of the second hour, Mr. Chen returned with a foam support wedge for the photograph volume.

“The binding complains if you open it flat,” he said.

Mara shifted the book onto the wedge.

“Who wrote these captions?”

Chen checked the title page.

“Institutional communications, mostly. Some were transferred from the platform records.”

“Transferred when?”

“Depends which transfer.”

He pulled a paper accession sheet from the rear pocket. Three different catalog numbers had been crossed out. The oldest named no academy at all. It named the site as **ASTERION RESEARCH ANNEX**.

Mara kept her expression still.

“Can I photograph this?”

“It is part of the public volume.”

“That was not the question.”

Chen studied her for a moment.

“Yes. No flash.”

She took the picture.

The phrase did not prove anything about Aaron. It proved the academy’s history had a predecessor the official display did not describe.

Progress.

Not payoff.

Then one page had been removed.

Not torn.

Cut carefully from the binding.

The pages before and after jumped from Year Three construction to Year Five expansion.

Mara stared at the missing interval.

The cut sat close to the stitching and ran straight from top to bottom. No ragged fibers. No damage to the facing page.

Someone had used a blade while the book lay open.

“What happened to page 214?” she asked.

Chen checked the binding.

“Archive note says removed under federal retention authority twenty-six years ago.”

“Can I see the authority request?”

“If it is public.”

It was not.

Chen brought the removal register anyway. The request itself had been sealed, but the register preserved its physical description.

One photographic plate.

One personnel caption.

One equipment caption.

Mara read the entry again.

The personnel names were not reproduced.

The equipment caption was not reproduced.

But someone had considered all of them important enough to remove together.

“Do federal retention requests normally remove the page?” she asked.

“Now? No. The archive would restrict the volume.” Chen looked down at the clean cut. “Twenty-six years ago, apparently someone preferred scissors.”

“Blade.”

He glanced at her.

“Apparently someone preferred a blade.”

Mara thanked him and returned the books.

On her way out, she passed a display celebrating Asterion’s eleven publicly known Standard Eight alumni and faculty.

Eleven portraits.

Eleven names.

She had seen the display hundreds of times.

Today the spacing bothered her.

The portraits were arranged in three rows.

Four.

Four.

Three.

The final row had an empty architectural panel at the end, exactly wide enough for another portrait.

Probably design.

Probably.

Mara took a picture anyway.

Her tablet chimed as she crossed Meridian Bridge.

A message from Taren Holt.

**You missed dinner last night. Everything okay?**

Mara frowned.

She had told no one she was staying in.

Eli knew because he had passed her in the library.

Kade knew because Eli told him.

Taren should not have known yet unless somebody had told him after.

Possible.

Normal.

Still worth noting.

She typed:

**Fine. Studying.**

His response came immediately.

**Terrible hobby. See you tomorrow.**

Innocent.

Probably.

Mara stared at it longer than necessary.

Then continued walking.

At Meridian, Bell Watch had become a daily argument about whether the House Trial victory reset the historical count or began a new one.

Taren stood on a chair arguing for “Day Three of the New Era.”

Sera told him eras required more than seventy-two hours.

Kade sat at the table doing supplemental notation work and visibly pretending not to enjoy the argument.

Eli had three drone parts in his breakfast.

Ordinary.

Mara almost laughed at how much relief the scene produced.

She sat beside Kade.

He looked at her.

“Enough yet?”

“Not quite.”

He nodded and went back to his work.

No pressure.

That made the evidence she was holding feel heavier.

---

That evening she reopened the old archive page.

One collapsed metadata field remained.

She expanded it.

**PROGRAM STATUS: DISCONTINUED AFTER CONTACT EVENT**

No explanation.

No public reference.

Mara searched the phrase.

Nothing.

She searched *contact event* across every public Asterion historical record.

Nothing.

Then she tried the oldest research-index syntax.

One result appeared and vanished before the page finished loading.

A cached title fragment.

**CONTACT ZERO — COMPATIBILITY STUDY**

The system refreshed.

Access denied.

Mara stopped breathing.

Zero.

Not Kade’s score.

Not necessarily.

But the word connected the precursor site, Aaron Mercer, and a hidden event old enough to sit beneath the entire academy.

She saved the cache reference offline.

Then closed everything.

The evidence table remained.

Mara read it from the beginning.

Alaric’s restricted language.

His reaction to Aaron Mercer.

The old clearance marker.

The Asterion precursor facility.

The Research Annex accession.

A deliberately removed precursor photograph.

A contact-event status attached to the same buried program.

Contact Zero.

Individually, most entries permitted an ordinary explanation.

Together, they did not permit the same ordinary explanation.

That distinction mattered.

She added one final row.

**CLAIM:** Aaron Mercer had a concealed connection to Asterion before his reported death.

**SOURCE:** precursor clearance index plus public record chronology.

**ALTERNATIVE:** `MERCER, A.` referred to another person and Alaric’s reaction was unrelated.

Possible.

No longer persuasive.

Her success condition had been an independent link.

She had one.

The next step could have been another archive. Another syntax. Another hour spent making the evidence cleaner before allowing Kade to touch it.

That was how investigation became possession.

Mara exported the table to a local file and removed Kade’s student-file summary. The public facts about Aaron remained. The boundary she had almost crossed did not need to become part of the case simply because she had stopped herself in time.

Then she created a second copy.

One for Kade.

One for her.

Same evidence.

No private version where she remained the only person capable of interpreting his life.

Her father had asked about Kade’s broken floor.

He had reacted to Aaron Mercer’s name.

He had warned Mara not to investigate.

Someone had removed a year of the facility’s history from a physical book.

And a dead archive index connected Aaron to something called Contact Zero.

For the first time in her life, Mara looked at the academy she had spent years trying to enter and wondered what, exactly, she had been admitted into.

She opened a message to Kade.

**Tomorrow. Breakfast. I have enough.**

She stared at the words.

Then sent them.

The reply came almost immediately.

**That sounds ominous.**

Mara typed:

**It is informational.**

Three dots.

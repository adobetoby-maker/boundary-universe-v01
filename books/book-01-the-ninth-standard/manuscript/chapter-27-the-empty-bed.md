# Chapter 27 — The Empty Bed

Eli’s side of the room was too neat.

That was the first thing Kade noticed.

The second was the drone rotor on the desk.

Eli had promised to fix it three weeks earlier.

He never had.

The third was the half-finished fellowship application still floating above his workstation.

**LOW-COST FIELD STABILIZATION FOR CIVILIAN RESPONSE**

Below it, in Eli’s notes:

**Version 7 still too expensive. Stop pretending rare materials are free just because Asterion buys them.**

Kade sat on his own bed and looked at the empty one.

People kept coming by.

Food appeared.

Messages appeared.

Someone left a card signed by most of the first-year engineering cohort.

A professor delivered Eli’s tool case and placed it carefully beneath the desk.

Nobody knew what to say.

That made two of them.

Kade answered questions with the smallest possible words.

Yes.

No.

Thanks.

Later.

His old talent for making himself unavailable returned with frightening ease.

Close the doors.

Make the room smaller.

Need less.

It had worked before.

It did not work now.

Eli’s existence had spread too far through the room.

The bent shelf from the private simulation core.

A scorch mark above the desk.

A dent in the ceiling from the drone incident everyone officially agreed had not happened.

Twenty-three cables labeled in handwriting only Eli could read.

A jar of terrible protein powder Kade had repeatedly threatened to throw away.

The mug that said **WORLD’S MOST ADEQUATE ENGINEER**.

Kade had bought it as a joke after House Trial.

Eli had used it every day.

Kade hated the mug.

He hated that buying it now felt important.

House Meridian had a rule about missing students.

Kade learned this because at noon Sera came in carrying a blue cloth strip and tied it around the empty bedpost.

“What is that?” he asked.

“Seven-day hold.”

“For the room?”

“For the person.”

Kade looked at her.

Sera adjusted the knot.

“When somebody is missing, dead, evacuated, arrested, transferred unexpectedly—whatever—the House does not clear their room for seven days.”

“That is a strange list.”

“Meridian has history.”

“Why seven?”

“So administration cannot turn a person into available capacity before their friends learn what happened.”

Kade stared at the strip.

Blue cloth.

Nothing dramatic.

No official crest.

Another ritual nobody had explained because it had existed before him.

“What happens after seven?”

Sera looked at Eli’s bed.

“Depends who comes back.”

The wording caught Kade.

Who comes back.

Not what gets removed.

He looked away before hope could become something sharp enough to hurt.

Mara came in that afternoon.

She saw him awake and sat on the floor with her back against Eli’s bed.

No questions.

No careful face.

They stayed that way for twenty minutes.

Kade finally said, “You don’t have to stay.”

“I know.”

That was why he let her.

After she left, Sera brought Eli’s engineering notebook.

“I found this in response staging.”

Kade stared at it.

“You read it?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because it isn’t mine.”

Consent again.

Even grief apparently had boundaries.

Kade took the notebook.

Sera sat across from him.

For once, she did not steal food or tell him what he was doing wrong.

“How was the response debrief?” Kade asked.

Sera blinked.

“Why do you care?”

“Because you were good.”

She looked away.

“They offered me accelerated Response Corps assessment.”

Kade stared.

“That’s huge.”

“Probably.”

“You sound terrible at being happy.”

Sera’s jaw tightened.

“Eli is dead.”

The sentence hit the room.

Kade looked at the empty bed.

“Presumed.”

Sera closed her eyes.

“Kade.”

“I know.”

She took a breath.

“Part of me was still happy when they offered it.”

He looked back at her.

“That feels wrong.”

“Yes.”

“It isn’t.”

Sera studied him.

“How do you know?”

“I don’t.”

He thought about fame after House Trial.

About liking applause while being embarrassed that he liked it.

“Maybe people can feel two things without one canceling the other.”

Sera’s expression softened.

“Look at you.”

“Don’t.”

“Growing.”

“Stop.”

She almost smiled.

Then she left.

---

Asterion resumed classes the next morning.

Not all of them.

The eastern engineering wing remained sealed, and any course that used it moved online or into borrowed rooms.

But breakfast happened at seven.

Vector Analysis happened at eight.

Combat Theory happened at ten.

The ranking boards updated at noon.

Kade hated the academy for continuing.

He also understood that ten thousand students could not remain emotionally synchronized with one missing boy they did not know.

That felt like betrayal anyway.

Professor Amadi did not mention Eli when Kade entered Vector Analysis alone.

She only looked at the empty seat beside him and said, “Navarro’s assignments are suspended.”

Kade sat.

The statement was practical.

Mercifully practical.

Halfway through class, Amadi assigned a three-person coupling problem.

Kade turned automatically toward Eli.

The chair was empty.

His mind stopped.

Across the aisle, Owen Park—who had once argued that a field solved symmetrically even when bodies did not—looked at the empty chair too.

Then moved his own tablet over.

“You can work with us.”

Kade almost said no.

The old reflex came ready-made.

Do not replace him.

Do not make the empty space less empty.

Then he recognized the stupidity in the thought.

Accepting another person did not erase Eli.

“Okay,” Kade said.

Owen nodded.

No speech.

They worked.

Kade finished third.

He did not care.

That was how he knew something was wrong.

After class, Amadi stopped him.

“Mercer.”

He turned.

“Grief does not excuse missing every assignment.”

Kade stared.

Then anger arrived.

Amadi continued before he could use it.

“It does justify asking for help before you do.”

The anger lost direction.

She sent a revised workload to his tablet.

“Two extensions. One office hour. No penalty.”

Kade looked at her.

“Why say the first part like that?”

“Because you would hear pity in anything softer.”

He hated how correct she was.

“Thank you.”

“Do the work.”

“Recovered.”

Outside, the Spire elevators carried students up and down like nothing had happened.

Kade stood among them and understood one more irritating thing about belonging.

The world did not stop when someone important disappeared.

You had to keep living inside the place that remained.

---

Julian handled Meridian logistics Kade could not bring himself to think about.

Room rotations.

Training cancellations.

Messages to faculty.

He never announced that he was doing it.

Kade found out because a schedule problem disappeared before anyone asked him to solve it.

Julian came by that evening.

Kade said, “Why are you doing this?”

“Doing what?”

“Managing everything.”

Julian leaned against the desk.

“My father says leadership is what people call control when the outcome is good.”

“That sounds healthy.”

“It isn’t.”

Kade looked at him.

Julian’s face was harder than usual.

“He thinks if you are the best person in a room, every failure in it belongs to you.”

Kade understood immediately why Julian had cut him off outside Meridian.

“You don’t believe that.”

“I am trying not to.”

There it was.

A crack in Rank One.

Not weakness.

Pressure.

Julian looked at Eli’s empty bed.

“I can organize meals. I cannot fix this.”

Kade said, “Welcome to the group.”

Julian gave him a tired smile.

Before leaving, Julian nodded at the blue strip tied to the bedpost.

“My House does three days.”

“Meridian does seven.”

“I know.”

“Of course you do.”

Julian looked at it.

“Seven is better.”

Then he left.

Kade wondered what it said about Asterion that every House had a ritual for people who did not come back on schedule.

He did not like the answer.

---

The incident investigators asked for Eli’s tablet two days after the blast.

Kade watched a systems officer unplug it from the desk.

“Do you need the whole thing?”

“Yes.”

“What are you looking for?”

“Network activity before the incident.”

Kade thought of NULL-7.

“The dead maintenance channel?”

The officer looked at him.

“Navarro used one?”

“NULL-7.”

The officer wrote it down.

“Anything else?”

“He said standard traffic was being mirrored.”

“We know.”

Kade’s attention sharpened.

“You know?”

The officer hesitated.

“Some status packets were duplicated to an unauthorized endpoint.”

“Directorate?”

“We have not attributed it publicly.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“No.”

Kade almost pushed.

Then the officer added, “There is also one unresolved device identifier from the lower lab.”

Hope moved too fast.

“What does that mean?”

“It means a device stopped reporting before the main coolant failure and never reappeared on the recovery network.”

“Eli’s tablet?”

“No. Different hardware.”

“What hardware?”

“We do not know.”

Kade stared.

The officer’s face remained professional.

“Do not turn an incomplete log into a story, Mercer.”

Kade almost laughed.

Everyone in his life had apparently agreed on that sentence.

“What if the story is already there?”

“Then evidence will still be there tomorrow.”

The officer left with Eli’s tablet.

Kade sat beside the empty desk.

One unresolved device identifier.

It could be a sensor.

A maintenance controller.

A broken drone part.

Anything.

He refused to make it Eli.

Hope without evidence felt too much like another way to avoid grief.

He opened Eli’s notebook instead.

Not the private personal pages.

The project section.

Most of it was engineering shorthand.

One page had a heading large enough to understand.

**WHY CIVILIAN STABILIZATION MATTERS**

Below it:

*Because people should not need to be rich, military, famous, or lucky to survive the weird future we are building.*

Kade read the sentence three times.

He had known what Eli wanted.

He had not understood how much.

Now the unfinished fellowship application on the desk felt less like paperwork and more like a missing future.

There was a second note beneath it.

**If version 8 works, ask Sera to break it. She will enjoy that too much.**

Kade laughed.

One sound.

Then covered his face with both hands.

The laugh hurt more than crying would have.

He did both anyway.

---

At dinner, Meridian left Eli’s chair empty.

Nobody had assigned it.

Nobody sat there.

A plate remained in the cupboard rather than on the table, because apparently the House had rules about that too.

Kade sat beside the empty space.

For ten minutes, conversation stayed quiet.

Then Priya complained about a training instructor.

Anton argued with her.

Nessa told a story about a second-year who had accidentally field-locked himself inside a storage room.

Someone laughed.

The room changed.

Kade felt anger.

How could they laugh?

Then he remembered Eli laughing in every room where something had gone wrong.

The anger broke apart.

He let himself laugh at the story.

The empty chair remained empty.

Both things could be true.

---

The old instinct returned after midnight.

If caring could do this, care less.

If belonging could make a room hurt this much, make the room smaller.

Kade sat on the floor with his back against his bed.

Mara had gone.

Sera had response training at six.

Julian had left three messages he had not answered.

Darius had sent three terrible jokes and one message that simply said:

**I’m here.**

Elena had sent a photograph of Northline’s vending machine because Darius had apparently convinced her that was supportive.

Kade stared at all of it.

The hole did not become smaller.

Neither did the world.

Sera found him awake at 00:41.

“You eating?”

“No.”

“That wasn’t a question.”

“Then why add the question mark?”

She put a bowl on his desk.

Kade did not touch it.

Sera sat beside him.

“He mattered,” she said.

Kade closed his eyes.

“I know.”

“Then let it hurt.”

He opened them.

“That’s your advice?”

“Yes.”

“Terrible.”

“Probably.”

He looked at the empty bed.

“What if this is the part I was right about?”

“What part?”

“Needing people gives them a way to hurt you.”

Sera was quiet for a long moment.

Then she said, “Of course it does.”

Kade looked at her.

She shrugged.

“What did you think the alternative was? A trick where you get connection without risk?”

“That would be nice.”

“Doesn’t exist.”

He hated that answer.

Sera stood.

“Memorial is tomorrow.”

Kade looked at the empty bed.

“I can’t.”

“Yes, you can.”

“I don’t want to.”

“That’s different.”

She headed for the door.

“Sera.”

She looked back.

“The unresolved device thing.”

“What about it?”

“You think it means anything?”

Sera’s face stayed still.

“I think investigators have incomplete data.”

“That was very careful.”

“Yes.”

Kade waited.

Sera looked at Eli’s bed.

“Do not use hope to skip grief.”

The sentence landed exactly where it needed to.

Kade nodded.

She left.

He stayed awake until sunrise.

For the first time in years, he did not tell himself none of it mattered.

He did not turn a missing device into a miracle.

He did not turn an empty bed into proof that caring had been a mistake.

He let the room remain too large.

He let the blue strip stay tied to the bedpost.

And when morning came, he got dressed for the memorial.

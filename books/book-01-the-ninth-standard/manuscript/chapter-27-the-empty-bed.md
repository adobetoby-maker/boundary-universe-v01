# Chapter 27 — The Empty Bed

Eli’s side of the room was too neat.

That was the first thing Kade noticed.

The second was the drone rotor on the desk.

Eli had promised to fix it three weeks earlier.

He never had.

The third was the half-finished fellowship application still floating above his workstation.

**LOW-COST FIELD STABILIZATION FOR CIVILIAN RESPONSE**

Below it, in Eli’s notes:

**Version 7 still too expensive. Stop pretending rare materials are free just because Asterion buys them.**

Kade sat on his own bed and looked at the empty one.

People kept coming by.

Food appeared.

Messages appeared.

Someone left a card signed by most of the first-year engineering cohort.

A professor delivered Eli’s tool case and placed it carefully beneath the desk.

She did not leave immediately.

The case was gray composite with one broken latch and a strip of orange tape across the handle. Kade had carried it back from Systems Lab once. Eli had spent the entire walk explaining that good tools were a form of institutional memory.

Kade had told him tools could not remember things.

Eli had replied that this was why Kade was not allowed to borrow the precision driver.

The professor crouched beside the case and pressed the broken latch twice, as if the second attempt might change its mind.

“He repaired this for everyone except himself,” she said.

Kade looked at the case.

“That sounds like him.”

She held out a slim academy form.

**TEMPORARY CUSTODY OF STUDENT PROJECT MATERIALS**

The line beneath Eli’s name asked for a responsible recipient.

“Administration needs someone to acknowledge the transfer,” she said. “It does not assign ownership.”

Kade did not take it.

“Why me?”

“Because Navarro listed you as his emergency room contact.”

That was such an Eli category that Kade almost smiled.

Not emergency contact.

Emergency *room* contact, because apparently buildings had emergencies too.

“What happens if I sign?”

“The case remains here until the investigation releases it. If no one accepts temporary custody, academy storage collects it this afternoon.”

Storage.

A clean room. A numbered shelf. Eli’s tools becoming inventory while his bed was still warm enough to remember him.

Kade took the form.

The signature field waited.

He could hear Eli objecting to every part of this transaction. The vague word *materials*. The absence of an itemized list. The assumption that a signature meant informed acceptance of anything.

“There’s no inventory.”

The professor looked at him more closely.

“No.”

“Then I’m acknowledging custody of an undefined set.”

“That is correct.”

“He would hate this form.”

“He filed three complaints about it last term.”

There it was.

Not a ghost.

A procedural irritation Eli had left embedded in the academy.

The professor opened the case. Every tool sat in a fitted slot except one empty space shaped like a narrow wrench. She activated the inventory panel inside the lid. Forty-two entries appeared, each with a serial number and condition note.

“He made his own list,” she said.

Of course he had.

At the bottom, Eli had added:

**If this case is returned because I am dead, missing, expelled, or finally arrested for unauthorized fabrication, check Lab Five for the ten-millimeter torque key. It is not lost. It is being useful somewhere else.**

Kade read the sentence twice.

“Can I sign his list instead?”

The professor turned the panel toward him.

“I will attach it as the controlling inventory.”

He signed beneath Eli’s last entry.

His hand shook halfway through his surname.

The professor pretended to inspect the case until he finished.

Then she closed the lid, leaving the orange handle turned outward.

“Navarro’s fellowship committee suspended review,” she said. “Suspended is not rejected.”

Kade’s throat closed.

“You think that distinction helps?”

“No.”

She stood.

“But it belongs to him, so I am keeping it accurate.”

That helped more than comfort would have.

Nobody knew what to say.

That made two of them.

Kade answered questions with the smallest possible words.

Yes.

No.

Thanks.

Later.

His old talent for making himself unavailable returned with frightening ease.

Close the doors.

Make the room smaller.

Need less.

It had worked before.

It did not work now.

Eli’s existence had spread too far through the room.

The bent shelf from the private simulation core.

A scorch mark above the desk.

A dent in the ceiling from the drone incident everyone officially agreed had not happened.

Twenty-three cables labeled in handwriting only Eli could read.

A jar of terrible protein powder Kade had repeatedly threatened to throw away.

The mug that said **WORLD’S MOST ADEQUATE ENGINEER**.

Kade had bought it as a joke after House Trial.

Eli had used it every day.

Kade hated the mug.

He hated that buying it now felt important.

At eleven, a housing coordinator arrived with a tablet and stopped at the open door.

“Navarro’s room?” she asked.

Kade sat up.

“Why do you need a House representative?”

“Meridian can place a hold before housing makes any disposition. I come back after their noon round.”

She waited for Kade to wave her in.

Even housing policy apparently understood that a bedroom had a boundary.

The coordinator read from her tablet without entering.

“I need confirmation of occupancy, perishables, dependent devices, hazardous materials, and any living organisms.”

Kade looked around.

“Does protein powder count as hazardous?”

“Not under housing policy.”

“It should.”

The answer came automatically.

For one second, it sounded like the room before.

The coordinator’s face did not perform sympathy. She merely waited.

“No organisms,” Kade said. “One battery stack under the desk. It’s discharged. The drone cells are in the wall locker.”

“Perishables?”

Kade opened the small refrigeration drawer between the desks.

Half a container of noodles. Two sealed nutrient drinks. One piece of fruit that had crossed from food into research.

Each item had a label.

**MINE.**

**ALSO MINE.**

**KADE, THIS DOES NOT BECOME YOURS WHEN I AM IN CLASS.**

Kade shut the drawer.

“Yes.”

“Do you want House support to clear them?”

He pictured the labels in a disposal chute.

“Not today.”

The coordinator marked the answer.

“A House hold, if Meridian places one, prevents reassignment. It does not require you to preserve spoiled food.”

“I understand.”

“Most people do not on the first day.”

She turned the tablet off.

“I’ll return after Meridian’s noon round. Nothing in this room moves before then unless it is unsafe.”

Kade looked at the open doorway after she left.

The academy was still taking attendance.

For now, absence counted as a kind of presence.

House Meridian had a rule about missing students.

Kade learned this because at noon Sera came in carrying a blue cloth strip and tied it around the empty bedpost.

“What is that?” he asked.

“Seven-day hold.”

“For the room?”

“For the person.”

Kade looked at her.

Sera adjusted the knot.

“When somebody is missing, dead, evacuated, arrested, transferred unexpectedly—whatever—the House does not clear their room for seven days.”

“That is a strange list.”

“Meridian has history.”

“Why seven?”

“So administration cannot turn a person into available capacity before their friends learn what happened.”

Kade stared at the strip.

Blue cloth.

Nothing dramatic.

No official crest.

Another ritual nobody had explained because it had existed before him.

“What happens after seven?”

Sera looked at Eli’s bed.

“Depends who comes back.”

The wording caught Kade.

Who comes back.

Not what gets removed.

He looked away before hope could become something sharp enough to hurt.

Sera held out the loose end of the cloth.

“Pull.”

“You already tied it.”

“First knot marks the bed. Second means somebody accepts the hold.”

Kade stared at her hand.

“Accepts what?”

“Seven days of answering housing. Keeping people from turning this into a shrine Eli would hate. Making sure you leave the room sometimes.”

“That last one sounds invented.”

“Oldest part of the rule.”

He took the cloth.

The weave was rough and slightly faded. It had been cut from something larger, then reused enough times for the edges to soften.

“Whose was it?”

“No idea.”

“Comforting.”

“The House keeps a roll in the bell cabinet. You do not get a fresh strip just because your grief is special.”

That sounded cruel until Kade understood it.

Other hands had pulled this cloth tight.

Other rooms had become impossible to enter and worse to leave.

Meridian had not solved any of those absences. It had only kept a piece of fabric because the next person might need proof that the House knew what to do for seven days.

Kade pulled.

Sera finished the second knot.

It sat crooked against the bedpost.

“Now what?” he asked.

She opened the House schedule on her tablet.

Every hour through the memorial already had a name beside it. Meal delivery. Faculty contact. Housing contact. Door duty during visiting hours. One line simply read **SIT HERE IF HE ASKS**, and six people had added themselves before the slot closed.

Kade’s chest hurt.

“Who made this?”

“Julian started logistics. Priya threatened him until he let Meridian run its own room.”

“He isn’t even in this House.”

“That was Priya’s point.”

Sera showed him two controls at the bottom.

**DOOR OPEN**

**DOOR CLOSED**

“You choose. It can change whenever you want.”

Kade selected **DOOR CLOSED**.

Then, before he could turn privacy into disappearance, he set it to reopen at fourteen hundred.

Sera said nothing about the choice.

That was why he did not change it back.

Mara came in that afternoon.

She saw him awake and sat on the floor with her back against Eli’s bed.

No questions.

No careful face.

They stayed that way for twenty minutes.

The fellowship application continued floating above Eli’s desk.

At minute six, Mara asked, “Do you want that off?”

Kade looked at the words.

“No.”

She left it alone.

At minute eleven, his tablet accumulated four more requests for comment. One came from the academy bulletin. Two came from outside news services. The fourth offered compensation for an exclusive account of *the Lab Six tragedy*.

Kade turned the tablet facedown.

“I can sort those,” Mara said.

“Into what?”

“People who need an answer, people who think they are owed one, and people who should be introduced to Sera.”

“That third category seems unsafe.”

“It is.”

Kade handed her the tablet.

Mara did not unlock it.

“You need to tell me what I am allowed to open.”

He nearly said *everything* because decisions required effort and effort was in short supply.

That would have made exhaustion into consent.

“Only messages from today. Not Elena. Not Darius. Don’t answer anyone. Just flag them.”

Mara nodded.

She worked without narrating. Faculty went into one group. Meridian into another. Media disappeared behind a muted filter. She paused over a message from House Sol asking whether Kade wanted his evening training slot held.

“What do you want?”

“I don’t care.”

“That is not a choice.”

“Cancel it.”

“Today or permanently?”

The question irritated him.

Which meant it mattered.

“Today.”

She marked one session and left the rest untouched.

At minute eighteen, a new message arrived from Darius.

**No answer needed. Your mom ate. I checked.**

Mara turned the screen so Kade could read it without opening the thread.

He swallowed.

“Flag that one.”

“Which group?”

“People who need an answer.”

Mara marked it.

Then she placed the locked tablet between them rather than keeping it.

Kade finally said, “You don’t have to stay.”

“I know.”

That was why he let her.

After she left, Sera brought Eli’s engineering notebook.

“I found this in response staging.”

Kade stared at it.

“You read it?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because it isn’t mine.”

Consent again.

Even grief apparently had boundaries.

Kade took the notebook.

Sera sat across from him.

For once, she did not steal food or tell him what he was doing wrong.

“How was the response debrief?” Kade asked.

Sera blinked.

“Why do you care?”

“Because you were good.”

She looked away.

“They offered me accelerated Response Corps assessment.”

Kade stared.

“That’s huge.”

“Probably.”

“You sound terrible at being happy.”

Sera’s jaw tightened.

“Eli is dead.”

The sentence hit the room.

Kade looked at the empty bed.

“Presumed.”

Sera closed her eyes.

“Kade.”

“I know.”

She took a breath.

“Part of me was still happy when they offered it.”

He looked back at her.

“That feels wrong.”

“Yes.”

“It isn’t.”

Sera studied him.

“How do you know?”

“I don’t.”

He thought about fame after House Trial.

About liking applause while being embarrassed that he liked it.

“Maybe people can feel two things without one canceling the other.”

Sera’s expression softened.

“Look at you.”

“Don’t.”

“Growing.”

“Stop.”

She almost smiled.

Then she left.

---

Asterion resumed classes the next morning.

Not all of them.

The eastern engineering wing remained sealed, and any course that used it moved online or into borrowed rooms.

But breakfast happened at seven.

Vector Analysis happened at eight.

Combat Theory happened at ten.

The ranking boards updated at noon.

Kade hated the academy for continuing.

He also understood that ten thousand students could not remain emotionally synchronized with one missing boy they did not know.

That felt like betrayal anyway.

Professor Amadi did not mention Eli when Kade entered Vector Analysis alone.

She only looked at the empty seat beside him and said, “Navarro’s assignments are suspended.”

Kade sat.

The statement was practical.

Mercifully practical.

Halfway through class, Amadi assigned a three-person coupling problem.

Kade turned automatically toward Eli.

The chair was empty.

His mind stopped.

Across the aisle, Owen Park—who had once argued that a field solved symmetrically even when bodies did not—looked at the empty chair too.

Then moved his own tablet over.

“You can work with us.”

Kade almost said no.

The old reflex came ready-made.

Do not replace him.

Do not make the empty space less empty.

Then he recognized the stupidity in the thought.

Accepting another person did not erase Eli.

“Okay,” Kade said.

Owen nodded.

No speech.

They worked.

The exercise used three linked field plates and a moving load. One operator maintained the center plate. One shifted power between the outer plates. The third tracked the load and called transfers before it crossed a seam.

Kade took center because that was where he always stood.

Owen’s partner took the left plate.

Owen opened the instrumentation channel.

“Ready,” he said.

Amadi started the first run.

The load moved.

Kade stabilized center and waited for a quiet stream of numbers from his right.

None came.

The load crossed the first seam. Their left plate dipped. Kade compensated too late, pulling power from center after the transfer had already begun.

All three plates flashed red.

**DISCONTINUITY: 0.31 SECONDS**

Amadi reset them without comment.

Owen looked at Kade.

“You did not call the transfer.”

“Instrumentation calls it.”

“Not on this exercise.”

Kade checked the instructions.

Owen was right.

Eli would have called anyway. He never treated a role boundary as a reason to let a system fail quietly.

“Again,” Kade said.

They reset.

The second run began.

Kade tracked the load himself, called left, then shifted center compensation. Owen reported the seam value after the move.

“Earlier,” Kade said.

“The sensor only confirms after crossing.”

“Eli could predict it from the acceleration curve.”

The words came out before Kade could stop them.

Owen’s hands remained over the instrument controls.

“I’m not Eli.”

Kade’s face went hot.

“I know.”

“Then stop giving me his job.”

The load reached the second seam.

Their right plate dropped. Owen’s partner caught it with a rough overcorrection that kept the system active and ruined their efficiency score.

The run ended amber instead of red.

Owen pointed at the acceleration display.

“I can widen the sensor window. That gives you the curve before the seam, but it adds noise.”

“How much?”

“Enough that you cannot use the raw number.”

Kade studied the display.

Eli would have cleaned the noise.

Eli was not there.

“Don’t give me a number,” Kade said. “Give me direction and confidence.”

Owen changed the display. The precise trace became three bands: rising, steady, falling. A confidence value appeared beside each.

“That is less information,” Owen’s partner said.

“It is information we can use in time,” Kade replied.

Third run.

“Rising, high confidence,” Owen called.

“Left in two,” Kade said.

The partner prepared her plate.

“One. Transfer.”

The load crossed. Center lost power exactly as left accepted it.

No discontinuity.

At the second seam, Owen called medium confidence. Kade delayed half a count. The partner adjusted her timing without waiting for another instruction.

The load finished.

Their score was not elegant.

It was stable.

Owen saved the configuration.

“Navarro would probably hate those bands,” he said.

“He would make five better ones and insist he had agreed with you from the beginning.”

Owen’s partner gave a brief laugh.

Kade’s hands tightened against the edge of the desk.

Then loosened.

Another person could be unlike Eli and still help build something that held.

Kade finished third.

He did not care.

That was how he knew something was wrong.

After class, Amadi stopped him.

“Mercer.”

He turned.

“Grief does not excuse missing every assignment.”

Kade stared.

Then anger arrived.

Amadi continued before he could use it.

“It does justify asking for help before you do.”

The anger lost direction.

She sent a revised workload to his tablet.

“Two extensions. One office hour. No penalty.”

Kade looked at her.

“Why say the first part like that?”

“Because you would hear pity in anything softer.”

He hated how correct she was.

“Thank you.”

“Do the work.”

“Recovered.”

Outside, the Spire elevators carried students up and down like nothing had happened.

Kade stood among them and understood one more irritating thing about belonging.

The world did not stop when someone important disappeared.

You had to keep living inside the place that remained.

---

Julian handled Meridian logistics Kade could not bring himself to think about.

Room rotations.

Training cancellations.

Messages to faculty.

He never announced that he was doing it.

Kade found out because a schedule problem disappeared before anyone asked him to solve it.

Julian came by that evening.

Kade said, “Why are you doing this?”

“Doing what?”

“Managing everything.”

Julian leaned against the desk.

“My father says leadership is what people call control when the outcome is good.”

“That sounds healthy.”

“It isn’t.”

Kade looked at him.

Julian’s face was harder than usual.

“He thinks if you are the best person in a room, every failure in it belongs to you.”

Kade understood immediately why Julian had cut him off outside Meridian.

“You don’t believe that.”

“I am trying not to.”

There it was.

A crack in Rank One.

Not weakness.

Pressure.

Julian looked at Eli’s empty bed.

“I can organize meals. I cannot fix this.”

Kade said, “Welcome to the group.”

Julian gave him a tired smile.

Before leaving, Julian nodded at the blue strip tied to the bedpost.

“My House does three days.”

“Meridian does seven.”

“I know.”

“Of course you do.”

Julian looked at it.

“Seven is better.”

Then he left.

Kade wondered what it said about Asterion that every House had a ritual for people who did not come back on schedule.

He did not like the answer.

---

The incident investigators asked for Eli’s tablet two days after the blast.

Kade watched a systems officer unplug it from the desk.

“Do you need the whole thing?”

“Yes.”

“What are you looking for?”

“Network activity before the incident.”

Kade thought of NULL-7.

“The dead maintenance channel?”

The officer looked at him.

“Navarro used one?”

“NULL-7.”

The officer wrote it down.

“Anything else?”

“He said standard traffic was being mirrored.”

“We know.”

Kade’s attention sharpened.

“You know?”

The officer hesitated.

“Some status packets were duplicated to an unauthorized endpoint.”

“Directorate?”

“We have not attributed it publicly.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“No.”

Kade almost pushed.

Then the officer added, “There is also one unresolved device identifier from the lower lab.”

Hope moved too fast.

“What does that mean?”

“It means a device stopped reporting before the main coolant failure and never reappeared on the recovery network.”

“Eli’s tablet?”

“No. Different hardware.”

“What hardware?”

“We do not know.”

Kade stared.

The officer’s face remained professional.

“Do not turn an incomplete log into a story, Mercer.”

Kade almost laughed.

Everyone in his life had apparently agreed on that sentence.

“What if the story is already there?”

“Then evidence will still be there tomorrow.”

The officer left with Eli’s tablet.

Kade sat beside the empty desk.

One unresolved device identifier.

It could be a sensor.

A maintenance controller.

A broken drone part.

Anything.

He refused to make it Eli.

Hope without evidence felt too much like another way to avoid grief.

He opened Eli’s notebook instead.

Not the private personal pages.

The project section.

Most of it was engineering shorthand.

One page had a heading large enough to understand.

**WHY CIVILIAN STABILIZATION MATTERS**

Below it:

*Because people should not need to be rich, military, famous, or lucky to survive the weird future we are building.*

Kade read the sentence three times.

He had known what Eli wanted.

He had not understood how much.

Now the unfinished fellowship application on the desk felt less like paperwork and more like a missing future.

There was a second note beneath it.

**If version 8 works, ask Sera to break it. She will enjoy that too much.**

Kade laughed.

One sound.

Then covered his face with both hands.

The laugh hurt more than crying would have.

He did both anyway.

---

At dinner, Meridian left Eli’s chair empty.

Nobody had assigned it.

Nobody sat there.

A plate remained in the cupboard rather than on the table, because apparently the House had rules about that too.

Kade sat beside the empty space.

For ten minutes, conversation stayed quiet.

Then Priya complained about a training instructor.

Anton argued with her.

Nessa told a story about a second-year who had accidentally field-locked himself inside a storage room.

Someone laughed.

The room changed.

Kade felt anger.

How could they laugh?

Then he remembered Eli laughing in every room where something had gone wrong.

The anger broke apart.

He let himself laugh at the story.

The empty chair remained empty.

Both things could be true.

---

The old instinct returned after midnight.

If caring could do this, care less.

If belonging could make a room hurt this much, make the room smaller.

Kade sat on the floor with his back against his bed.

Mara had gone.

Sera had response training at six.

Julian had left three messages he had not answered.

Darius had sent three terrible jokes and one message that simply said:

**I’m here.**

Elena had sent a photograph of Northline’s vending machine because Darius had apparently convinced her that was supportive.

Kade stared at all of it.

The hole did not become smaller.

Neither did the world.

At 00:06, he called Darius.

Darius answered before the first full ring.

The image shook, showed a ceiling tile, then corrected to half his face beneath Northline’s security desk.

“Hey,” Darius said.

Kade had no next line.

Darius did not rescue him.

Behind him, Room Four’s vending machine hummed with the same mechanical indifference it had given every disaster Kade brought into that building.

“Why are you at Northline?” Kade asked.

“Night shift called out. Your mom was reorganizing a kitchen drawer by frequency of use, so I left before she assigned me a category.”

“She does that when she’s scared.”

“I know.”

Kade looked at Elena’s message.

“Is she okay?”

“No.”

The answer should have been too blunt.

It was a relief.

Darius adjusted the tablet against the desk. The full frame settled: security monitors, a stack of visitor forms, the orange chair Kade used to sleep in when the buses stopped running.

Northline at midnight.

Nothing there had become less ugly while he was gone.

“She ate,” Darius said. “She called the academy twice. They told her the same thing they told you. She asked me if flying to the island would help, and I said they probably have food there already.”

Kade’s mouth moved despite him.

“That was your argument?”

“I had others. That one worked.”

Silence stretched.

A student crossed behind Darius carrying a laundry bag and stopped.

“You Mercer?” the student asked.

Darius turned his head.

“He is currently a phone.”

“My cousin says he’s famous.”

“Your cousin needs hobbies.”

The student considered Kade’s face on the screen, decided fame looked disappointing, and continued down the hall.

Darius waited until the corridor emptied.

“Tell me one thing about him.”

Kade looked at Eli’s desk.

“You know things about him.”

“I know he’s an engineer. I know he made a drone insult me. I know he once sent a six-page correction to a joke because my battery math was inaccurate.”

“It was inaccurate.”

“See, that. But tell me one I don’t know.”

Kade almost refused.

Turning a person into stories people requested was still a form of making them available.

This was Darius, though.

Not a reporter.

Not the academy.

Someone asking for a piece he could carry.

“He labels food like it’s contested territory,” Kade said.

“That is reasonable with you around.”

“He talks in his sleep before engineering deadlines.”

“Useful information?”

“Once he recited a load table for eight minutes. I recorded it because I thought he was awake.”

“Please tell me you still have that.”

“He deleted it.”

“Tragic.”

The word entered the conversation lightly and changed weight before it landed.

Darius’s expression tightened.

Kade looked away from the screen.

“He wanted to build rescue equipment people could afford.”

“You told me.”

“I didn’t understand it.”

The half-finished application cast pale letters over the desk.

“I thought it was an engineering project. A thing he liked because he likes fixing things. But he had this whole test model. He knew which rural services couldn’t buy academy materials. He knew procurement limits. He was designing for people he had never met.”

“Sounds annoying.”

“Extremely.”

“Good.”

Kade looked back.

“Good?”

“Now I have something specific to miss.”

The sentence opened him.

Kade pressed the heel of his hand against his mouth.

Darius stayed on the screen.

No instructions.

No promise that Eli would be found.

No warning that he might not.

Just Northline’s ugly security desk holding its end of a call across an ocean.

When Kade could speak again, he said, “I don’t know what to tell Mom.”

“Tell her you’re alive.”

“She knows.”

“Parents are inefficient. They require updates.”

“You are not a parent.”

“I have supervised you near vending equipment.”

“Not the same.”

“Debatable.”

Kade opened Elena’s message thread.

He typed:

**Still here. Ate dinner. Memorial tomorrow. I’ll call after.**

He showed Darius before sending it.

“Too much?”

“For your mother? Barely proof of life.”

Kade added:

**I love you.**

Then sent it.

Darius nodded once.

“Do you want me to keep talking?”

“About what?”

“I can explain why the vending machine is now banned from accepting credit.”

Kade leaned his head against the bed frame.

“That sounds like your fault.”

“It is a story with multiple valid interpretations.”

Darius began at the least relevant possible point.

Kade listened.

The story was terrible.

The call lasted twenty-seven minutes.

Sera found him awake at 00:41.

“You eating?”

“No.”

“That wasn’t a question.”

“Then why add the question mark?”

She put a bowl on his desk.

Kade did not touch it.

Sera sat beside him.

“He mattered,” she said.

Kade closed his eyes.

“I know.”

“Then let it hurt.”

He opened them.

“That’s your advice?”

“Yes.”

“Terrible.”

“Probably.”

He looked at the empty bed.

“What if this is the part I was right about?”

“What part?”

“Needing people gives them a way to hurt you.”

Sera was quiet for a long moment.

Then she said, “Of course it does.”

Kade looked at her.

She shrugged.

“What did you think the alternative was? A trick where you get connection without risk?”

“That would be nice.”

“Doesn’t exist.”

He hated that answer.

Sera stood.

“Memorial is tomorrow.”

Kade looked at the empty bed.

“I can’t.”

“Yes, you can.”

“I don’t want to.”

“That’s different.”

She headed for the door.

“Sera.”

She looked back.

“The unresolved device thing.”

“What about it?”

“You think it means anything?”

Sera’s face stayed still.

“I think investigators have incomplete data.”

“That was very careful.”

“Yes.”

Kade waited.

Sera looked at Eli’s bed.

“Do not use hope to skip grief.”

The sentence landed exactly where it needed to.

Kade nodded.

She left.

He stayed awake until sunrise.

For the first time in years, he did not tell himself none of it mattered.

He did not turn a missing device into a miracle.

He did not turn an empty bed into proof that caring had been a mistake.

He let the room remain too large.

He let the blue strip stay tied to the bedpost.

And when morning came, he got dressed for the memorial.

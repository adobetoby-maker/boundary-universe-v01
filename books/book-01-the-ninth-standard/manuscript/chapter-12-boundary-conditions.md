# Chapter 12 — Boundary Conditions

Sen did not begin with an answer.

She began with a line.

On the wall display, a clear glass of water appeared on a wooden table. A thin white box formed around the glass.

“What is the system?” Sen asked.

Kade looked at her.

“Is this a trick?”

“Probably.”

“The glass and water.”

Sen drew a larger box around the table.

“And now?”

“The table too.”

A third box enclosed the room.

Kade sighed.

“I understand boundaries.”

“No. You understand that somebody can draw them.”

She added a laptop beside the glass.

The water tipped.

Kade watched it spread across the table toward the computer.

Sen froze the image.

“Did the water change?”

“No.”

“Did the table?”

“No.”

“Then why did the problem change?”

“Because now the laptop is about to die.”

“Exactly. The physics did not become different. What mattered did.”

She erased everything.

The display changed to an arena simulation. A training sphere struck a padded barrier. Colored arrows showed the impact moving into the barrier frame and down through the floor.

“Most Resonant instruction begins after someone else has drawn the box,” Sen said. “You are told which objects count. Then you learn to move momentum, pressure, charge, heat, or structural load.”

“Different ways of saying something has to go somewhere.”

“Yes.”

“That version would improve several textbooks.”

“It would shorten them. Textbook authors dislike that.”

The simulation replayed.

This time a Redirection user diverted the sphere sideways.

The vector map brightened at contact. Energy entered the barrier, the frame, and the concrete under it, then spread outward in branches too small for the display to label.

Sen paused the footage.

“Did the operator redirect the sphere?”

“Yes.”

“Did they redirect the barrier?”

“No.”

“Then why did its bolts flex?”

“Because the sphere still hit it.”

“So the operator changed the route. They did not erase the consequences.”

“That sounds less impressive when you say it that way.”

“It should sound more dangerous.”

She advanced the footage one frame at a time. The side rail flexed. A floor seam compressed. The table holding the recording equipment moved less than a millimeter.

“The academy model stops here,” Sen said, drawing a box around the instrumented arena. “Not because the world stops here. Because the sensors stop paying attention here. Beyond this line, the contribution is too small to matter to this exercise and too expensive to measure.”

“So the system is whatever the sensors can afford.”

“Sometimes.”

She changed the image. The same sphere struck the same barrier, but now two students and a referee occupied the arena. One student stood close enough that the diverted sphere passed across the front of her uniform.

“Same equipment. Same transfer. Same physical walls. Same system?”

Kade watched the student recoil.

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because now someone can get hurt.”

“The equations have not changed.”

“The reason for solving them did.”

Sen let the answer remain between them.

Then she drew a new box. It did not follow the arena walls. It enclosed the sphere, the barrier, and the student in its path.

“Every model contains a judgment about what matters,” she said. “Most instruction hides that judgment by giving you the boundary in advance.”

Kade looked at the crooked white shape.

“And I don’t keep the boundary I’m given.”

“You may not.”

“You make that sound like bad manners.”

“In research, bad manners are survivable. Bad bookkeeping is not.”

She pointed to the sphere, then the barrier, then the building beneath it.

“Most operators never need to ask whether the sphere belongs to the barrier, the barrier to the building, or the building to the island. Those relationships stay stable enough to treat as facts.”

Kade thought of the examination.

The selection box that had stopped snapping to objects.

**REDEFINE.**

“And me?”

Sen folded her hands behind her back.

“You appear to challenge the fact.”

“Appear?”

“You have done something consistent with that interpretation twice at meaningful scale.”

“Three times.”

Sen turned.

Kade immediately regretted how pleased he sounded.

“Explain.”

He told her about Eli’s tactical model.

The smaller anomalies.

A block that pushed an opponent farther than expected. An impact that seemed to pass through Kade with less effect than the measured acceleration predicted. A stumble whose path did not quite match the force that caused it.

“Nothing large,” Kade said. “Nothing I chose.”

“Did Navarro design tests around the pattern?”

“No.”

“Did you ask him not to?”

“Yes.”

Sen’s posture changed by a fraction.

Approval, maybe.

“Good.”

“He has observations.”

“I want a copy.”

Kade nodded. “I’ll send it.”

“No.”

He looked at her.

“You will ask him whether he wants to share it.”

“He will.”

“Then ask anyway.”

Kade noticed the distinction.

Consent.

He thought about Eli in the dorm, waiting for Kade to decide whether to keep the model.

“Why does that matter to you so much?”

Sen looked at the arena simulation.

“Because power makes shortcuts around consent very easy to justify.”

The answer did not sound like something she had invented for him.

Kade filed that away too.

Sen brought up the cracked-floor footage.

The image froze at Pike’s impact.

“Here is what we know,” she said. “Plain version first.”

Kade leaned forward.

“Pike hit you. His strike supplied almost all the energy. Your body and stance added a smaller amount. The building added nothing unexplained.”

“So I did not create extra force.”

“Correct. Nothing appeared from nowhere.”

“Good start.”

“The bad part is where the force went.”

She magnified the support lattice.

The strike should have loaded the floor directly beneath Kade. Instead, the stress had traveled sideways into structural members designed for a different job.

“The arena felt the impact through supports that should not have been carrying it,” Sen said. “Our normal field telemetry never saw the transfer happen.”

“Because my output was zero.”

“Your *conventional* output was below measurable threshold.”

Kade looked at her.

“That sounded like a new adjective.”

“It is an important one.”

She traced the unexpected path with one finger.

“Pike supplied the hit. The floor paid for it. Something changed which route counted.”

Kade stared at the fracture line.

“You changed where it counted,” he said.

Sen’s eyes moved to him.

“Possibly.”

“Possibly is doing a lot of work.”

“That is science.”

“I thought science had numbers.”

“Science also has the sentence ‘we do not know yet.’ You should become comfortable with it.”

Kade looked at the wall.

“What did it cost?”

Sen did not answer immediately.

His hands remembered the tremor.

“The headache,” he said. “The pupil thing. Losing the day after Shah.”

“We cannot yet prove the Boundary event caused every symptom, but the pattern is strong.”

“So every time I do this, my brain pays for it.”

“We do not know what job your brain is doing.”

“That sounds concerning.”

“It is.”

Sen counted on her fingers.

“It may be the engine producing the effect. The steering wheel directing it. The dashboard detecting it. Or the fuse that fails when the rest of the system overloads.”

Kade looked at her hand.

“Those are four bad options.”

“They are also four different medical problems. We cannot treat them as one because the symptoms happen in the same head.”

That was much easier to understand and not remotely reassuring.

Kade appreciated it more than reassurance.

Sen opened Park’s recovery chart beside the fracture map.

The first graph showed his pupil response. The second tracked motor tremor. A third plotted reaction-time tests he barely remembered taking after the match.

His line fell hard after the first hour, improved, then dipped again the next morning.

“Why did it get worse twice?”

“We do not know.”

“You really are becoming comfortable with that sentence.”

“You should try it.”

Kade studied the second dip. Park had asked him to sort colored shapes while a tone sounded at irregular intervals. He had complained that the test was designed for children.

He had missed four tones.

“I thought I was fine by then.”

“That is why feeling recovered is not the same as being cleared.”

The irritation arrived first. Then the memory of standing in the medical bay, insisting he could return to class while Park watched him put the same shoe on the wrong foot twice.

“What is the rule?” he asked.

Sen closed the chart.

“Observation only. No deliberate Boundary attempt without Park present, a briefed safety team, and the structural load mapped before you begin.”

“Until when?”

“Until we can provoke the effect without unacceptable risk.”

“That could take weeks.”

“Yes.”

“What if it happens in a match?”

“You stop.”

“What if stopping means I lose?”

Sen’s expression stayed flat.

Kade rubbed his thumb against the edge of the chair.

“Right. Stupid question.”

“No. It is an important question with an easy answer while nobody is hitting you. We need the answer to remain easy when someone is.”

That was worse than a lecture because it sounded true.

Sen cleared the wall and placed three words on it.

**SOURCE. PATH. SINK.**

“Before the symbols,” she said, “these mean three ordinary things. Where the trouble starts. What carries it. Where it ends.”

“That sounds less mathematical.”

“It is the mathematics before the letters dress it up.”

A diagram appeared beneath the words: three connected circles with a movable line passing through them.

“Conventional training asks what can move from source to sink and how an operator changes the path.”

She moved the line so that it cut the middle circle in half.

The labels changed. Part of **PATH** became **SOURCE**.

“Boundary architecture asks who gets counted in each job before the transfer begins.”

Kade frowned.

“That is just relabeling.”

“Usually.”

“Usually?”

“A map can label a river as a road. Most of the time, reality ignores the mistake and your car gets wet. The danger is discovering a model can make the mistake operational.”

“That analogy became violent quickly.”

“Education should have consequences.”

Sen placed three metal disks on the desk. One copper, one black composite, one steel.

She touched the copper disk.

“This is a power cell.”

The black disk.

“A damaged conduit.”

The steel.

“A containment shell. The cell discharges through the conduit and heats the shell. Where does the trouble start?”

“The cell.”

“What carries it?”

“The conduit.”

“Where does it end?”

“The shell.”

“Source. Path. Sink.”

“Too easy.”

Sen moved the steel disk away.

“The shell is part of a medical transport holding a patient.”

Kade included the empty space beyond the desk.

“Patient too.”

“The patient has an implanted field regulator drawing from the same cell.”

Kade looked at the disks again.

The answer he had been about to give stopped working.

If the regulator belonged to the failing circuit, isolating it could protect the shell and kill the patient. If it belonged to the patient, the discharge path was not merely waste heat. Same parts. Different obligation.

“You can’t choose the sink until you know what every connection does.”

“Good.”

“And if you don’t know?”

“You do not pretend the cleanest diagram is the true one.”

Kade turned the copper disk between two fingers.

“Is that what happened with the floor?”

“We know Pike’s strike, your body, and the support lattice participated. We do not know what definition made that arrangement available to you.”

“Available.”

“Not obedient.”

Sen took the disk from his hand and returned it to its place.

“Do not confuse perceiving a boundary with owning what is inside it.”

The sentence landed harder than the metal.

“You already had it.”

Sen became still.

Mara had been right.

Kade sat forward.

“You said it yesterday like you knew what it meant before the floor broke.”

Sen did not answer.

“Has someone done this before?”

A pause.

“No.”

Kade watched her.

The answer was clean.

Too clean.

“Never?”

“Not in any verified modern training program.”

That was not the same answer.

He heard the missing space immediately.

“Modern.”

Sen’s expression did not change.

“Training program.”

No reaction.

Kade leaned back.

“You’re good at this.”

“At what?”

“Answering the exact question you wish I had asked.”

Something almost like amusement crossed Sen’s face.

“Your teachers found that frustrating too.”

“You read more than my admissions file.”

“I read what I needed.”

“Is this connected to Zero Protocol?”

Sen reached for the wall controls.

The simulation vanished.

“This session is over.”

Kade laughed once.

“That means yes.”

“It means this session is over.”

“Director—”

“Kade.”

Her voice sharpened for the first time.

“Curiosity is useful. So is survival.”

He stared at her.

Sen took a slow breath.

When she spoke again, the edge was gone.

“We are going to teach you carefully. We are going to learn what you can do without assuming novelty makes you invulnerable or significance makes you immortal.”

“I never assumed either.”

“Good.”

Her gaze dropped briefly to his hands.

“Because whatever happened to that floor happened through you.”

The headache became more memorable.

Kade stood.

At the door he paused.

One question had been sitting behind every other question since Northline.

He did not know why it surfaced now.

Maybe because Sen was lying.

Maybe because his mother had almost said something at dinner before he left.

“Did you know my father?”

For the first time, Sen lost control of her face.

Almost.

A small stillness around the eyes.

A breath that did not finish normally.

Then the director returned.

“No,” she said.

Kade looked at her.

Aaron Mercer had been dead most of Kade’s life.

There should have been no reason for the question to matter.

Sen had made it matter.

“Okay,” Kade said.

He left.

---

The first person Kade saw outside was Mara.

That was becoming statistically suspicious.

She sat on the stone ledge across the courtyard with a book open on her lap. A physical book. The kind Asterion kept in climate-controlled rooms like historical artifacts.

Kade stopped.

Mara looked up.

“Well?”

“She lied.”

Mara closed the book.

“About what?”

“At least two things.”

Kade sat beside her.

Students crossed the courtyard around them. Somewhere above, a maintenance drone buzzed along the old building’s facade.

He gave her the ordinary version first.

“Pike supplied the hit. I did not add unexplained power. The force reached the wrong supports. Sen thinks I may be changing which parts count as one system.”

“Boundary architecture.”

“Yes.”

Then he gave her the evidence beneath it.

Conventional output near zero.

Unexpected load path.

Neurological cost.

Mara listened without interrupting.

When he reached Sen’s answer about prior examples, she said, “Verified modern training program.”

“Exactly.”

“That is lawyer language.”

“Director language.”

“Same family.”

Kade looked at her.

Mara almost smiled.

Then he said, “I asked about my father.”

The almost-smile disappeared.

“What did she say?”

“No.”

“And?”

“She lied.”

Mara studied him.

“How sure?”

Kade thought of Sen’s half-finished breath.

“Completely.”

Mara looked toward the administration building.

“That means your father mattered to whatever she is protecting.”

“Or she met him once.”

“You don’t believe that.”

“No.”

Neither did she.

Mara opened the book to the index.

“I looked for the term after you told me what Sen said last night.”

Her finger moved down the page.

Boundary feedback. Boundary-layer interference. Boundary-state estimation.

No architecture.

“Nothing?” Kade asked.

“Not in this edition. Not in the academy catalog either.”

“You searched the whole catalog between last night and now.”

“I searched one phrase.”

“At length.”

Mara ignored that.

“Absence proves very little. A provisional term may never have been published. Classified research would not appear in the public catalog. Sen may have developed it privately.”

“Or she learned it from my father.”

“That is possible. It is not what we know.”

Kade’s jaw tightened.

Mara turned the book so he could see a marked passage. It described recursive coupling as a problem of observation. Where an analyst drew the system edge determined which feedback looked like cause and which looked like response.

“What do we know?” she asked.

He recognized the tactic. She was making him separate evidence from the story anger wanted to build around it.

He disliked it because it worked.

“Sen knew the term before she explained it. She restricted her denial to verified modern training programs. She ended the session when I connected it to Zero Protocol. She reacted when I asked about Aaron.”

“Good.”

“None of that tells us how she knew him.”

“Correct.”

“You enjoy this too much.”

“Precision?”

“Being right near me.”

“That is not rare enough to remain enjoyable.”

Kade stared at her.

Mara’s attention returned to the page, but the corner of her mouth betrayed her.

For a minute they sat without speaking.

Kade expected anger to fill the space.

Instead he felt something worse.

Uncertainty.

He had always had a simple story about Aaron Mercer.

Dead young.

Industrial accident.

Sad enough to explain the photographs. Final enough to avoid questions.

Now Sen had made even that unstable.

Mara said, “Do not confront her again today.”

Kade looked at her.

“That sounded like an instruction.”

“It was.”

“Why?”

“Because you are angry.”

“That has never stopped me before.”

“I know.”

He hated how much that sounded like Sera.

Mara reopened the book.

Kade looked at the cover.

**Field Stability Under Recursive Coupling**

“Light reading?”

“Apparently.”

“You carry textbooks to stakeouts?”

“This is not a stakeout.”

“You were waiting outside Sen’s office.”

“I had reason to believe you would leave through this courtyard.”

“That is the definition of waiting.”

Mara went back to reading.

Kade smiled despite himself.

---

He asked Eli about the model that evening.

Not in passing.

Actually asked.

They sat at their desks with the tactical file open between them.

“Sen wants the observations,” Kade said. “Not your notes unless you want to include them.”

Eli stopped spinning a stylus between his fingers.

“She said that?”

“She said I have to ask you.”

Eli looked surprised.

Then thoughtful.

“What does she want to do with it?”

“I don’t know.”

“Who gets access?”

“I don’t know.”

Eli frowned.

Kade waited.

It was harder than he expected not to fill the silence with reasons Eli should say yes.

The data might help.

Sen already knew most of it.

They needed answers.

All true.

Not his decision.

Finally Eli said, “Observations only. No private notes. No raw dorm sensor logs. And she tells me if anything I collected violates a restriction so I can delete it.”

Kade nodded.

“I’ll tell her.”

“That’s it?”

“What?”

“You’re not going to argue for more?”

“No.”

Eli looked almost disappointed.

“Growth is upsetting.”

“Everyone says that.”

They prepared a clean copy.

It took forty minutes because Eli had written several comments about faculty models that could not safely be shared with faculty.

It also took forty minutes because “observations only” required a definition.

The tactical model contained time stamps from their matches, which Eli approved. It contained dorm-room motion readings from the night Kade’s hands shook, which he did not. It contained three guesses about Kade’s sleep schedule, one note about Sera’s habit of checking the door before she sat down, and a chart correlating Taren’s lateness with dining-hall dessert quality.

“Why is this in my file?” Kade asked.

“The model needs environmental variables.”

“Pudding is not an environment.”

“Taren disagrees.”

They stripped every observation that belonged to someone who had not been asked. What remained was smaller and better: match time, measured impact, predicted path, observed result, confidence.

Eli added a final column labeled **ALTERNATE EXPLANATIONS**.

“That column makes everything look less certain,” Kade said.

“Correct.”

“You sound like Sen.”

Eli deleted an entire row with unnecessary force.

One note beside a transfer anomaly read:

**THIS MAKES NO SENSE UNLESS THE FLOOR IS LYING.**

Kade pointed at it.

“Delete.”

“That is analysis.”

“That is slander against architecture.”

Eli sighed and removed it.

When they finished, Kade sent the file to Sen with Eli’s conditions attached.

Sen replied six minutes later.

**Accepted. Thank him. Conditions honored.**

Eli read the message.

“Huh.”

“What?”

“She could have taken the data through academy systems.”

Kade looked at him.

“Could she?”

“Technically.”

The word hung between them.

Technically was one of Kade’s favorite hiding places.

Sen had chosen not to use it.

That did not make her honest.

It made her complicated.

Kade was beginning to find complicated adults more difficult than cruel ones.

Cruel people were easy to reject.

---

The next morning, Park scheduled Kade for what she called a “non-provocative boundary observation session.”

Kade stared at the title.

“That sounds provocative.”

“It means you are not allowed to do anything.”

“Ah. Science.”

Park placed him behind a reinforced window overlooking three ordinary Resonance demonstrations.

A Redirection student changed the path of a rolling mass.

A Storage student absorbed a controlled impact and released it into a damping block.

Two Coupling students stabilized a suspended platform while technicians deliberately changed the load.

Kade wore no active Conduit.

No field authorization.

He simply watched.

Before the first trial, Park placed a hand control on the ledge in front of him. It had one red button and no label.

“That is comforting,” Kade said.

“Press it if you develop a headache, visual narrowing, tremor, missing time, sudden nausea, or the sense that you can influence the apparatus.”

“What does it do?”

“Stops every demonstration and alerts the safety team.”

“That seems dramatic.”

“So was the floor.”

Park recorded his baseline before allowing the technicians to begin. Follow the light with his eyes. Tap each finger to his thumb. Repeat six numbers backward. Describe the taste of the electrolyte drink without insulting it.

“Artificial orange,” Kade said.

“That was not an insult.”

“It was accurate enough to become one.”

She marked the response.

“Are jokes a neurological measure?”

“Yours are becoming one.”

Park set a five-minute timer beside the red control.

“At each interval, report symptoms before observations.”

“What if there are none?”

“You say none.”

“Efficient.”

“This is not an endurance test, Mercer. Success is not staying in the room longest. Success is leaving with an accurate record and no injury.”

The distinction bothered him.

He was used to tests in which continuing earned something.

Rank. Approval. Proof.

Here, pressing the button first might be the most correct thing he could do.

Kade rested two fingers beside it.

“Ready.”

“Describe what you perceive,” Park said.

“First one pushes momentum into the rail.”

“You cannot see the field.”

“I can see the rail move.”

“Continue.”

“Second one stores less than the display says.”

Park looked at the telemetry.

“Why?”

“The damping block is heating early.”

The technician checked.

Kade was right.

On the third demonstration, the paired operators fought a slow oscillation in the platform.

Kade leaned forward.

“What?” Park asked.

“They’re correcting each other.”

“That is the point.”

“No. They’re correcting the correction.”

Park watched the platform.

Kade saw the same idea from Mara’s assignment.

Every intervention changed what the next intervention meant.

The two operators were separate people.

The system they created was not simply the sum of them.

The cold line did not appear.

No headache.

No tremor.

Just understanding.

Kade exhaled.

Park noticed.

“Relieved?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“Medical enthusiasm?”

“You are allowed to learn without injuring yourself.”

Kade looked through the glass.

That had not always been obvious to him.

Park spoke to the technicians through the room channel.

“Repeat the coupled-platform sequence. Reverse primary and support roles after ten seconds.”

The students below exchanged places. One took responsibility for vertical load. The other managed rotation. Their Conduits shifted from blue to white as the trial reset.

“What am I looking for?” Kade asked.

“You tell me.”

The platform rose.

For ten seconds it remained level. At the signal, the students traded roles.

The platform tipped left.

Both corrected.

It tipped right, faster.

“They switched the labels,” Kade said.

“They switched tasks.”

“No. The first student still expects her correction to be primary. The second is waiting to support it. They are both behaving as though the first arrangement still exists.”

The left edge dropped again.

“What happens next?” Park asked.

Kade followed the sequence. The first student pushed up. The second compensated late. That delay would make the first reduce too early.

“Front right rises. Then they overcorrect backward.”

The corner lifted.

The platform rolled back.

One technician reached for emergency control, but the students separated their inputs and brought the platform level.

Park marked something on her tablet.

“Again,” she said over the channel. “This time, announce ownership before each correction.”

The sequence reset.

“Upward load,” the first student called.

“Rotation,” the second answered.

The platform shifted. Each named the next change before making it. The motion looked slower than the first attempt and settled in half the time.

Kade waited for the cold line.

Nothing.

He could understand why the system changed without reaching for it.

Park lowered her tablet.

“What was different?”

“They made the boundary between their jobs explicit.”

“And?”

“They kept changing it on purpose instead of pretending the first assignment stayed true.”

Park nodded once.

Approval from her was small enough to require medical imaging.

“Is that Boundary architecture?” he asked.

“It is two Coupling students communicating properly.”

“That was almost an answer.”

“Do not become greedy.”

The session ended with no anomaly.

Park called that a successful result.

Kade was starting to understand why.

---

That evening Elena called while he was walking back to Meridian.

He almost did not tell her about Sen.

Then remembered how that story ended when adults made the same choice for him.

So he did.

Not classified details.

The part that belonged to him.

Sen knew the term.

Sen had lied about Aaron.

Elena stopped moving on the screen.

“Kade.”

“What?”

“Do not go looking for old records alone.”

He slowed on the bridge.

“That was specific.”

“I am serious.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know enough to give you the answer you want.”

“That is becoming a family tradition.”

Her face tightened.

Kade regretted it immediately.

“Sorry.”

Elena took a breath.

“Your father worked around people connected to early Resonance research. That is all I know for certain.”

Kade stopped walking.

“Early Resonance research.”

“Yes.”

“You told me industrial systems.”

“He did both.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

“Because after he died, I was told parts of his work were classified. I did not know what mattered and what did not.”

“Who told you?”

Elena looked away.

“I do not remember everyone involved.”

Not a lie.

Not a full answer either.

Kade had become very good at hearing the difference.

“Did Sen?” he asked.

Elena’s gaze returned to him.

“I don’t know.”

Kade believed her.

That was somehow worse.

Traffic moved behind her through the hospital corridor. A cart rattled past. Someone called for a consult, and Elena raised one finger toward the sound without looking away from Kade.

Wait.

Even now, the gesture worked on both of them.

“What did he actually do?” Kade asked when the corridor quieted. “Not the classified version. What did you think he did?”

Elena leaned against the wall.

“He was hired through an industrial-controls contractor. Ports, power routing, automated safety systems. That was what the pay records said, and some of it was real. He would come home complaining about load-balancing software and spend half of dinner drawing diagrams on whatever paper was near him.”

“What kind of diagrams?”

“Circuit layouts, mostly. Lists of failures. Once, a very detailed argument with the refrigerator.”

“Who won?”

“The refrigerator. He took it apart at two in the morning, put it back together at five, and we kept the milk in a cooler for three days.”

Kade tried to place the story beside the photographs.

None of them contained a broken refrigerator.

“Was he bad at fixing things?”

“He was bad at accepting that some things should be replaced.”

The answer carried enough weight that Kade almost pulled at it.

Elena caught the change in his face.

“Do not turn every ordinary memory into evidence.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You did not need to.”

The rebuke stopped him because it was not a defense of Sen.

It was a defense of Aaron’s right to have been a person before he became a clue.

Elena rubbed at the faint mark left by her mask.

“He was patient with machines,” she said. “Less patient with people. He believed reading the instructions after something broke counted as planning.”

Kade looked over the bridge rail. Far below, evening water moved black between the academy lights.

“That sounds like me.”

“It sounds like him at twenty-six. He also left wet towels on the floor and believed every shelf could hold one more book. Do not turn resemblance into destiny because he is dead.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were considering it.”

He had been.

“Did he ever mention Zero Protocol?”

“No.”

“Boundary architecture?”

“No.”

“Any person I could ask?”

“Not one I trust. The contractor changed names, supervisors changed, and after the accident everyone who called already knew what they were allowed to tell me.”

“Did you meet them?”

“A few people at holiday parties. First names, maybe. We had a baby. I worked nights. He traveled. Most conversations were about whether there was milk and whose turn it was to sleep. I am sorry I cannot turn seventeen-year-old small talk into the answer you need.”

Kade had never pictured Aaron tired at a kitchen table.

The photographs made him permanent: smiling, young, already gone.

This version left towels on the floor.

“If you find something,” Elena said, “you tell me before you confront anyone.”

“Before?”

“Before.”

“That sounds like permission to look.”

“It is recognition that ordering you not to look would waste both our time.”

“You do know me.”

“Unfortunately.”

The line should have been easy. Her voice made it tired instead.

Kade rested both arms on the rail.

“I’ll tell you.”

“And you will not search alone.”

He thought of Mara with the book already marked. Eli asking who got access. Sera, who would identify every unsafe assumption before he reached the first locked door.

“I won’t be alone.”

“That is not exactly what I said.”

At Meridian, the argument about that morning’s Bell Watch had begun again over dinner.

Eli’s joke disappeared.

He took the bread.

The fish was dry. Eli had been correct about that too. Kade ate it anyway while Sera timed his recovery stretches and Taren appealed the Bell Watch ruling to an upper-year who refused jurisdiction. Mara reopened the book only after Kade finished half his plate.

Ordinary life rushed in around the mystery.

Kade had once believed serious things required being alone with them.

Asterion kept proving otherwise.

He sat beside Eli.

The questions remained.

So did dinner.

For now, both could exist.

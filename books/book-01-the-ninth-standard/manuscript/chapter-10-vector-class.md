# Chapter 10 — Vector Class

Kade had been at Asterion long enough to learn that there were at least four different ways to feel stupid.

There was ordinary stupid.

There was tired stupid.

There was being corrected by Mara Vey stupid.

And there was Vector Analysis at eight in the morning.

Professor Ife Amadi wrote three coupled equations across the wall display and turned to the class.

“Resolve the transfer manifold.”

Kade understood every word individually.

Together, they were apparently an attack.

Around him, students began writing.

Styluses moved over tablets. Equations populated shared displays. Nobody screamed.

Mara’s hand started almost immediately.

Eli leaned toward Kade.

“Do you know what a manifold is?”

“Yes.”

“Do you know what this one is?”

“No.”

“Excellent.”

Kade stared at the equations.

The physical problem made sense.

Three bodies interacting through a constrained field. One allowed transfer path. Two correction loops. Loss terms that increased as the coupled system approached instability.

He could see the motion in his head.

Not numbers.

Motion.

The first mass shifted. The second compensated. The third inherited a force everyone else’s notation seemed determined to call secondary even though Kade could see it becoming the whole problem.

What he could not do was translate that picture into the language everybody around him appeared to speak fluently.

That part was new.

At Northline, being ahead of a lesson had been boring.

At Asterion, being behind one was almost exhilarating.

He hated that too.

Professor Amadi looked toward him.

“Mercer.”

Of course.

Kade sat straighter.

“Where does the instability begin?”

He studied the diagram.

“The upper-left coupling.”

A few students stopped writing.

Amadi’s expression remained neutral.

“Why?”

“Because the model treats the second body like it stays isolated after the first exchange.”

One of her eyebrows rose.

“Show me.”

Kade walked to the board.

He took the stylus.

Then stopped.

He knew what was wrong.

He had absolutely no idea how to write it.

There were few experiences more humiliating than understanding a thing and being unable to prove you understood it.

A quiet voice from the third row said, “Use lambda three.”

Mara.

Kade glanced back.

She pointed with two fingers, barely moving.

He wrote the symbol.

Then the next term.

Wrong index.

Mara made a tiny rotating gesture.

Kade corrected it.

Then another term.

The structure clicked into place.

He stepped back.

Professor Amadi looked from the board to Kade.

“Correct.”

Kade returned to his seat.

Eli leaned toward him.

“You just solved a problem you cannot write.”

“I prefer conceptual elegance.”

“You prefer avoiding homework.”

Also true.

Amadi continued class.

Kade spent the next forty minutes discovering that his gaps were worse than he thought.

He understood vectors intuitively and occasionally abused notation in ways that made classmates physically uncomfortable. He had never seen two of the tensor conventions Asterion treated as prerequisites. His algebra was fast, his proofs were inconsistent, and his habit of skipping steps worked poorly in a room full of instructors who wanted to know whether the skipped step was genius or a mistake.

By the end of class, he had four pages of corrections.

Mara had one.

Kade found that offensive on principle.

The final exercise involved a six-user stabilization grid around a collapsing platform.

Students worked in pairs.

Amadi assigned Kade to a boy named Owen Park, rank 2,804, who had the formal vocabulary Kade lacked and no patience for explanations that could not be expressed in symbols.

Owen wrote three lines.

Kade stared at the simulated platform.

“You’re going to overload the southwest anchor.”

Owen did not look up.

“The field solves symmetrically.”

“The bodies don’t.”

“The exercise assumes equal operator response.”

“Then the exercise is lying.”

Owen finally looked at him.

“You can’t just change the assumptions.”

“I’m not changing them. I’m noticing the operators are standing in different places.”

Owen sighed.

Kade leaned over and shifted one response node.

The simulation stabilized two seconds longer.

Then failed somewhere else.

Owen frowned.

Kade moved another.

Failure time doubled.

Owen’s expression became less dismissive.

“Why?”

“Because your equations are pretending the geometry doesn’t matter after the field activates.”

Owen looked at the model again.

Then at Kade.

“Write that.”

Kade grimaced.

“That is where our partnership becomes difficult.”

For ten minutes, Owen translated Kade’s intuition into notation while Kade kept finding assumptions that produced physically stupid outcomes.

They finished third in the class.

Owen stared at the result.

“You’re infuriating.”

“I’ve received that feedback.”

“Do you actually know the notation?”

“No.”

“That makes it worse.”

Kade smiled.

For once, being behind did not feel like being less intelligent.

It felt like having a language problem.

Language problems could be fixed.

Professor Amadi stopped him before he reached the door.

“Mercer.”

He turned.

“Your prior transcript indicates you completed introductory calculus independently.”

“Technically.”

“What does technically mean?”

“I passed the assessment.”

“That was not my question.”

Kade looked at her.

Amadi waited.

“I learned enough to pass the assessment.”

“And not enough to build the language around the ideas.”

“That seems judgmental.”

“It is diagnostic.”

Asterion loved that word.

Amadi handed him a list of supplemental modules.

Kade looked at the length.

“Is this punishment?”

“This is the cost of skipping foundations.”

He wanted to argue.

Park’s voice came back instead.

*Fix the boring things.*

Kade took the list.

“Fine.”

Amadi smiled faintly.

“You say that as if I need your permission.”

Kade left before he accidentally liked another adult.

Mara waited near the corridor junction.

Kade stopped.

“Thank you.”

“You’re welcome.”

He narrowed his eyes.

“That was disturbingly easy.”

“You need tutoring.”

There it was.

“I was happier two seconds ago.”

“You understand systems and guess notation.”

“I do not guess.”

“You wrote a tensor index backward.”

“Stylistic choice.”

Mara started walking.

“Library. Nineteen hundred.”

Kade followed.

“You’re assigning me tutoring?”

“No. I am preventing you from embarrassing Meridian in team analysis.”

“That is much warmer.”

“You can ask Eli instead.”

Kade considered it.

“He would build a simulator.”

“Yes.”

“And somehow damage the library.”

“Yes.”

“Nineteen hundred.”

At lunch, Eli reacted to the arrangement exactly as badly as Kade expected.

“So you and Mara are studying alone.”

“Yes.”

“In the library.”

“Yes.”

“At night.”

“It is nineteen hundred.”

“Romantically academic.”

Kade pointed his fork at him.

“Do you want me to tell Sera about your illegal drone calibration yesterday?”

Eli’s face became neutral.

“I support education.”

Sera, sitting across from them, said, “Tell me what?”

Eli stood.

“I have class.”

“You are holding soup.”

“Portable.”

He left carrying the bowl.

Sera watched him go.

Then looked at Kade.

“Library?”

Kade closed his eyes.

“I regret everyone.”

“Good.”

The rest of the school day made the tutoring feel less like a social event and more like survival.

In Applied Mechanics, Kade lost points for failing to show a derivation he had done mentally.

In Conduit Theory, he misunderstood an abbreviation everyone else seemed to know from feeder programs.

At conditioning, Mara corrected his stance without stopping long enough for Eli to make a face.

At four, Elena called during her break.

Kade showed her the supplemental module list.

She stared.

“You have homework.”

“I have always had homework.”

“You have homework you intend to do.”

“That sounds accusatory.”

Elena smiled.

“Your father used to hate notation too.”

The sentence came out naturally.

Then both of them went still.

Kade looked at her.

“He did?”

Elena’s expression tightened a fraction.

“In engineering school. He said if the machine worked, the symbols were showing off.”

Kade smiled despite himself.

“That is objectively correct.”

“No.”

“You are taking the side of notation?”

“I am taking the side of bridges staying up.”

Kade laughed.

The moment passed.

But he stored it.

Another small piece of Aaron Mercer that did not fit neatly into the industrial-accident photograph in the hallway.

At seventeen hundred, Kade reported to Foundations Clinic in a room beneath the mathematics wing.

He had expected remedial education to hide somewhere.

Asterion put it behind a glass wall facing the busiest academic bridge and labeled the door **FOUNDATIONS ARE LOAD-BEARING**.

Kade stood outside long enough for three students to walk past him.

The fourth stopped.

Owen Park held a stack of practice tablets under one arm.

“You coming in?”

“I’m studying the sign.”

“It has three words.”

“Dense sign.”

Owen opened the door.

Kade followed because refusing in front of Owen would turn embarrassment into a measurable decision.

The room contained fourteen students, none of whom looked relieved to be there. Two wore first-year academy gray. The rest wore House colors from every year. A fourth-year from Sol sat at the front relearning fractional exponents beside a first-year who had apparently missed an entire unit on proofs. Nobody had been arranged by rank.

That alone made the room strange.

The clinic tutor, a compact third-year named Dev Batra, checked Kade’s module list.

“Vector notation, proof structure, tensor convention C.”

“When you say it together, it sounds worse.”

“It is worse. You skipped the parts that tell other people whether you’re right.”

“Professor Amadi has already developed this theme.”

Dev pointed toward a table.

“Diagnostic set. No field use, no projection hints, and every line shown.”

Kade sat beside Owen.

“Why are you here?” he asked.

Owen placed his tablets on the table.

“Proof structure.”

“You told me to write what I meant.”

“I know what a correct proof looks like. I produce ugly ones.”

Kade looked at him.

Owen looked back.

“Were you hoping this room contained only people less prepared than you?”

“No.”

He had been.

The diagnostic began with an ordinary load transfer across three supports. Kade reached the answer in his head, wrote four lines, and stopped.

Dev appeared beside him.

“You have omitted the initial condition.”

“It is in the diagram.”

“The diagram is not your proof.”

“It is very involved.”

Dev tapped the empty first line.

Kade wrote the condition.

On the next problem he omitted a unit.

On the third, he changed coordinate frames halfway through because the second one made the answer easier.

Dev drew a box around the change.

“Declare it.”

“Anyone reading can see what I did.”

“Anyone reading should not have to reconstruct your mood.”

Across the table, Owen made a choking sound.

Kade looked up.

“Are you enjoying this?”

“Immensely.”

The fourth problem was harder.

A rescue platform hung between two field anchors while a third operator moved an injured body across it. The official solution treated the passenger as a changing load and the operators as fixed control sources.

Kade drew the first two transfer arrows.

Then he stopped.

“The third operator moves differently once the passenger reaches center.”

Dev leaned over the table.

“Why?”

“Because the left anchor has to compensate.”

“Write it.”

“You people use that phrase as violence.”

Dev waited.

Kade wrote an interaction term.

Wrong symbol.

He crossed it out before anyone corrected him and tried again.

Owen leaned closer.

“You’re making the carrier part of the control loop.”

“They are part of it.”

“The problem defines three operators and one load.”

“The injured person grabs the carrier at midpoint.” Kade pointed to the small note beneath the diagram. “Now the load can change the carrier’s balance.”

Dev read the note.

“Most people treat that as flavor text.”

“Then most people drop the patient.”

The Sol fourth-year turned around from the front table.

“Response practical?”

Kade shook his head.

“Bad diagram.”

She came over anyway.

“I did this practical last year. He’s right about the grip. The training dummy locks one arm at the midpoint and everyone loses the left anchor the first time.”

Owen studied Kade’s interaction term.

“Your sign is wrong.”

Kade checked it.

It was.

He fixed it.

The fourth-year supplied the actual carrier position. Owen rebuilt the proof around a cleaner frame. Kade added the correction loop. Dev made all three of them label every assumption.

Their result used twice as many lines as the official solution.

It also kept the platform level.

Dev sent a copy to the Response faculty with a note attached.

Kade stared at the submission marker.

“Does remedial work usually become faculty complaints?”

“Foundation work,” Dev said. “And only when the foundation is wrong.”

Around them, students kept working openly on things they should supposedly have mastered before Asterion admitted them.

The shame had nowhere private to live.

After a while, that made it smaller.

When the clinic ended, Dev handed Kade a marked diagnostic.

“Tomorrow.”

Kade looked at the corrections.

“I have tutoring.”

“Then you’ll have two kinds of help.”

Outside, Owen headed toward House Sol.

Kade called after him.

“Your proofs are ugly.”

Owen kept walking.

“Your coordinate frames are crimes.”

It was not friendship.

But it was starting to become a language both of them spoke.

The library occupied six floors inside the base of the Spire and apparently considered books insufficiently dramatic.

Most information lived in searchable projections, field simulations, or interactive models. Physical books occupied one circular room behind glass because, according to Eli, “Asterion likes pretending paper is an endangered species.”

At nineteen hundred, Mara was already at a table with three textbooks open and a fourth projected above them.

Kade sat opposite her.

For the first hour, tutoring was painful.

Mara refused to let him skip steps.

Kade refused to accept notation without a reason.

They argued about coordinate conventions.

Then sign conventions.

Then whether a model that concealed physically meaningful assumptions deserved to be called elegant.

Mara looked almost offended by that one.

“Elegance is compression without losing truth.”

“Then half these textbooks are lying beautifully.”

“They are simplifying.”

“Same family.”

She stared at him.

Then, unexpectedly, smiled.

“Do that again.”

“What?”

“Explain the last problem without notation.”

Kade leaned back.

The problem showed two Resonant operators stabilizing a loaded beam through independent control loops.

“Two people carrying a table through a narrow door.”

Mara waited.

“One person thinks the table belongs to them. The other thinks it belongs to the room. Both are wrong. The important part is where the forces are allowed to go when the edge hits the frame.”

Mara’s expression changed.

“What?” Kade asked.

“Nothing.”

“That’s usually not true.”

She turned the tablet toward him.

“Write it.”

Kade did.

Badly.

Mara circled three things.

He did it again.

Less badly.

Again.

They worked until the library’s evening lights shifted warmer.

At 20:12, Kade’s attention failed completely.

He read the same line four times.

Mara noticed.

“Break.”

“I’m fine.”

“You just wrote lambda where you meant mass.”

“Creative notation.”

“Break.”

They went to the vending alcove.

Asterion’s machines contained fruit, protein drinks, coffee, six kinds of tea, and exactly one candy bar that cost enough to offend Kade personally.

Mara bought tea.

Kade bought the candy bar because resentment had limits.

They sat on the floor near the window.

Below them, the island’s walkways glowed blue-white.

Kade said, “Did you always know you were coming here?”

Mara looked at him.

“To Asterion?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

He waited.

She took a sip of tea.

“My family assumed.”

“That sounds like yes.”

“It is different.”

“How?”

“A choice can become invisible if everyone expects you to make it.”

Kade unwrapped the candy bar.

That sounded too precise to be casual.

“Did you want to?”

Mara looked through the glass.

“Yes.”

Then, after a moment, “I think.”

Kade nodded.

No joke.

No question about her father.

No push.

Mara glanced at him.

“You’re not going to say anything?”

“I’m learning boundaries.”

Her eyes narrowed.

“That was terrible.”

“Still true.”

She almost smiled.

They returned to the table.

Mara replaced the textbook with a blank display.

“The beam problem again,” she said. “No diagram this time.”

“That seems hostile to visual learners.”

“You’re going to make the diagram.”

She read the conditions aloud while Kade drew.

Two operators. One loaded beam. Unequal field strength. A delayed sensor on the east support. A limit on how quickly either operator could change output without losing coherence.

Kade drew the beam first.

Then the forces.

Then two separate control loops.

Mara watched without helping.

He added the sensor delay and wrote the first transfer equation.

His notation survived two full lines before he used the same symbol for output and correction.

Mara circled both.

“You knew what I meant.”

“I knew there were two possible meanings and guessed which one kept the beam in the air.”

“Correct guess.”

“That is not a defense.”

Kade looked at her.

Mara’s expression remained composed.

He changed the symbol.

On the fifth line, the east operator’s correction arrived late. The west operator corrected the correction. Kade started a third loop, stopped, and erased the boundary between the first two.

Mara leaned forward.

“Why did you do that?”

“Because neither loop owns the beam anymore.”

“They still control their own output.”

“Yes, but every choice starts from a state the other person helped create.”

Mara looked at the blank space where the dividing line had been.

“Separate control. Shared state.”

“That.”

“That is not notation.”

“I was hoping you wouldn’t notice.”

She turned the display toward herself.

Instead of restoring the dividing line, she wrote a bracket around both loops and marked each operator’s limit separately inside it.

Kade watched the symbols assemble.

“You can write that?”

“Provisionally.”

“Meaning?”

“Meaning Professor Amadi can tell me why it’s wrong tomorrow.”

Mara Vey had just written uncertain mathematics on purpose.

Kade sat back.

“I’m a terrible influence.”

“You’re an incomplete one.”

She passed the display back.

“Now derive the correction without changing coordinate frames halfway through.”

He tried.

The first attempt failed because the shared bracket concealed which operator reached limit first.

Mara did not fix it.

She asked, “What information did you lose?”

Kade restored the individual limits.

The second attempt failed because his delay term described the sensor but not the person reacting to it.

He added both.

The third attempt held.

Not elegantly.

Not quickly.

But every assumption remained visible, including the dangerous one he had wanted to hide inside the picture in his head.

Mara read the final proof.

“Acceptable.”

Kade looked at the crowded display.

“Your praise has collapsed under load.”

“Still standing.”

By the end of the second hour, the symbols felt less like a foreign language and more like an irritating dialect.

Kade stared at the final line.

“So notation is basically proof that I am not quietly changing the problem in my head.”

“Among other things.”

“That is disappointingly reasonable.”

“It happens.”

Mara closed the textbook.

“You’re improving.”

“Careful.”

“At notation.”

“Recovered.”

They packed up.

Kade noticed a schematic still open on Mara’s tablet.

A training harness. Two Resonant users connected through a stabilization frame.

“Your assignment?”

Mara looked down.

“Yes.”

“The coupling model is wrong.”

She stopped walking.

“It came from the faculty problem set.”

“Still wrong.”

Mara folded her arms.

“Why?”

Kade pointed at the paired control loops.

“It assumes each user corrects independently.”

“They do.”

“Not after the first correction.”

“That delay is modeled.”

“Timing is. Ownership isn’t.”

Mara frowned.

Kade enlarged the schematic.

“If you compensate for me, my next correction starts from the state you created. Then your next correction starts from mine. After enough cycles, saying there are two independent controllers is mathematically convenient and physically dishonest.”

Mara’s eyes moved over the model.

“What would you change?”

Kade drew a line linking the loops.

“Stop treating them as two operators.”

“For part of the calculation?”

“Whenever they are actually coupled.”

“That creates a shared state.”

“Yes.”

“Which reduces individual control.”

“Maybe.”

“It could amplify error.”

“Also maybe.”

“Dangerously.”

Kade looked at her.

“You say impossible too quickly.”

“You say maybe too comfortably.”

Neither spoke.

Then Mara saved the note.

Kade smiled.

“You wrote it down.”

“I record anomalies.”

“Still almost flattering.”

She started walking.

Kade fell into step beside her.

“Why do you care so much about the model?”

“It’s an assignment.”

“That is not the answer.”

Mara looked ahead.

For several steps he thought she would ignore him.

Then she said, “My parents both work in coupled-field systems.”

Kade remembered the limited things public profiles said about the Vey family. Famous Resonants. Research. Government contracts. Enough prestige to make Mara’s rank seven seem almost expected.

“That sounds convenient.”

“It is not.”

The tone closed the subject.

Kade let it.

That was also new.

House Meridian treated homework as a contact sport.

Kade discovered this at twenty-two hundred when he carried the two-page note into the common room and found every table occupied.

First-years filled the long central table. Two second-years argued over orbital mechanics beside the window. Nessa had taken the floor with a field-perception exercise spread around her in colored tiles. Somebody near the kitchen whispered vocabulary into a recorder while making noodles.

Nobody was quiet.

Everyone was working.

Sera sat on a couch reading an emergency-procedure manual with one boot braced against the coffee table. Eli had dismantled a pocket projector across three cushions.

Kade held up his note.

“I need a surface.”

Eli swept components into a pile.

“Academic emergency.”

“Mara says ideas need proper notation.”

The room nearest them became quieter.

Taren looked over from the central table.

“Mercer has been assigned scholarship.”

“I have always been scholarly.”

“You corrected my spelling on a challenge notice.”

“Public service.”

Kade sat.

The title of his note read: **DEPENDENT CORRECTION IN COUPLED OPERATOR SYSTEMS**.

The page beneath it contained three false starts and one paragraph that became less comprehensible each time he read it.

Eli leaned over.

“What are you trying to say?”

“That two operators stop being independent when their corrections change what the other one has to correct.”

“That sentence was free. Why are the next two pages hostile?”

“Notation.”

Eli picked up the pocket projector.

“Simulator.”

“Mara predicted this.”

“That is because Mara respects engineering.”

“She predicted property damage.”

“Related discipline.”

He projected two white circles above the table and linked each to a blue square representing a control target. When one circle moved, its square followed.

“Independent,” Eli said.

He linked both circles to one square.

The square jittered between their commands.

“Coupled and terrible.”

Kade adjusted the response delay.

The jitter slowed, then amplified.

“Because both corrections arrive after the other one has already changed the state.”

“So give one priority.”

Eli increased the left operator’s authority.

The square stabilized under the left circle.

Kade frowned.

“That solves it by making the second operator decorative.”

“Efficiently decorative.”

Sera lowered her manual.

“What is the actual task?”

“Keeping the target stable,” Eli said.

“For what?”

Eli looked at Kade.

Kade looked at the simulation.

“The model does not say.”

Sera returned to her manual.

“Then it is not a task.”

Kade stared at her.

“You read one emergency handbook and become philosophical?”

“Page forty-three. A response plan without a success condition is movement with uniforms.”

Eli changed the blue square into a stretcher.

“Fine. Keep the patient level while moving through a doorway.”

Sera got up.

“Now neither carrier can be decorative.”

She moved the right operator. Eli controlled the left. The projected stretcher reached the doorway and struck the frame.

“Your side was high,” Eli said.

“Your side entered first.”

They tried again.

This time Eli slowed at the door. Sera turned. The stretcher cleared, but the patient indicator slid toward the foot.

Kade watched their hands.

Each was controlling a separate end.

Neither could succeed alone.

He moved the operator limits inside one bracket, preserving two inputs and one success condition.

“Again.”

Eli looked at the revised model.

“Who controls the shared state?”

“Both of you.”

“That is not how control works.”

“Maybe that is the problem.”

Sera glanced from Kade to the simulation.

“Do we still choose our own movement?”

“Yes.”

“Then we’re not one operator.”

“Not one person.” Kade moved the bracket. “One active system.”

The phrase quieted something in his head.

Eli ran the simulation again.

With the shared state visible, each could see the load the other had created. Sera turned before the frame. Eli lowered his end to preserve level. The stretcher passed through without touching either side.

The patient indicator remained centered.

Eli stared at the result.

“That is either obvious or illegal.”

“Mara said dangerous.”

“Promising.”

Sera sat again.

“In a real carry, shared information does not remove separate responsibility.”

Kade wrote that down.

Sera caught him.

“Do I get citation credit?”

“You get the satisfaction of contributing to academia.”

“Delete it.”

He kept writing.

The common room returned to its arguments and whispered vocabulary. Eli rebuilt the simulator with operator limits visible. Taren dropped a packet of noodles beside Kade without asking whether he wanted them.

Kade finished the first page.

Then the second.

He showed the note to Eli, who marked the place where the machine state changed without a cause. Sera crossed out a sentence that claimed the load remained passive. Nessa read the diagram and pointed out that the operators would perceive the shared change differently.

By midnight, four people had corrected the paper Mara had taught Kade to write.

His name stayed at the top.

It no longer looked like the only important one.

The next morning, Professor Amadi returned the problem set.

Mara’s score was perfect.

Kade’s was not.

He had improved enough to be annoyed by exactly where he had lost points.

Written at the bottom of his page, beneath a long correction in red, was a single line.

**You understand more than your notation suggests. Fix the notation before it limits the understanding.**

Kade read it twice.

Then a third time.

He folded the page and put it into his bag instead of throwing it away.

At lunch, Eli asked why he was carrying paper.

“Evidence.”

“Of what?”

Kade looked toward Mara at another table, arguing with a classmate over a projected coupling model.

Then at his own corrected equations.

“That I might be teachable.”

Eli pressed a hand to his chest.

“I need to sit down.”

“You are sitting.”

“Then the system is working.”

Kade laughed.

That afternoon, Professor Amadi assigned a team exercise using the same two-operator coupling model Mara had questioned.

Her group changed the assumptions.

The simulation score improved twelve percent.

Amadi stared at the result.

“Vey.”

Mara stood.

“Explain.”

For the first time since Kade had met her, Mara glanced at him before answering an instructor.

Not asking permission.

Acknowledging origin.

Then she explained the shared-state correction in clean mathematical language Kade could almost follow.

Professor Amadi listened.

When Mara finished, Amadi said, “Interesting. Dangerous if generalized.”

Mara nodded.

“Yes.”

Amadi looked toward Kade.

“Mercer.”

He sighed.

“Yes?”

“Did you contribute to this?”

“Mara did the math.”

“That was not my question.”

Kade looked at the model.

“I said the system changed once the operators started correcting each other.”

Amadi studied him for a long moment.

“Write a note on it.”

“I did.”

Mara looked down to hide what was absolutely a smile.

Amadi looked less surprised than Kade wanted.

“Then submit it. Two pages. Proper notation.”

“This feels like punishment for having ideas.”

“Welcome to academia.”

The class laughed.

Kade returned to his seat.

Eli whispered, “You accidentally invented homework.”

Kade looked at Mara.

She mouthed one word.

*Progress.*

He hated her a little.

The feeling was becoming suspiciously comfortable.

Two days later, Amadi tested whether the improvement existed when Mara was not sitting beside him.

She called it a timed technical recitation.

Kade called it an ambush with stationery.

Teams of four received a failing stabilization system and twenty minutes to diagnose it, write the governing transfer equations, and propose a correction before the simulated structure crossed its failure threshold.

Mara was assigned across the room.

Eli’s team worked on the mezzanine, safely beyond signaling range.

Kade’s team included Owen Park, Nessa Kim, and a feeder-program student named Leila Tran who had been writing tensor notation since approximately birth.

The simulation started.

The north support began oscillating.

Leila wrote equations immediately.

Owen checked constraints.

Nessa watched the live field changes.

Kade watched the structure.

For thirty seconds, everybody did the thing they were best at.

Then the model began failing faster.

“Your damping term is late,” Kade said.

Leila did not look up.

“It is synchronized to the field update.”

“The field update is synchronized to the sensor.”

“Yes.”

“The structure moves before the sensor finishes reporting it.”

Owen looked at Kade.

“Write what you mean.”

Old Kade would have pointed at the simulation and expected that to count.

Kade took the stylus.

His first line used the wrong subscript.

Owen tapped it.

Kade corrected it without defending himself.

Second line.

Third.

He wrote the delay term Mara had drilled into him the night before.

Leila stopped.

“Oh.”

Kade looked at her.

That was a satisfying sound.

They changed the model.

Failure time increased.

Not enough.

Nessa said, “West operator is compensating twice.”

Kade saw it.

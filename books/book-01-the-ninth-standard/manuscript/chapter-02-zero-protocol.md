# Chapter 2 — Zero Protocol

By three in the afternoon, Kade had been told not to leave Northline by four different people who all insisted he was not being detained.

This seemed to him like a distinction created by people who had never been detained.

He sat in Vice Principal Russell’s office with Darius sprawled in the chair beside him and Ms. Alvarez standing against the wall, arms folded. Outside the narrow window, two black government vehicles occupied spaces usually reserved for district maintenance vans.

A third vehicle had arrived without markings at all.

Darius tilted his head toward it.

“That one’s definitely secret.”

Kade looked through the blinds. “Because it’s black?”

“Because it has no logo.”

“Maybe they’re embarrassed.”

“I’d be embarrassed if I drove that.”

“It probably costs more than your house.”

Darius considered this. “Then I’d be embarrassed with heated seats.”

Russell stopped pacing.

“Could both of you please understand that this is serious?”

Darius looked at Kade.

Kade looked at Darius.

“No,” they said together.

Russell closed his eyes.

Ms. Alvarez’s mouth moved in what might have been an attempt not to smile.

The national testing network had come back online nineteen minutes after it collapsed.

Everyone’s scores had returned.

Everyone except Kade’s.

His candidate page now displayed a gray rectangle and one sentence.

**RESULT WITHHELD — AUTHORIZED REVIEW**

That was somehow worse than zero.

Zero had been funny for at least eight seconds.

Authorized review sounded expensive.

Kade’s phone buzzed on Russell’s desk.

He reached for it.

Russell slapped one finger onto the screen before he could.

“No.”

“It’s my phone.”

“You have received forty-seven messages in the last twenty minutes.”

“Forty-eight now.”

“That is exactly my point.”

Darius leaned over. “Tell him about the video.”

Russell’s face changed.

Kade frowned. “What video?”

Darius became fascinated by the ceiling.

“Darius.”

“People filmed the network screen.”

“People?”

“Some people.”

“How many?”

“Define many.”

Kade reached for his phone again.

Russell moved it farther away.

“Mr. Mercer, until the district’s legal office tells me otherwise, you are not posting anything, answering questions, or speaking to anyone claiming to represent a news organization.”

“Does my mother count as a news organization?”

Russell went still.

Ms. Alvarez’s expression shifted.

Kade looked between them.

“What?”

Russell cleared his throat. “We have been attempting to contact her.”

Kade checked the wall clock.

“She's at work.”

“We know.”

“You called the hospital?”

“Yes.”

Kade stood.

The room changed.

Not dramatically. Nobody reached for him.

But the humor went out of him all at once.

“You called her floor?”

Russell spread his hands. “Kade—”

“She works emergency. You don’t call emergency unless somebody is actually hurt.”

“We needed a parent or guardian present.”

“You could have texted me.”

“We did.”

Kade looked at his phone sitting facedown under Russell’s hand.

Darius coughed into his fist.

Kade sat again.

“Fine.”

Russell slid the phone across the desk.

There were three messages from his mother.

**CALL ME.**

**Kade. Now.**

Then, two minutes later:

**I am leaving work. If this is because you punched someone I am putting you back myself.**

Kade smiled despite himself.

He typed:

**No punching. Weird test thing. Sorry.**

Three dots appeared almost immediately.

**That sentence did not improve my mood.**

He put the phone away.

Darius leaned closer. “Your mom scary?”

“Yes.”

“Cool.”

The office door opened.

Everyone stopped talking.

The man who entered did not look like Kade’s idea of a secret government official.

He was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders, with silver beginning at his temples and a face that looked more tired than dangerous. He wore a dark suit without a tie and carried a thin case under one arm.

Behind him came a woman in a charcoal uniform with no visible rank markings.

She was smaller than him, maybe thirty-five, with dark hair pulled back and eyes that took in the entire room before settling on Kade.

The man looked at Russell.

“Vice Principal Russell?”

“Yes.”

“David Renn, International Resonance Authority. This is Lieutenant Commander Nia Vale, Federal Resonance Security.”

Darius whispered, “Called it.”

Kade whispered back, “You said secret.”

“She has no rank badge.”

“She literally introduced her rank.”

Vale looked at them.

Darius sat straighter.

Renn set the case on Russell’s desk.

“We need the room.”

Russell frowned. “These students are in my care.”

“One student,” Vale said. “Mr. Bell may leave.”

Darius pointed at himself. “Mr. Bell?”

Kade said, “Don’t get attached.”

Darius rose slowly.

He looked at Kade.

For once, there was no joke in his face.

“You good?”

Kade almost answered automatically.

Fine.

Always fine.

Instead he looked at the black vehicles outside.

“No idea.”

Darius nodded as though that were a better answer.

“Text me.”

“Russell’s going to confiscate my personality next.”

“I’m not sure he’d know where to find it.”

Darius left.

Vale’s eyes followed him until the door closed.

“Ms. Alvarez may remain,” Renn said. “Mr. Russell as well. We will wait for Mrs. Mercer before discussing protected material.”

Kade leaned back.

“How protected?”

Renn regarded him.

“That depends on how much of what happened today you remember.”

The pressure behind Kade’s eyes returned as if invited.

He remembered the blank screen.

The word.

**REDEFINE.**

He kept his face still.

“All of it.”

Vale and Renn looked at one another.

It was brief.

It was enough.

Kade noticed.

“You hoped I wouldn’t.”

Renn sat in the chair Darius had vacated.

“I hoped the system had done what it was designed to do.”

“What was it designed to do?”

“Stop.”

“That seems like bad testing.”

“Sometimes stopping is the most important thing a machine can do.”

Kade’s skin prickled.

Ms. Alvarez unfolded her arms.

“Mr. Renn, if there is any risk to Kade—”

“There is no immediate risk that we know of.”

“That sentence has several problems.”

Kade glanced at her.

“See? Philosophically inconsistent instructions.”

“Not now, Mercer.”

He shut up.

Renn opened the case.

Inside was a tablet, two paper folders, and what looked like an old-fashioned metal key.

The key interested Kade most.

Nobody carried physical keys for information unless they wanted everyone to understand that the information mattered.

Renn touched the key to the tablet.

The screen lit.

A red header appeared.

**ZERO PROTOCOL**

Kade’s stomach tightened.

“So it has a name.”

Vale answered this time.

“Yes.”

“What is it?”

“A failsafe.”

“For what?”

Neither answered.

Kade laughed once without humor.

“Very productive meeting.”

Renn folded his hands.

“Mr. Mercer, the Aptitude Ascension Examination does not issue a score of zero.”

“I saw it.”

“You saw three digits. Those digits are not a score.”

Kade stared at him.

“Then what are they?”

“A routing code.”

“To you?”

“To a restricted review process.”

Kade looked at the tablet again.

ZERO PROTOCOL.

“What makes it trigger?”

Renn’s face went blank in the particular way adults’ faces did when they were deciding whether a teenager counted as a person who deserved an answer.

Kade hated that expression.

Vale noticed.

“An examination path you were never supposed to reach,” she said.

Renn glanced at her.

Vale continued anyway.

“Your test entered a diagnostic branch that has not appeared in the public exam specification in decades.”

“Why was it still there?”

“That,” Vale said, “is one of the questions we are asking.”

Kade thought of the floating selection handle.

The boundary moving anywhere.

The blank gray screen.

“What if I answered wrong?”

Vale’s eyes sharpened.

“You didn’t.”

The office became very quiet.

Renn leaned back.

“Nia.”

“He deserves that much.”

Kade looked between them.

“What did I answer?”

Vale said nothing.

Kade leaned forward.

“What did I answer?”

The door opened again.

“Kade Mercer.”

He closed his eyes.

There were moments in life when a person wanted their mother.

There were other moments when hearing their full name in their mother’s voice made them consider alternate citizenship.

Elena Mercer stood in the doorway wearing navy hospital scrubs under a gray coat she had clearly thrown on while moving. Her dark hair, usually tied neatly at the base of her neck, had escaped in three directions. An employee badge from St. Vincent Medical still hung from her pocket.

She looked first at Kade.

Head.

Hands.

Clothes.

No blood.

Then the strangers.

Then Russell.

“I was told there was a federal incident involving my son.”

Kade lifted one hand. “No punching.”

Her eyes cut to him.

“I got that message.”

“It seemed important.”

“It was the only reassuring part.”

She came to him and put both hands on his face.

Kade tolerated this because resistance would only extend the process.

“You’re okay?”

“Yes.”

“Headache?”

He hesitated.

Her eyes narrowed.

“A little.”

“How little?”

“Normal little.”

“You do not have a normal little headache category.”

Vale almost smiled.

Elena saw it.

“And you are?”

Introductions happened again.

Elena listened without interrupting.

That was how Kade knew she was angry.

When she was merely irritated, she asked questions while other people were still talking.

Renn explained the national shutdown, the withheld result, and the Zero Protocol in careful language that managed to contain almost no useful information.

When he finished, Elena said, “You have described procedure. You have not described why my son is involved.”

Renn paused.

Elena waited.

Kade looked at Ms. Alvarez.

She gave the smallest nod, as if to say: yes, this is where you got it.

Renn finally said, “We believe Kade may possess an atypical Resonance profile.”

Kade felt something inside him go still.

His mother did not.

“How atypical?”

“We do not know.”

“Dangerous?”

“No evidence of that.”

“To himself?”

“No evidence.”

“To other people?”

“No evidence.”

“Then why are there three government vehicles outside his school?”

Renn looked at Vale.

Vale said, “Because the last time Zero Protocol generated a valid route, the program that contained it was classified.”

Kade stopped breathing for a moment.

Elena’s gaze sharpened.

“The last time?”

Renn closed his eyes briefly.

Vale did not look sorry.

Kade said, “You told me it hadn’t appeared in decades.”

“Yes.”

“How many decades?”

Vale looked directly at him.

“Three.”

Thirty-one years since Resonance entered the world.

The number formed in his mind before he could stop it.

Renn must have seen something on his face.

“Do not build conclusions from incomplete information.”

“That’s basically my hobby.”

“Kade,” Elena said.

He looked at her.

She had gone pale around the mouth.

“Sorry.”

Renn slid one of the paper folders across the desk.

On the front was the silver crest of Asterion Academy.

Kade recognized it immediately.

Everyone did.

Asterion was what happened if a university, military base, professional sports league, and national obsession had a very expensive child.

He did not touch the folder.

“That’s not funny.”

“No one is joking,” Renn said.

Kade stared at the crest.

He had seen Asterion matches. Everybody had. He knew the names of famous graduates even though he pretended not to. He knew that admission rates were ridiculous, that Resonant students spent years training before applying, that families moved cities to improve academy placement scores.

Northline Alternative did not send students to Asterion.

Northline struggled to send students back to regular school.

“This is a mistake.”

Renn shook his head.

“Asterion has invoked a classified admissions authority.”

“I didn’t apply.”

“You do not need to.”

“I’m not Resonant.”

“We no longer know that.”

“I scored zero.”

“You did not score zero.”

That was becoming irritating.

Kade pushed the folder away.

“No.”

Everyone looked at him.

Elena’s eyes narrowed.

Renn said, “Mr. Mercer, I do not think you understand the offer.”

“I understand the part where a test breaks, the government arrives, nobody answers anything, and then you hand me a brochure for the most selective academy on Earth.”

“It is not a brochure.”

“Excellent. That fixes it.”

“Kade,” Elena said again.

He stood.

“I’m going home.”

Vale stepped away from the door rather than blocking him.

That made him pause.

Renn said, “You are permitted to go home.”

“Generous.”

“You are also required to report to Asterion for evaluation within seventy-two hours.”

Kade turned.

“Required?”

“A valid Zero Protocol triggers federal Resonance safety statutes.”

“So it isn’t an offer.”

“No.”

Kade laughed once.

Of course.

Of course his first invitation to somewhere impossible came with compulsory attendance.

He looked at Elena.

She was reading the folder now.

Her face gave away nothing.

“Can we go?” he asked.

She looked at Renn.

“He leaves with me?”

“Yes.”

“No surveillance inside our home.”

Vale said, “Agreed.”

“No press contact.”

“Agreed.”

“No medical testing without my consent or his.”

Renn hesitated.

Elena’s expression changed by half a degree.

Renn said, “Non-emergency procedures require consent.”

“Put it in writing.”

He did.

Twenty minutes later, Kade walked out of Northline beside his mother.

Darius waited across the parking lot with his backpack hanging from one shoulder.

He raised both eyebrows at the government cars.

Kade lifted the Asterion folder.

Darius’s mouth opened.

Then closed.

Then opened again.

Kade pointed at him. “Enjoy this moment. It may never happen again.”

Darius found his voice.

“You’re going to Asterion?”

“Apparently I’m being kidnapped educationally.”

Elena kept walking. “He is going for an evaluation.”

Darius looked at Kade with an expression Kade could not read.

That was unusual.

Then he grinned.

“Try not to get expelled before dinner.”

“Ambitious.”

Darius held out his fist.

Kade bumped it.

“Text me,” Darius said.

“I will.”

“And if they give you powers—”

“I know. Posture.”

Darius laughed.

Elena unlocked their car.

Kade climbed into the passenger seat.

They drove six blocks before she spoke.

“You were going to refuse.”

He stared through the window.

“I did refuse.”

“No. You performed refusal.”

He looked at her.

“What does that mean?”

“It means you decided you hated it before you had to admit you wanted it.”

Kade turned back toward the glass.

Streetlights moved across his reflection.

“That sounds rehearsed.”

“I have been your mother for seventeen years.”

“Long-term research project.”

“Kade.”

The joke died halfway out.

She kept both hands on the wheel.

“You think I don’t know what those schools did to you?”

He said nothing.

“You think I didn’t read every report? Sit in every meeting? Listen to people tell me that if you would only apply yourself, only cooperate, only stop asking why?”

“I caused some of it.”

“Yes.”

He looked at her, surprised.

Elena glanced over.

“You absolutely caused some of it.”

“Thanks.”

“You’re welcome. You are difficult when you are afraid.”

“I’m not afraid.”

“There.”

“What?”

“That.”

He frowned.

“You rejected the word before you checked whether it fit.”

He looked away again.

She drove another block.

Then said, softer, “You have spent years demanding somebody give you a real chance.”

Kade’s throat tightened.

“I never demanded Asterion.”

“No. You demanded to be measured by what you can actually do instead of how well you sit still while someone explains something you already understand.”

He could not argue with that without lying.

Elena stopped at a red light.

She looked at him fully.

“Do not get scared now that somebody finally asked the right question.”

Kade thought of the test.

**Identify the boundary of the active system.**

He thought of drawing a line where the software said no line should be.

He thought of the proctor stepping backward.

“Mom.”

“Yes?”

“What if the question wasn’t supposed to have an answer?”

The light turned green.

Elena looked ahead.

“Then I suppose they should not have asked you.”

Kade smiled despite himself.

That evening, Asterion Academy sent a transport itinerary.

Departure: forty-three hours.

Destination: Asterion Island.

Status: **MANDATORY EVALUATION — ZERO PROTOCOL**.

Kade read the words three times.

Then opened the closet and stared at everything he owned that could fit into one bag.

For the first time in years, he had no joke ready.

# Chapter 2 — Zero Protocol

By three in the afternoon, Kade had been told not to leave Northline by four different people who all insisted he was not being detained.

This seemed to him like a distinction invented by people who had never been detained.

He sat in Vice Principal Russell’s office with Darius folded badly into the chair beside him and Ms. Alvarez standing against the wall, arms crossed. Outside the narrow window, two black government vehicles occupied spaces normally reserved for district maintenance vans.

A third vehicle had arrived without markings at all.

Darius tilted his head toward it.

“That one’s definitely secret.”

Kade looked through the blinds. “Because it’s black?”

“Because it has no logo.”

“Maybe they’re embarrassed.”

“I’d be embarrassed if I drove that.”

“It probably costs more than your house.”

Darius considered the vehicle again. “Then I’d be embarrassed with heated seats.”

Russell stopped pacing.

“Could both of you please understand that this is serious?”

Darius looked at Kade.

Kade looked at Darius.

“No,” they said together.

Russell closed his eyes.

Ms. Alvarez’s mouth shifted in what might have been an attempt not to smile.

Kade appreciated the effort.

The national testing network had come back online nineteen minutes after it collapsed. Everyone’s results had returned.

Everyone except his.

His candidate page now showed a gray rectangle and four words.

**RESULT WITHHELD — AUTHORIZED REVIEW**

That was somehow worse than zero.

Zero had been funny for at least eight seconds.

Authorized review sounded expensive.

His phone buzzed on Russell’s desk.

Kade reached for it.

Russell planted one finger on the screen before he could.

“No.”

“It’s my phone.”

“You have received forty-seven messages in twenty minutes.”

The phone buzzed again.

“Forty-eight.”

“That is exactly my point.”

Darius leaned forward. “Tell him about the video.”

Russell’s face changed.

Kade looked at Darius. “What video?”

Darius became intensely interested in the acoustic tile ceiling.

“Darius.”

“People filmed the network map.”

“People?”

“Some people.”

“How many?”

“Define many.”

Kade reached for the phone again.

Russell moved it farther away.

“Mr. Mercer, until the district legal office tells me otherwise, you are not posting anything, answering questions, or speaking to anyone claiming to represent a news organization.”

“Does my mother count as a news organization?”

Russell went still.

Ms. Alvarez unfolded her arms.

Kade looked between them.

“What?”

Russell cleared his throat. “We have been attempting to contact her.”

Kade checked the wall clock.

“She’s at work.”

“We know.”

“You called the hospital?”

“Yes.”

Kade stood.

Nobody reached for him. Nobody had to. The room changed anyway.

The humor went out of him so quickly that Darius sat upright.

“You called her floor?”

Russell spread his hands. “Kade—”

“She works emergency. You don’t call emergency unless somebody is actually hurt.”

“We needed a parent or guardian present.”

“You could have texted me.”

“We did.”

Kade looked at his phone sitting facedown under Russell’s hand.

Darius coughed into his fist.

Kade sat again.

“Fine.”

Russell slid the phone across the desk.

There were three messages from Elena Mercer.

**CALL ME.**

**Kade. Now.**

Then, two minutes later:

**I AM LEAVING WORK. IF THIS IS BECAUSE YOU PUNCHED SOMEONE I AM PUTTING YOU BACK MYSELF.**

Kade smiled despite himself.

He typed:

**No punching. Weird test thing. Sorry.**

Three dots appeared almost immediately.

**That sentence did not improve my mood.**

He put the phone away.

Darius leaned closer. “Your mom scary?”

“Yes.”

“Cool.”

The office door opened.

Everyone stopped talking.

The man who entered did not look like Kade’s idea of a secret government official. He was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders, with silver beginning at his temples and a face that looked more tired than dangerous. He wore a dark suit without a tie and carried a narrow case under one arm.

Behind him came a woman in a charcoal uniform with no visible insignia. She was smaller, maybe thirty-five, with dark hair pulled back and eyes that took in the whole room before settling on Kade.

The man looked at Russell.

“Vice Principal Russell?”

“Yes.”

“David Renn, International Resonance Authority. Lieutenant Commander Nia Vale, Federal Resonance Security.”

Darius whispered, “Called it.”

Kade whispered back, “You said secret.”

“She has no rank badge.”

“She literally introduced her rank.”

Vale looked at them.

Darius sat straighter.

Renn set the case on Russell’s desk.

“We need the room.”

Russell frowned. “These students are in my care.”

“One student,” Vale said. “Mr. Bell may leave.”

Darius pointed at himself. “Mr. Bell?”

Kade said, “Don’t get attached.”

Darius rose slowly. At the door, he looked back at Kade.

For once, there was no joke waiting on his face.

“You good?”

Kade almost gave him the normal answer.

Fine.

Always fine.

Instead he looked at the government vehicles outside, then at the case on Russell’s desk.

“No idea.”

Darius nodded as though that were somehow better.

“Text me.”

“Russell’s going to confiscate my personality next.”

“I’m not sure he’d know where to find it.”

Darius left.

Vale watched the door close behind him.

“Ms. Alvarez may remain,” Renn said. “Mr. Russell as well. We will wait for Mrs. Mercer before discussing protected material.”

Kade leaned back.

“How protected?”

Renn regarded him.

“That depends on how much of what happened today you remember.”

The pressure behind Kade’s eyes returned as if the question had invited it.

The blank gray screen.

The invisible system.

The word that had appeared after he drew a boundary where no boundary was supposed to exist.

**REDEFINE.**

“All of it,” he said.

Vale and Renn exchanged a glance.

Brief.

Controlled.

Enough.

Kade noticed.

“You hoped I wouldn’t.”

Renn took Darius’s chair.

“I hoped the examination did what it was designed to do.”

“What was it designed to do?”

“Stop.”

“That seems like bad testing.”

“Sometimes stopping is the most important thing a machine can do.”

Something prickled between Kade’s shoulder blades.

Ms. Alvarez said, “If there is a risk to Kade, say so.”

“There is no immediate risk that we know of.”

She stared at him.

“That sentence has three problems.”

Kade glanced at her. “See? Philosophically inconsistent instructions.”

“Not now, Mercer.”

He shut up.

Renn opened the case.

Inside were a tablet, two paper folders, and an old-fashioned metal key.

The key interested Kade most.

Almost nobody used physical keys anymore. Carrying one for information was theater—or paranoia.

Possibly both.

Renn touched it to the edge of the tablet.

The screen came alive beneath a red header.

**ZERO PROTOCOL**

Kade’s stomach tightened.

“So it has a name.”

Vale answered. “Yes.”

“What is it?”

“A failsafe.”

“For what?”

Neither answered.

Kade looked at Ms. Alvarez. “Excellent meeting so far.”

Renn folded his hands.

“The Aptitude Ascension Examination does not issue a score of zero.”

“I saw it.”

“You saw three digits. Those digits were not a score.”

“Then what were they?”

“A routing code.”

“To you?”

“To a restricted review process.”

Kade looked again at the words ZERO PROTOCOL.

“What makes it trigger?”

Renn’s face went neutral in the particular way adults’ faces did when they were deciding whether a teenager counted as someone entitled to the truth.

Kade hated that expression.

Vale noticed.

“An examination path you were never supposed to reach,” she said.

Renn turned his head toward her.

Vale continued anyway.

“Your test entered a diagnostic branch that has not appeared in the public examination specification in decades.”

“Why was it still there?”

“That,” Vale said, “is one of the questions we are asking.”

Kade pictured the selection handle breaking free from the objects on the screen.

The boundary moving anywhere.

The blank field asking him to define a system when no system had been drawn.

“What if I answered wrong?”

Vale’s eyes sharpened.

“You didn’t.”

The office became very quiet.

Renn leaned back. “Nia.”

“He deserves that much.”

Kade leaned forward.

“What did I answer?”

Vale said nothing.

“What did I answer?”

The office door opened.

“Kade Mercer.”

He closed his eyes.

There were moments in life when a person wanted their mother.

There were other moments when hearing their full name in their mother’s voice made them consider alternate citizenship.

Elena Mercer stood in the doorway wearing navy hospital scrubs under a gray coat she had clearly thrown on while moving. Her dark hair, usually tied neatly at the base of her neck, had escaped in three directions. Her St. Vincent Medical badge still hung from one pocket.

She looked first at Kade.

Head.

Hands.

Clothes.

No blood.

Then the strangers.

Then Russell.

“I was told there was a federal incident involving my son.”

Kade raised one hand. “No punching.”

Her eyes cut to him.

“I got that message.”

“It seemed important.”

“It was the only reassuring part.”

She crossed the office and put both hands against his face.

Kade endured the inspection because resistance only extended it.

“You’re okay?”

“Yes.”

“Headache?”

He hesitated.

Her eyes narrowed.

“A little.”

“How little?”

“Normal little.”

“You do not have a normal-little-headache category.”

Vale almost smiled.

Elena saw it.

“And you are?”

Introductions happened again.

Elena listened without interrupting.

That was how Kade knew she was angry.

When she was merely irritated, she asked questions while other people were still speaking.

Renn explained the shutdown, the withheld result, and Zero Protocol in careful sentences that managed to contain almost no useful information.

When he finished, Elena said, “You have described procedure. You have not described why my son is involved.”

Renn paused.

Elena waited.

Kade looked toward Ms. Alvarez.

She gave the smallest nod.

Yes. This was where he got it.

Renn finally said, “We believe Kade may possess an atypical Resonance profile.”

Something inside Kade went very still.

His mother did not.

“How atypical?”

“We do not know.”

“Dangerous?”

“No evidence of that.”

“To himself?”

“No evidence.”

“To other people?”

“No evidence.”

“Then why are there three government vehicles outside his school?”

Renn looked at Vale.

Vale said, “Because the last time Zero Protocol generated a valid route, the program that contained it was classified.”

Kade stopped breathing for a moment.

Elena’s gaze sharpened.

“The last time?”

Renn closed his eyes briefly.

Vale did not look sorry.

“You told me it hadn’t appeared in decades,” Kade said.

“Yes.”

“How many?”

Vale looked directly at him.

“Three.”

Thirty-one years since Resonance had entered the world.

The number formed in Kade’s mind before he could stop it.

Renn must have seen something on his face.

“Do not construct conclusions from incomplete information.”

“That’s basically my hobby.”

“Kade.”

He looked at his mother.

She had gone pale around the mouth.

“Sorry.”

Renn slid one of the paper folders across the desk.

On the cover was a silver crest Kade recognized immediately.

Everyone would.

**ASTERION ACADEMY**

Asterion was what happened if a university, military base, professional sports league, and national obsession had a very expensive child.

Kade did not touch the folder.

“That’s not funny.”

“No one is joking,” Renn said.

Kade stared at the crest.

He had watched Asterion matches. Everybody had. He knew famous graduates even though he pretended not to. He knew families moved cities for academy feeder programs, that applicants trained for years, that admission percentages were absurdly small.

Northline Alternative did not send students to Asterion.

Northline struggled to send students back to regular school.

“This is a mistake.”

“Asterion has invoked a classified admissions authority.”

“I didn’t apply.”

“You did not need to.”

“I’m not Resonant.”

“We no longer know that.”

“I scored zero.”

“You did not score zero.”

That sentence was becoming irritating enough to qualify as an institutional slogan.

Kade pushed the folder back across the desk.

“No.”

Everyone looked at him.

Elena’s eyes narrowed.

Renn said, “Mr. Mercer, I do not think you understand the offer.”

“I understand the part where a national test breaks, the government arrives, nobody answers anything, and then you hand me a folder for the most selective academy on Earth.”

“It is not a standard admissions offer.”

“Excellent. That makes it normal.”

“Kade,” Elena said.

He stood.

“I’m going home.”

Vale moved away from the doorway rather than toward it.

That made him pause.

Renn said, “You are permitted to go home.”

“Generous.”

“You are also required to report to Asterion for evaluation within seventy-two hours.”

Kade turned.

“Required?”

“A valid Zero Protocol route triggers federal Resonance safety statutes.”

“So this isn’t an offer.”

“No.”

Of course.

Of course the first invitation Kade Mercer ever received to someplace impossible came with compulsory attendance.

He looked at Elena.

She had opened the folder.

Her face gave away nothing.

“Can we go?” he asked.

She looked at Renn.

“He leaves with me?”

“Yes.”

“No surveillance inside our home.”

Vale answered. “Agreed.”

“No press contact through your offices.”

“Agreed.”

“No non-emergency medical testing without his informed consent and mine while he is a minor.”

Renn hesitated.

Elena’s expression changed by half a degree.

Renn said, “Non-emergency procedures require consent.”

“Put it in writing.”

He did.

Twenty minutes later Kade stepped out of Northline beside his mother.

The desert light had softened toward evening. Three government vehicles still sat in the lot, looking much more dramatic than a school wedged between a tire warehouse and self-storage had ever requested.

Darius waited across the parking lot with his backpack hanging from one shoulder.

He raised both eyebrows.

Kade lifted the Asterion folder.

Darius’s mouth opened.

Then closed.

Then opened again.

Kade pointed at him. “Enjoy this moment. It may never happen again.”

“You’re going to Asterion?”

“Apparently I’m being kidnapped educationally.”

Elena kept walking. “He is going for an evaluation.”

Darius looked at Kade with an expression Kade could not read.

That was unusual.

Then the grin arrived.

“Try not to get expelled before dinner.”

“Ambitious.”

Darius held out his fist.

Kade bumped it.

“Text me.”

“I will.”

“And if they give you powers—”

“I know. Fix your posture.”

“Exactly.”

The grin faded a little.

“You’re actually going?”

Kade looked at the folder.

“Apparently.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

For a second Kade had the strange sensation that Darius and his mother had attended the same secret class about him.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Darius nodded.

“Okay.”

No speech. No prediction about destiny. No sudden treatment of Kade as something special.

Just okay.

Kade appreciated it more than he wanted to explain.

Elena unlocked their car.

They drove six blocks before she spoke.

“You were going to refuse.”

Kade watched shops and parking lots slide past the window.

“I did refuse.”

“No. You performed refusal.”

He looked at her.

“What does that mean?”

“It means you decided you hated it before you had to admit you wanted it.”

“That sounds rehearsed.”

“I have been your mother for seventeen years.”

“Long-term research project.”

“Kade.”

The joke died halfway out.

Elena kept both hands on the wheel.

“You think I don’t know what those schools did to you?”

He said nothing.

“You think I didn’t read every report? Sit in every meeting? Listen to people tell me that if you would only apply yourself, only cooperate, only stop asking why?”

“I caused some of it.”

“Yes.”

He looked at her, surprised.

Elena glanced over.

“You absolutely caused some of it.”

“Thanks.”

“You are welcome. You can be exhausting.”

“Better.”

“You are also very good at becoming difficult exactly when something starts mattering.”

Kade turned back toward the window.

Streetlights passed across his reflection.

“I’m not afraid.”

“There.”

“What?”

“That.”

He frowned.

“You rejected the word before checking whether it fit.”

He looked away again.

She drove another block.

Then, quieter, “You have spent years demanding somebody give you a real chance.”

Kade’s throat tightened.

“I never demanded Asterion.”

“No. You demanded to be measured by what you can actually do instead of how well you sit still while somebody explains something you already understand.”

He could not argue without lying.

Elena stopped at a red light.

For the first time since leaving Northline she looked at him fully.

“Do not get scared now that somebody finally asked the right question.”

Kade thought about the examination.

**Identify the boundary of the active system.**

He thought about the clean boxes the test had wanted him to accept.

He thought about drawing a line through them instead.

He thought about a federal officer telling him he had not answered wrong.

“Mom.”

“Yes?”

“What if the question wasn’t supposed to have an answer?”

The light turned green.

Elena looked ahead again.

“Then they should not have asked you.”

Kade smiled despite himself.

They reached home just after six.

Their apartment looked exactly as it had that morning: shoes by the door, two pieces of mail on the counter, Elena’s half-dead basil plant leaning toward the kitchen window, one dining chair with a stack of clean laundry on it because neither of them believed in finishing a task all the way to the last four percent.

The normality felt wrong.

Kade set the Asterion folder on the table.

Elena changed out of her hospital scrubs and returned in sweatpants with her hair loose. She heated leftover soup. Kade opened the folder only after she stopped watching him.

The first page was not a welcome letter.

**MANDATORY ZERO-PROTOCOL EVALUATION**

Below it:

**ASTERION ACADEMY — RESTRICTED ADMISSIONS / SAFETY AUTHORITY**

Then pages of transport instructions, consent forms, emergency contacts, prohibited devices, clothing recommendations, medical history, and language carefully designed to make compulsory travel sound like a scholarship opportunity.

Kade turned a page.

A photograph of Asterion Island filled the top half.

He had seen the island on broadcasts a hundred times.

Seeing his own name printed beneath it felt different.

**Candidate: Kade A. Mercer**

He closed the folder.

Elena set a bowl of soup in front of him.

“You hungry?”

“No.”

“Eat anyway.”

He did.

Halfway through, she said, “You know I cannot go with you past intake.”

He looked up.

“It says parents can attend medical review.”

“For the first day. After that, if you remain, it becomes academy housing.”

“If I remain.”

“If.”

He studied her.

“You want me to.”

Elena rested her elbows on the table.

“I want you to go long enough to find out what this actually is.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

“You’d be okay if I came home.”

“Yes.”

The answer arrived without hesitation.

That mattered.

She continued, “I would also be disappointed if you came home because you were afraid to find out whether you belonged there.”

Kade stared into his soup.

“Everybody is very interested in my emotional growth today.”

“I can stop and tell you to wash your bowl.”

“Comforting.”

She smiled.

Then her face softened, just a little.

“Your father would have—”

She stopped.

Kade looked up.

Elena’s eyes moved to the Asterion folder.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That was clearly not nothing.”

She stood and took her bowl to the sink.

“Your father would have hated the paperwork.”

Kade watched her back.

Aaron Mercer had been dead since Kade was too young to remember him clearly. Industrial accident. Equipment failure. A few photographs. A handful of stories Elena almost never volunteered.

“That sounded like a safer sentence than the first one.”

Elena turned on the faucet.

“It was.”

Kade waited.

She did not say more.

He filed the moment away because that was what he did with things people tried to make look unimportant.

At 20:14 his phone chimed.

Asterion Academy had sent a transport itinerary.

Departure: forty-three hours.

Destination: Asterion Island.

Status:

**MANDATORY EVALUATION — ZERO PROTOCOL**

Kade read the words three times.

Then he went to his room, opened the closet, and stared at everything he owned that could fit into one bag.

The old instinct suggested several jokes.

He ignored all of them.

For the first time in years, Kade Mercer let himself admit the thing before he could ruin it.

He wanted to know what happened next.

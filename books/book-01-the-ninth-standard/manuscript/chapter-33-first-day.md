# Chapter 33 — First Day

Three months later, Asterion looked repaired from a distance.

Up close, Kade could still find the scars.

A replacement wall panel near Meridian that reflected morning light at the wrong angle.

A darker strip of flooring outside Lab Six.

New fire doors in the east engineering wing.

A hairline repair across the arena once called the Mercer Fault.

He preferred that.

Perfect repairs would have felt dishonest.

The official story of the siege occupied four pages and said almost nothing.

An unlawful security breach.

Internal extremist activity.

Infrastructure damage.

No public mention of Contact Zero.

No Refuge Node.

No Aaron Mercer.

No explanation for why several governments had quietly sent representatives to Asterion before the smoke was fully gone.

The unofficial story occupied every conversation on campus.

Students knew the Directorate had attacked from inside.

They knew some faculty had been involved.

They knew something had happened below the Spire that caused every high-level field sensor on the island to record the same impossible spike.

And they knew Kade Mercer had somehow been in the middle of it.

Again.

He was getting tired of being historically adjacent.

House Meridian’s bronze bell had been repaired first.

That had been Sera’s demand.

“It is an evacuation signal as well as a victory bell,” she had told facilities.

Facilities had attempted to explain that this was not its documented purpose.

Sera had handed them the siege response report.

The bell was repaired the next day.

The first time it rang after repairs, nobody celebrated.

Meridian used it for an evacuation drill.

The hammer struck bronze at nine in the morning.

Conversation stopped throughout the House.

For one second, nobody moved.

The sound was not the siege alarm. It was lower, older, familiar from dinners and House Trial victories. But Kade's body had already counted the distance to the west stair before the second strike.

Across the common room, a girl dropped her tablet.

Sera stood beside the courtyard doors with a timer in one hand and her response bag over one shoulder.

“This is a drill,” she called. “East stair is blocked. West stair and kitchen service exit. Do not run.”

That broke the stillness.

Students moved.

Kade had been assigned to observe the west stair because Sera claimed his talent for identifying flaws should occasionally benefit civilization. For the first minute, he found none. Floor captains cleared rooms. Two older students checked washrooms. Someone held the fire door instead of letting it slam into the next person.

Then the line stopped.

Kade moved down three steps and found a first-year sitting on the landing with a bright orange band around one ankle. The band meant simulated fracture. Everyone behind him was trying to help at once.

“I can hop,” the first-year insisted.

“On stairs?” asked one of the helpers.

“Probably.”

“That is not a reassuring word.”

Six people had formed a wall around one pretend injury. Nobody could pass them. The service exit was twenty meters away through an unmarked utility corridor, and the nearest floor captain was shouting instructions from below that could not be heard over the bell.

Kade pointed at two students.

“Chair carry. Everybody else keep moving. You, open the service route from this side.”

The first-year looked up at him.

“Are you allowed to give orders?”

“No. Move anyway.”

They did.

By the time Kade reached the courtyard, Sera had already written three lines on her tablet.

Three hundred students cleared the House in six minutes forty-two seconds.

Sera was furious.

“Too slow.”

Kade looked at the empty courtyard.

“That was faster than the published target.”

“The published target assumes nobody has a broken ankle and everybody reads signs.”

“People do read signs.”

Sera stared at him.

Kade remembered Northline.

“Fair.”

“Thirty-one seconds lost at the west landing,” Sera said. “Kitchen route had no photoluminescent marker. Two floor captains tried to use the same channel. And half the second floor waited for someone with a title.”

The first-year with the orange band lowered himself from the improvised chair.

“He gave orders.”

Sera looked at Kade.

“He hates titles. Different problem.”

She walked back inside before facilities could arrive and explain why all the missing signs were compliant with code.

She had accepted the accelerated Disaster Response Corps assessment two weeks later.

She did not talk about it much.

Which meant everyone in Meridian talked about it constantly.

The first assessment required her to evacuate a simulated transit collapse while two instructors deliberately gave contradictory orders.

One ordered her to leave six trapped passengers until structural support arrived.

The other ordered her to overload the transit shell and lift it before the simulated fire reached them.

Sera rejected both instructions, divided the shell load across four portable supports, and sent the lightest responder through a maintenance hatch the assessment designers had apparently considered decorative.

She passed.

Kade bought her a small plastic emergency whistle from the campus store.

She threw it at him.

Then attached it to her response bag.

Eli’s Systems Lab fellowship application had also been approved.

He claimed the academy had been emotionally manipulated by his temporary death.

Professor Kim claimed the stabilization design was simply good.

Kade suspected both were true.

Eli now spent three evenings a week trying to reduce civilian unit cost without accidentally starting fires.

Progress was mixed.

The first prototype failed because its thermal sink cost more than the rest of the unit.

The second worked for eleven minutes and then produced a smell nobody could identify.

The third survived Sera.

That was the important one.

They tested it on the south training deck with Captain Ortiz observing by secure call.

The third prototype fit inside a case the size of a lunch box.

That mattered more to Eli than its appearance, which was fortunate because its appearance suggested an appliance assembled during a power outage. Two heat sinks protruded from one side. The control pad had been salvaged from a training sensor. A strip of red tape covered the manufacturer name on the casing.

“Why the tape?” Kade asked.

“Because the manufacturer declined to donate parts.”

“You stole it?”

“Professor Kim used the phrase unauthorized reclamation.”

Behind the safety barrier, Professor Kim raised one finger without looking up from her tablet.

“I used that phrase while explaining why you may never do it again.”

Eli crouched beside the device.

“Academic nuance.”

Captain Ortiz's face occupied a screen bolted to the training-deck wall. She had required the call after learning that Prototype Two's unidentified smell had reached the ventilation system.

“State the limit,” she said.

Eli's grin disappeared.

“Six kilonewtons distributed load for ninety seconds. Three if the force changes direction faster than the field can re-center. Thermal cutoff at eighty-one degrees. Manual release only after the load transfers to physical support.”

“And if the cutoff fails?”

“The sacrificial bus opens.”

“And if that fails?”

Eli pointed toward the barrier.

“We stand over there.”

Ortiz did not smile.

“Good answer.”

Eli placed the portable stabilizer under a simulated collapsed beam.

Sera looked at him.

“You ready?”

“No.”

“Good.”

She overloaded the beam from three directions.

The device screamed electronically.

One casing bolt launched into a safety net.

The left heat sink flashed amber.

Eli's hand moved toward the control pad, then stopped short of touching it. The device had to correct without him. A civilian trapped under a collapsed ceiling would not have a systems engineer kneeling beside the rubble.

The load display climbed.

Five point two.

Five point eight.

Six.

The beam shifted as Sera changed the west-side force. For a fraction of a second, the field warped low enough that everyone behind the barrier bent their knees.

Then the stabilizer re-centered.

The field held.

Eli stared at the readout.

Sera stared at the beam.

Captain Ortiz said, “Again.”

Professor Kim pointed to the empty bolt housing.

“After that becomes less lethal.”

“The bolt was not part of stabilization,” Eli said.

“It attempted to stabilize itself in the ocean.”

Eli looked at Kade.

“You understood what I meant.”

“I am staying behind the barrier.”

Eli laughed.

Not his nervous laugh.

The other one.

The one Kade had missed when the bed was empty.

Behind the barrier, the knot under Kade's ribs loosened one turn.

Eli’s future had not been restored to what it had been before Lab Six.

Nothing worked like that.

But it had continued.

That was different.

And enough.

---

Taren remained at Asterion under restricted status while three separate investigations fought over what to do with him.

His Directorate cooperation had helped stop the siege.

His earlier reporting had helped make the siege possible.

Both facts were true.

Sera had not forgiven him.

Neither had Kade.

But Taren still attended classes under supervision and still sat with Meridian at meals when the rules allowed it.

The first time he returned to the table, nobody spoke for almost a minute.

Taren stood holding a tray.

A campus security officer waited four steps behind him. She made a disciplined attempt to appear uninterested in lunch.

It did not work.

Every table near Meridian had gone quiet too.

Kade looked at the empty chair across from him.

It had been Eli’s before the memorial.

Then empty by choice after Eli returned, because apparently everybody had become superstitious about chairs.

Taren looked ready to leave.

Eli said, “Pass the salt.”

Taren blinked.

Then sat down, reached across, and passed it.

“Thanks,” Eli said.

“You’re welcome.”

Sera kept eating.

Mara moved her bag off the chair beside her, not as an invitation but because the strap blocked the aisle. Julian asked the security officer whether she intended to stand there for the entire meal. She informed him that her position was operationally appropriate.

“It is making everyone operationally constipated,” Eli said.

Professor Kim, at the faculty table, lowered her cup.

The officer moved another three steps away.

Taren unfolded his napkin.

“I missed the warmth of Meridian.”

Sera set down her fork.

“Do not mistake a chair for forgiveness.”

His hands stopped.

“I don't.”

“Good.”

She resumed eating.

The whole table breathed again, except Kade, who was studying Taren's face. No smile. No quick answer shaped to make guilt entertaining. He looked thinner than before the siege. A temporary access band circled his wrist in Directorate gray because somebody in administration had an ugly sense of humor.

Kade pushed the bread basket across the table.

Taren took one piece.

That was not forgiveness either.

That had been the entire welcome.

Reconciliation was still too generous a word.

Two weeks later, Taren asked Kade if he wanted coffee.

Kade said no.

The week after that, Kade said yes.

They sat on the Meridian roof where Taren had first explained why half the school hated Kade and the other half wanted to buy him lunch.

Neither mentioned the symmetry.

Taren looked toward the ocean.

His temporary access band flashed every thirty seconds, reporting his location. Each flash reflected faintly from the coffee lid.

“They interview me on Mondays,” he said. “Academy inquiry in the morning. Federal team after lunch. Directorate oversight office on Thursdays, which is ambitious branding for people investigating their own people.”

“You want sympathy?”

“Not especially.”

“Good.”

Taren rolled the paper cup between both palms.

“I keep trying to divide it cleanly. Reports that mattered. Reports that didn't. Things they already knew. Things I gave them.”

“Does it divide?”

“No.”

Wind moved across the roof. Below them, two facilities crews replaced railing anchors along the path to the west bridge.

“Then stop trying to make it clean,” Kade said.

Taren's mouth tightened.

“That sounds like something Sera would say.”

“She says it louder.”

They let the next gust pass.

“You ever going to trust me again?”

Kade considered lying.

“No idea.”

Taren nodded.

“Fair.”

“Are you going to keep asking?”

“Probably.”

“That reduces the odds.”

“Good note.”

Kade tapped the edge of Taren's access band.

“You could leave.”

“The island?”

“Meridian. Stop walking into rooms where everybody remembers.”

Taren looked down through the roof railing toward the courtyard.

“That would be easier.”

“Usually a bad sign.”

“I learned that from watching you.”

Kade almost told him not to make this about Kade's growth. Then he remembered who had taught whom to understand the academy in the first place.

The debt did not erase the betrayal.

The betrayal did not erase the debt.

Apparently relationships were capable of terrible bookkeeping.

They drank coffee.

Trust did not return.

Something smaller did.

The possibility that one day it might.

Kade decided not to name it.

---

Julian remained number one in everyone’s head even during the three-month period when official rankings were suspended.

He hated when people said that.

Which encouraged them.

His father had arrived at Asterion forty-eight hours after the siege.

Kade met him once.

The meeting occurred in a recovery lounge that still had plastic over one window. Lucian Cross entered with two assistants and the assumption that furniture would rearrange itself for him.

Julian changed when his father crossed the room.

Not obviously. His posture was already straight. His uniform was already perfect. But every loose edge disappeared. Even the hand holding his water glass became precise.

Lucian checked Julian's medical bracelet before touching his shoulder.

“Neurologic readings?”

“Within recovery range.”

“The reports say you ceded tactical priority during the containment event.”

Julian's fingers tightened around the glass.

“The event required distributed authority.”

“Events require outcomes.”

“We achieved one.”

Lucian's attention shifted to Kade as if he had only just become relevant.

Lucian Cross shook Kade’s hand and said, “My son says you are difficult to plan around.”

Kade replied, “He says your compliments need calibration.”

Julian nearly choked on water.

Lucian did not laugh.

Kade liked him less immediately.

“Improvisation has uses,” Lucian said. “Provided someone reliable contains the cost.”

Kade released his hand.

“That isn't what Julian did.”

The lounge became quieter than a room with four people needed to be.

Lucian looked at his son.

“No?”

Julian put down the glass.

“No.”

One word, but not a retreat. Kade had heard Julian order a collapsing campus through emergency channels. This took a different kind of force.

Lucian studied them both, then asked one of his assistants for the medical director's schedule.

The conversation moved on because powerful men were good at treating resistance like a clerical delay.

Afterward Julian said, “You did that on purpose.”

“I have no idea what you mean.”

“Thank you.”

Kade looked at him.

“For insulting your father?”

“For not being impressed by him.”

That told Kade more than Julian had intended.

Kade did not file it anywhere.

He stayed with Julian until the assistants returned.

Mara had spent the break dividing her time between training, arguing with her father, and helping Sen’s independent inquiry into precursor records.

She and Alaric Vey were speaking again.

Carefully.

According to Mara, this was different from speaking normally because now they admitted when they were withholding things.

Kade found that darkly funny.

She had also beaten him six times in private sparring.

Kade had beaten her twice.

One of those wins was legitimate.

The other involved a practice barrier malfunction that Mara refused to count.

Kade counted it aggressively.

Their relationship had become easier in some ways and more complicated in others.

They studied together without needing an excuse.

They argued about coupling models in public.

Mara had started stealing Kade’s tea when hers went cold, which felt like Sera’s food theft but somehow more dangerous.

One night on the Meridian roof, Kade asked, “Are we doing something?”

Mara had his tea in one hand and a precursor translation open on her tablet. The wind kept lifting strands of hair across her face. She had refused to move inside because the library closed the roof stacks at ten and she considered rules about roofs philosophically weak.

Mara looked at him.

“Currently?”

“Do not make this difficult.”

“I thought that was your role.”

Kade stared at the ocean.

“This was a mistake.”

Mara smiled.

“Probably.”

“Excellent. Productive conversation.”

“You asked an imprecise question.”

“I asked the question people ask.”

“People are usually imprecise.”

Kade reached for his tea. Mara moved it out of range without looking.

“We study together,” she said. “We train together. You send me terrible jokes during meetings with my father.”

“He makes a face when you read them.”

“That is why I permit it.”

“So we're conducting a joint campaign against Alaric Vey.”

“Among other things.”

The answer removed every useful joke from Kade's head.

Mara set the tablet on the parapet.

“Do you want this to become simpler?”

Kade considered the question. Simpler meant a label he could use instead of paying attention. Simpler meant pretending the last three months had not changed every assumption either of them had brought to the roof.

“No.”

“Neither do I.”

Then she took his hand.

No speech.

No relationship declaration.

No dramatic change in background music because life did not have the decency to provide it.

They stood there for several minutes.

Kade's palm grew damp almost immediately. He became intensely aware that hands contained too many joints.

Mara looked down.

“Are you analyzing this?”

“Absolutely not.”

“Your thumb has not moved in ninety seconds.”

“There are wrong ways to move it.”

“Kade.”

“Fine.”

He laced his fingers through hers.

The world did not become simpler.

It became specific.

The next morning Mara corrected his notation exactly as harshly as before.

Kade found that reassuring.

---

Aaron Mercer was alive.

Almost nobody knew.

The first week after the siege, Kade visited him every day in the secure medical wing.

The second week, every other day.

By the third, they had settled into something less like reunion and more like negotiations between two people who happened to share a face.

Kade had spoken to him eleven times.

Eight conversations had ended badly.

One had ended with both of them silent for twenty minutes.

One had involved Aaron trying to explain why staying in containment had seemed necessary while Kade explained why necessity did not make seventeen years disappear.

The last conversation had gone differently.

Aaron had asked about Northline.

Not Resonance.

Not Consensus.

School.

Kade had told him about Darius and the vending machine.

Aaron had laughed.

Kade had hated how much he wanted to hear the sound again.

That was, according to Elena, progress.

The conversation after that went badly again.

Aaron tried to apologize for missing Kade’s childhood.

Kade asked which part.

Aaron did not know how to answer.

They argued for twenty minutes.

Then Aaron asked whether Kade had ever fixed the bad rear-foot plant Mara had complained about.

Kade stared.

“How do you know about that?”

Aaron looked guilty.

“Sen gave me some training reports.”

Kade became furious all over again.

Then Aaron said, “Your footwork was terrible.”

Kade laughed despite himself.

Family, apparently, could be rebuilt through highly specific criticism.

Slowly.

Elena had visited Asterion twice.

The first time she saw Aaron, she stood outside the medical-room door for six minutes before entering.

Kade did not stay for the conversation.

Some boundaries were his to respect.

Afterward, Elena sat with Kade on the seawall and cried without apologizing.

Kade had no idea what to do, so he sat beside her.

Apparently that counted.

The second visit was stranger.

Aaron came to a private dining room above the medical wing.

Park allowed him ninety minutes away from the containment support rig, provided he wore two monitors and did not attempt to be heroic about either one. A cable ran from the smaller monitor at his waist to a portable regulator beside his chair.

Aaron apologized for the machine's hum before he sat down.

Elena's face closed.

“You don't apologize for medical equipment.”

“I was apologizing for the noise.”

“Then stop doing that too.”

Aaron pulled out the chair across from her.

Kade remained standing until both of them looked at him.

“I am reconsidering this plan.”

“Sit,” Elena said.

He sat.

Three people sat at one table who had once been a family but had never actually lived together as one.

Nobody knew what to talk about.

The academy kitchen had provided roasted chicken, rice, green beans, and a cream sauce in its own covered dish. Kade suspected the separate sauce represented somebody's careful reading of the medical notes. He also suspected that person had not anticipated silence as a dietary hazard.

Elena served rice onto her plate.

Aaron adjusted his fork.

Kade drank water he did not want.

The regulator hummed.

“Your hair is shorter,” Aaron said to Elena.

Kade closed his eyes.

Elena set down the serving spoon.

“Seventeen years.”

“I know.”

“A lot of haircuts.”

Aaron looked as if he wanted the floor to open beneath him, which Kade considered both fair and unlikely in the newly repaired wing.

Elena picked up the spoon again.

“Mine got shorter. Yours got gray.”

Aaron touched one temple.

“Containment.”

“I raised a teenager. We can compare causes later.”

Kade pointed his fork at her.

“I was a delight.”

“You corrected a family therapist's grammar while he was asking about your anger.”

“His grammar was distracting.”

Aaron made a small sound into his water.

Kade looked at him.

“Do not encourage her.”

“I didn't say anything.”

“You have the same bad silent laugh.”

Aaron's expression shifted. Elena's hand stopped halfway to the beans.

For a moment, the years crowded back into the room.

Then the regulator chimed.

Aaron checked the display. His left hand trembled as he pressed the acknowledgment. He tucked it beneath the table afterward.

Elena noticed the motion but did not reach for him.

“What does that mean?” she asked.

“Load adjustment. Nothing urgent.”

“On whose definition?”

“Park's.”

“Acceptable.”

That answer seemed to surprise him.

Kade understood why. Elena trusted doctors who had not faked anyone's death.

She lifted the sauce lid, then frowned.

Elena finally asked Aaron whether he still hated mushrooms.

Aaron said yes.

Kade looked between them.

“That is the first useful family medical history anyone has given me.”

Elena laughed.

Aaron did too.

Then all three stopped because hearing both sounds together was too much.

The resemblance was not exact. Aaron's laugh broke lower. Elena's ended in a breath she tried to hide. Kade had pieces of both and had never known it.

He cut into the chicken until his knife struck the plate.

“The machine is loud,” he said.

Neither of them challenged the lie.

Elena passed Aaron the beans.

“Kade says you have letters.”

Aaron accepted the bowl with both hands.

“I do.”

“I don't want them tonight.”

His grip changed.

“All right.”

“That doesn't mean destroy them.”

“I won't.”

“And it doesn't mean I never want them.”

“I understand.”

Elena's mouth tightened at the phrase.

Aaron corrected himself.

“I'll wait for you to ask.”

“Good.”

She took a bite of rice. The boundary held.

Kade looked down at his own plate and recognized something from the chamber below the Spire. Not Consensus. Nothing that dramatic.

An answer offered without taking ownership of the next choice.

Aaron cleared his throat.

“I heard you repaired the kitchen sink.”

Elena turned to Kade.

“You told him about the sink?”

“He already knew.”

“From what?”

“A Sen report.”

Elena stared toward the ceiling as if calculating how many separate conversations she needed to have with Amara Sen.

“It was one leak,” Kade said.

“You redirected it into the cabinet.”

“That made it easier to collect.”

Aaron's shoulders shook once.

This time nobody stopped him.

Elena told the rest of the story, including details Kade considered legally irrelevant. Aaron listened without interrupting. When he asked a question, it was about the apartment and not the containment system. When Elena answered, she looked at him directly.

The regulator chimed twice more.

At eighty-seven minutes, Park appeared in the doorway.

“Time.”

Aaron's hand rested flat beside his plate.

“Could we have five more minutes?”

Park checked the monitor.

“Three.”

Nobody had a three-minute subject.

Elena folded her napkin.

“I can return in two weeks.”

Aaron looked at her.

She did not soften the offer.

“If you want dinner again,” she said.

“I do.”

“Then pick something without mushrooms.”

“I can do that.”

The remaining two minutes passed in silence, but not the helpless kind.

Dinner continued anyway.

No reconciliation.

No clean forgiveness.

Just a second dinner scheduled for two weeks later.

Kade considered that progress too.

---

The countdown continued the entire time.

Lunar Far-Side Array confirmed the returned coordinates were outside the solar system.

The sequence translated to just over one Earth year at receipt.

No government publicly acknowledged it.

Every government that knew about it treated the number like a lit fuse.

Asterion students knew only that unusual military traffic had tripled around the island.

They also knew international academy delegations had begun arriving early.

Kiyomizu sent six instructors and a systems team.

Helix sent three Standard Sevens and enough security to make everybody uncomfortable.

Vahana’s delegation brought a field vessel that hovered offshore because its captain refused to dock under American military control.

Kisiwa sent disaster-response specialists who spent an entire afternoon with Sera and left her looking happier than Kade had ever seen her.

Kade helped meet the Kisiwa team at the north dock because Sera needed someone with clearance and Julian had learned to avoid volunteering in her presence.

The dock still carried fresh welds where a Directorate field strike had broken the outer rail. Asterion security had painted over them. The ocean had already begun eating through the paint.

Four Kisiwa responders stepped off the academy launch wearing light gray field jackets with no visible rank markings. Their equipment came in identical red cases small enough for one person to carry.

Sera's attention locked onto the cases.

The team lead caught her looking.

“Collapsed-structure kit,” she said. “Variable load, independent power, no network requirement.”

Sera looked delighted for approximately half a second.

“Maximum lateral correction?”

“Depends what failed.”

“Good answer.”

They forgot Kade before reaching the end of the dock.

He followed while they compared evacuation protocols, placement-fire failure rates, and the problem of command channels becoming useless when the person holding authority could not see the structure. Sera challenged two of the team lead's assumptions. The team lead challenged four of hers. Neither treated disagreement as disrespect.

At the security arch, an Asterion officer instructed the visitors to surrender their independent communications modules.

Okafor stopped.

“Our rescue system does not enter a compromised site without an independent channel.”

The officer gestured toward the academy network certification posted beside the arch.

Sera looked at the west bridge, the Spire, and the repaired rail behind them.

“The site was compromised,” she said. “That is why they're here.”

The officer called a supervisor.

The Kisiwa team kept its modules.

On the walk to Meridian, the team lead asked Sera when she intended to sit the international response assessment.

Sera's stride shortened.

“I passed Asterion's accelerated assessment last month.”

“That was not my question.”

Sera was still trying not to smile when they disappeared into the rebuilt emergency-planning room.

Officially, the visits were part of a newly accelerated International Academy Exchange Season.

Unofficially, every major Resonance institution on Earth wanted to know what Asterion had done beneath the Spire.

Taren read the announcement over breakfast.

“International joint rankings.”

Eli looked up.

“That sounds terrible.”

“International House competition.”

Mara looked interested despite herself.

Julian, sitting two tables away, turned around.

“When?”

Taren smiled.

“There he is.”

Kade looked at the exchange schedule.

Schools he had only seen in broadcasts.

Students raised inside completely different Resonance systems.

New rankings.

New politics.

New ways to discover he was not as impressive as people currently believed.

He felt something dangerously close to excitement.

Then his tablet displayed the countdown.

Excitement adjusted accordingly.

---

The new semester began under clear skies.

On the path to the central forum, a new student stopped Kade beside the repaired east-wing doors.

The boy wore an unassigned gray uniform and held his orientation map upside down.

“Do you know where House Sol meets?”

Kade turned the map around.

The route line crossed the forum, climbed the north terrace, and vanished beneath the boy's thumb. A year earlier, Kade had arrived with one duffel bag and the conviction that asking anyone for directions created a debt.

He pointed past the Spire.

“North terrace. Gold doors. If you reach the transit platform, you went too far.”

The boy looked from the map to Kade's Meridian uniform.

“Aren't Houses supposed to hate each other?”

“Mostly during scheduled events.”

“Oh.”

“If somebody tells you otherwise before breakfast, they need a hobby.”

The boy hurried north.

Kade continued toward the forum. He did not ask the student's name or offer a speech about Asterion. Directions were enough for a first morning.

Thousands of students gathered in the central forum for the ranking reset.

Kade stood with Meridian.

The repaired Spire rose above them.

The ocean was bright enough to hurt his eyes.

International observers occupied one upper terrace.

Kade recognized uniforms from Kiyomizu, Helix, Vahana, and Kisiwa.

One Kiyomizu student looked down at the Asterion crowd with the same expression Mara used when evaluating a difficult problem.

Kade decided not to enjoy the coming semester yet.

Eli bounced on his heels.

“You nervous?”

“No.”

Mara looked at him.

Kade sighed.

“Yes.”

“Growth,” she said.

“I regret learning things.”

She smiled.

Not the tiny problem-solving smile.

A real one.

He did not make it weird.

That might have been his greatest advancement yet.

The reset rankings were not a direct continuation of the old ladder. Asterion recalculated each semester using tournament performance, challenge history, academic field evaluations, team events, instructor scoring, emergency performance, and safety-adjusted capability.

For most students the changes were modest.

For Kade, the academy had spent weeks arguing about what category he belonged in at all.

The boards illuminated.

Names populated from the middle outward.

Kade waited.

#211 disappeared into the recalculation.

Mara’s name appeared.

**VEY, MARA — #4**

Meridian cheered.

Mara looked mildly inconvenienced by being celebrated.

Julian appeared.

**CROSS, JULIAN — #1**

He accepted the obvious with great dignity.

Then Kade’s tablet vibrated.

His name appeared on the main display.

**MERCER, KADE**

**RANK: #37**

For several seconds, he simply stared.

Eli made a sound that should not have come from a human being.

Sera punched Kade lightly in the shoulder.

Taren, standing under supervision at the edge of Meridian’s group, laughed.

Julian called across the forum.

“Still thirty-six places to go.”

Kade looked at him.

“You’ve been saving that?”

“For weeks.”

“Terrible.”

Mara was smiling again.

Kade looked back at the board.

Thirty-seven.

He remembered ten thousand four hundred eighty-two.

He remembered how that first number had felt like every teacher report and every failed class compressed into one public verdict.

He remembered telling Darius to enjoy it while he could.

The strange part was that #37 did not feel like proof the old number had been wrong.

#10,482 had been true when it appeared.

He had been untrained.

Unprepared.

Terrible at footwork.

Still terrible at some notation.

The number had described something.

It simply had not described everything.

Kade liked that better.

Then the board flickered.

A second line appeared beneath his rank.

**STANDARD: IX**

The central forum went silent.

Not quiet.

Silent.

Standards did not use Roman numerals on public ranking boards.

There was no public Standard Nine.

The academy system had apparently disagreed.

Kade looked toward Sen on the administration platform.

For once, she looked surprised.

Eli whispered, “Did you know they were going to do that?”

“No.”

Mara said, “Neither did she.”

That was not reassuring.

On the international terrace, several delegates stood at the same time.

A Helix official started speaking urgently into a comm.

The Kiyomizu student Kade had noticed earlier did not look surprised.

That was interesting.

Kade had exactly one second to wonder why.

Every display on campus changed.

Asterion’s emblem vanished.

Rankings disappeared.

The coordinates appeared.

The same coordinates the lunar array had received three months earlier.

Then the countdown.

**00:364:17:42:08**

A collective sound moved through the forum.

Confusion first.

Then fear.

Eli stared.

“That is definitely days.”

Mara looked toward the administration platform.

Sen was already speaking into three secure channels.

Julian’s expression lost every trace of humor.

Sera automatically scanned exits and crowd density.

Taren looked at the numbers as if they had personally accused him of something.

Kade looked toward the Pacific.

Somewhere beyond the horizon, beyond Earth, beyond every problem he had understood while sitting in Room Four complaining about a physics question, something had answered.

His tablet buzzed.

For one absurd second he expected another classified alert.

It was Elena.

**Proud of you. Also eat something.**

Kade laughed.

Mara looked over.

“What?”

“My mother remains focused on the important threats.”

“What did she say?”

“Eat something.”

Mara considered the countdown.

“Reasonable.”

Another message arrived.

Darius.

**BRO YOU ARE THIRTY SEVEN WHAT**

Then:

**ALSO WHY IS SCHOOL SHOWING SPACE MATH**

Kade typed:

**Long story.**

Darius replied:

**YOU SAY THAT TOO MUCH**

True.

Kade put the tablet away.

The countdown continued.

A year ago, Kade would have looked for an exit.

Not necessarily a physical one.

A joke.

An excuse.

A reason none of this was really his problem.

Now he looked around.

Mara.

Eli.

Sera.

Julian.

Taren at the edge of the group.

Sen on the platform.

His mother eight hundred miles away.

Darius at Northline.

Aaron beneath the medical wing, alive and complicated and not yet forgiven.

People who made the system harder.

People who made leaving more expensive.

People he would not trade for simplicity.

Mara stepped closer.

Their shoulders touched.

Neither moved away.

“So,” she said. “First day.”

Kade looked at the countdown.

Then at his friends.

“Could be worse.”

Eli stared at him.

“Something in space just sent us a one-year countdown.”

Kade nodded.

“Fair point.”

The numbers kept falling.

And for once, Kade Mercer did not care where the exit was.

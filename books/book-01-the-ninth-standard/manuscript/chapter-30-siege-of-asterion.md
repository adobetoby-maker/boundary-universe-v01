# Chapter 30 — Siege of Asterion

The first thing Kade learned about real violence was that it did not wait for an arena signal.

The second was that nobody looked heroic while running from falling glass.

Asterion’s eastern transit spine went dark at 03:12.

Kade was awake because sleep had become theoretical.

Eli was still technically dead to most of campus. Taren was missing. Directorate personnel were moving beneath the island. Sen had ordered staged evacuation under the cover of storm damage.

At 03:14, every display in Meridian House flashed amber.

**INFRASTRUCTURE EMERGENCY — SHELTER PREPARATION**

Kade was already dressed.

Room 814 looked wrong with Eli physically absent but alive somewhere beneath them. The blue seven-day strip remained tied to the bedpost because removing it before the House understood the truth would have created questions they could not answer.

Kade stared at it for half a second.

Alive and missing.

Apparently those states could coexist too.

At 03:15, amber changed to red.

**SECURITY EVENT — EASTERN CAMPUS**

Then the power failed.

For two seconds Meridian went black.

Emergency lights came on low and red.

Somewhere outside, glass broke.

Students began shouting.

Sera’s voice cut through the corridor.

“Meridian, evacuation assignments. Move.”

No hesitation.

No rank check.

No question about who was supposed to be in charge.

She had spent months memorizing routes nobody cared about.

Now everyone cared.

Kade reached the common room as Julian Cross entered from the east stair wearing training clothes and one shoe.

Rank One looked less impressive with wet hair and a missing shoe.

He also looked completely calm.

“House Sol’s lower transit is locked,” Julian said. “Students are crossing through Meridian to north shelter.”

Sera pulled up an emergency map.

The display unfolded across the common-room wall at a scale large enough for frightened people to understand.

Kade had seen the same map during orientation, drills, and the night he had nearly left. Under normal conditions, Asterion was organized to look inevitable. The crescent of the island curved around its western harbor. Towers occupied the northern arc. Training arenas spread across the broad southern terraces. The Spire rose at the center, with academic buildings stepping away from it in glass bridges and planted courtyards.

Meridian House stood on the eastern shoulder of that arrangement, high enough to see the sea beyond the landing complex and close enough to Central Bridge that students treated it as the beginning of everything.

The emergency map showed the less flattering version.

The island had three movement bands, and all three depended on crossing points.

The lower transit ring carried most students beneath the open courtyards. The academic level connected the Houses to the Spire through enclosed bridges. The service level threaded through the island's foundations and was not supposed to matter to anyone outside maintenance.

Tonight the lower ring glowed black from the southeast arenas to Meridian.

The service level pulsed with red intrusion markers.

That left the academic bridges.

House Sol occupied the southeast terrace above the largest arena complex. Its students normally reached the north residential towers by riding the lower ring under Meridian, changing at the Spire, and continuing beneath the eastern academic blocks. With lower transit locked, nearly three hundred people had surfaced into Meridian courtyard at once.

The nearest hardened shelter sat under the northern towers, on the opposite side of the Spire.

Between them lay two usable paths.

Central Bridge was broad and direct. It was also already carrying Meridian's upper floors, two academic buildings, and everyone who had evacuated before being assigned a route.

The west academic bridge curved around the Spire through the older classroom blocks. Longer. Narrower. Protected from the ocean by storm shutters that had sealed automatically when the weather alert began.

Kade saw the problem before Sera spoke.

The map did not show buildings anymore.

It showed a crowd being poured into a shape with one exit.

“Primary north corridor will bottleneck in eight minutes.”

Julian studied it once.

“Use the west academic bridge.”

“Storm shutters are closed.”

“Can Navarro open them?”

Sera enlarged the western route.

Three shutter symbols crossed it in red. Behind those shutters, the bridge remained structurally green. Beyond it, the western academic court connected to a sheltered passage beneath the library and then north under the tower foundations.

“Opening all three at once puts the bridge against the storm,” she said.

Julian looked toward the windows. Rain moved sideways across Meridian courtyard. “Open the inner two. Keep the sea-facing wall closed.”

“That makes the west turn single-file.”

“Two lines,” Kade said.

They both looked at him.

Kade pointed to a maintenance recess just past the second shutter. He knew it because he had once hidden there to avoid an orientation group.

“There is a service bay behind the wall. Open that panel and the turn gets wider.”

Sera checked the plan.

The bay did not appear on the student layer.

Eli's voice arrived in Kade's ear.

“He is right. Annoyingly.”

Kade put the private channel through the wall display without identifying its source.

“Can you give Sera the maintenance layer?”

“Already trying.”

The map flickered.

Old corridors appeared in gray beneath the school.

For one bad second, red personnel markers moved through them like blood under skin.

Then Eli stripped the sublevel view away and left only the service bay.

Julian had seen the markers.

So had Sera.

Neither asked the question now.

Julian traced the reroute with one finger.

“Sol crosses Meridian courtyard, west bridge, library underpass, north shelter. Meridian floors three through five follow. Floors six and seven take Central Bridge before it reaches hard capacity.”

Sera corrected him.

“Sol does not cross the courtyard as one group. Split at the south gate. Half enter through dining, half through the training floor. They merge after the west stair.”

“Why?”

“Because three hundred frightened people will follow whichever person they can see. Give them two doors before they decide there is only one.”

Julian considered the common room, the two interior corridors, and the incoming flow.

“Agreed.”

No rank contest.

No argument about which House owned the route.

They were not deciding who led.

They were deciding where panic would go.

Every student within earshot looked at Kade.

Eli’s public status was still dead.

Kade said, “Long story.”

Then Eli’s voice came through Kade’s private comm.

“I can open them.”

Sera did not react to the impossible voice because Sera had already reacted enough for one night.

“Do it.”

A second later the west bridge icon changed from red to green.

Only the inner two shutter symbols changed.

The sea-facing wall stayed locked.

Eli said privately, “The service-bay motor is not answering.”

“Can you make it answer?”

“Eventually.”

“We have eight minutes.”

“Then eventually has become seven.”

Julian nodded as if dead roommates reopening infrastructure was ordinary.

“Meridian teams one through four, evacuation. Five and six, med support. Nobody engages unless necessary.”

Sera looked at him.

“You sound annoyingly competent.”

Julian nodded.

“I practice.”

The building shook.

Dust fell from the ceiling.

Students froze.

Julian did not.

“Move.”

They moved.

---

House Meridian had practiced evacuation twice that semester.

Both times students complained.

The first drill interrupted breakfast.

The second interrupted an arena semifinal.

Taren had called the route cards decorative anxiety.

Eli had modified his because the printed map failed to account for a service stair.

Sera had timed every floor.

Nobody complained now.

The House divided almost automatically.

Priya took the seventh-floor students and moved them down the west stair.

Anton and Nessa opened the lower common rooms as temporary shelter staging.

Two fourth-years turned the dining hall into a triage station before medical staff arrived.

Someone pulled old House Trial barriers from storage and mounted them across the glass doors facing the courtyard.

The same equipment students had used to score points two months earlier now caught broken window panels.

Kade watched a first-year he barely knew lift one end of a fallen cabinet while another student redirected the weight just enough to free a trapped ankle.

No audience.

No points.

No one asked their ranks.

The school story and the emergency story turned out to be the same story with different consequences.

Kade hated how much that mattered to him.

Sera moved through the common room with a response tablet in one hand.

“North shelter capacity?”

Nessa answered, “Seventy-eight percent.”

“West academic?”

“Forty-one.”

“Send floors three through five west. Keep six and seven north.”

A second-year asked, “Who authorized that?”

Sera looked at him.

“The building.”

He blinked.

She pointed at the load map.

“North bridge is taking too many people. Move.”

He moved.

Kade almost smiled.

Sera’s eyes cut toward him.

“Do not.”

“Nothing.”

“You have that face.”

Apparently Darius had spread.

---

The campus Kade had spent months learning became a different system.

Dining halls became triage stations.

Classrooms became shelter zones.

Training corridors became controlled movement lanes.

Arena barrier panels became blast shields.

The ranking displays vanished. In their place appeared evacuation routes, structural warnings, and medical capacity.

Nobody cared who was #211.

Kade found that strangely comforting.

He moved with Mara toward the east academic bridge while Sera directed students west.

“You should stay with evacuation,” Kade told Sera.

She gave him a look.

“That is what I am doing.”

“Then why are you following us?”

“Because the next shelter route passes the engineering junction, and if someone is attacking the island from below, that is where they will cut it.”

Kade remembered her talking about Disaster Response.

Forty people out of a collapsed building.

No one lost.

This was not a future ambition anymore.

This was practice with consequences.

At the west bridge, hundreds of students moved under emergency lights.

Some were crying.

Some were joking too loudly.

One boy carried a terrarium with a lizard inside because apparently evacuation priorities were personal.

A faculty member tried to make him leave it.

Sera said, “If he can carry it and keep pace, let him.”

The faculty member stared.

Sera had already moved on.

The west academic bridge began behind a set of fire doors Kade had passed hundreds of times without noticing.

Tonight the doors stood open and the corridor beyond them had become the most important forty meters on the island.

The bridge curved away from Meridian in a glass-sided arc. On the right, the sealed storm wall hid the Pacific. Rain hammered the shutters hard enough to make the metal flex. On the left, windows faced the dark center of Asterion: the Spire above, old academic roofs below, Central Bridge crowded with moving students between them.

The map had made the western route look like a line.

In person it had width, corners, frightened children, and a service panel that refused to move.

The first shutter had opened fully.

The second had stopped halfway, narrowing the bridge to a gap barely wide enough for two people.

The maintenance bay beyond it remained closed.

House Sol students pressed forward from behind. Meridian students arrived from the west stair. At the far end of the bridge, a faculty marshal waved both groups toward the same bottleneck.

Sera saw it and climbed onto a bench.

“Stop the Meridian line.”

Her voice did not need amplification.

Priya caught the order and turned sideways across the west stair with both arms raised.

“Hold here. Leave the center clear.”

Complaints started.

Then the building shook again.

Complaints stopped.

Sera pointed to the Sol students.

“You move through in pairs. No bags in your hands. Put straps over both shoulders or leave the bag.”

A third-year near the front looked toward the pressure of people behind him.

“They are not going to wait.”

“Then make the person behind you wait.”

He turned.

“Pairs only,” he shouted. “Back up.”

Nothing happened.

Julian stepped onto the opposite bench.

House Sol saw him.

Recognition moved through the crowd faster than instruction had.

Kade expected Julian to use it.

Instead Julian pointed at the third-year.

“He gave you an order.”

The crowd backed up.

The third-year stared at Julian.

Julian said, quieter, “Keep giving them.”

Then he jumped down.

Kade felt something in his estimation of Rank One adjust again.

Mara reached the jammed shutter and put one hand against its frame.

“The upper guide is twisted.”

Kade looked at the metal.

He saw nothing.

Mara looked through it anyway, reading the force path by touch.

“Motor pulled before the lower lock released. If Eli runs it again, it will shear.”

Eli said through Kade's comm, “I object to being discussed as if I cannot hear.”

“Can you release the lower lock?” Mara asked.

“No signal path.”

“Manual access?”

Kade found the hatch near the floor.

It required a maintenance key.

Of course it did.

He looked through the glass toward the crowd. An instructor on the Sol side was trying to move a student in a mobility chair through the narrowing gap while everyone else squeezed past.

The chair caught once.

The student flinched as bodies pressed around her.

Kade put his fingers against the hatch edge.

Not Boundary architecture.

Just a lock plate, four bolts, and the remembered feel of vector loading.

“Move back,” he told Mara.

He loaded the plate sideways.

Metal protested.

The latch tore free.

His shoulder lit with pain, ordinary and immediate.

The hatch fell open.

Mara crouched beside him.

Inside, a red lever sat behind two moving cables.

“That one,” Eli said.

“You cannot see it.”

“I have the maintenance specification.”

“The specification that failed to mention the service bay?”

“Asterion contains many disappointments.”

Kade reached toward the lever.

Mara caught his wrist.

“The cable is loaded.”

He stopped.

She studied the mechanism.

“When I lift the guide, the tension moves to the lower mount. Pull once. Do not hold it.”

“That sounds reassuring.”

“It was not intended to.”

Mara braced one hand under the warped guide.

Her field gathered with almost no visible light.

The metal shifted three millimeters.

Kade pulled.

The lever snapped down.

The shutter released so abruptly that Julian had to catch its edge before it struck a passing student.

He held two hundred kilograms of storm panel long enough for Mara to redirect the weight into the wall track.

Then the shutter rose.

The bridge opened to full width.

The student in the chair crossed first.

Sera did not tell anyone to applaud.

They did not.

She widened the flow from pairs to three across, left one lane empty for medical movement, and sent Meridian's waiting floors after Sol without allowing the groups to collide.

Kade stood near the torn access hatch, breathing hard.

On Central Bridge, the moving crowd had slowed.

From here he could see why.

A training drone hovered above the central court with no instructor beside it.

Its status lights were dark.

It rotated once toward the bridge.

Then drifted away.

Kade watched until it disappeared behind the Spire.

“That drone is wrong.”

Mara followed his gaze.

“It is not assigned to emergency support.”

Sera heard them.

“Tell Eli.”

Kade did.

There was a pause.

“I cannot see the training network,” Eli said. “It went local-only six minutes ago.”

The map in Kade's head changed.

Not only locked doors below.

Weapons above.

Sera looked at the last Sol group entering the bridge.

“We clear the academic level before those drones receive instructions.”

“What if they already have?” Kade asked.

Sera looked toward the Spire.

“Then we make sure they find empty corridors.”

Kade looked at Mara.

“Response is going to love her.”

“They already do.”

“How do you know?”

“I read.”

“Of course.”

The first Directorate operative they saw wore academy maintenance gray.

That frightened Kade more than a military uniform would have.

The man stepped from a service alcove and fired a compressed field pulse down the corridor.

No warning.

No bell.

No controlled-contact rule.

Mara moved before Kade understood what was happening.

She redirected the pulse upward.

Ceiling panels burst.

Sera shoved two younger students behind a structural column.

Julian crossed the corridor in three steps and forced the operative’s next line away from the crowd.

Kade felt the whole space sharpen.

Wall.

Floor.

Supports.

People.

Transfer paths waiting to become dangerous.

The cold line threatened behind his eyes.

He did not reach for Boundary architecture.

Not in a corridor full of students.

Instead he used the ordinary things.

Timing.

Angles.

A fire door.

He saw the operative retreat toward a service junction.

“Julian, left.”

Julian trusted the call.

Mara moved right without being asked.

The operative found his retreat cut off and surrendered when Sera arrived behind him with three Response Corps trainees and a restraint field.

The whole encounter lasted twenty seconds.

Kade’s hands shook afterward.

Not Boundary strain.

Fear.

Mara saw.

“So do mine,” she said.

Kade looked.

They did.

That helped more than reassurance would have.

The detained operative looked at Kade and smiled.

Not pleasantly.

“You have no idea what you are protecting.”

Kade’s fear sharpened into anger.

“Apparently neither do you, or you would not be breaking into your own school.”

The operative’s smile disappeared.

Sera handed him to campus security.

No one made a speech.

There were too many doors left to secure.

---

The next hour tried to fracture into tasks.

Sera refused to let it.

She made every team report three things in the same order: people, route, failure.

How many people were with you.

Which route you were using.

What would make that route stop working.

At 03:27, a faculty team reported forty-six students moving through the east academic bridge toward Meridian.

Route: engineering junction to east court.

Failure: a cracked lower support beneath the bridge.

The warning reached Sera while she was transferring the detained operative to campus security.

She looked at the island map.

The east bridge formed the last connection between the engineering blocks and Meridian now that transit was gone. If she closed it, forty-six students and three staff would be stranded on the wrong side of a corridor where an operative had just fired into a crowd.

If she kept it open, the support might drop them into the lower court.

“How cracked?” she asked.

The faculty voice answered through static. “Visible separation on the south mount. Structural monitor says sixteen percent loss.”

Mara was already moving.

“I need to see it.”

Julian looked toward the detained operative, the remaining students, and the route they had just secured.

“I will hold the junction.”

Sera pointed at two Response trainees.

“With him. Nobody follows us east until I reopen the route.”

Then she ran.

Kade followed because nobody had told him not to quickly enough.

The bridge support had not cracked.

It had pulled six centimeters out of the wall.

The enclosed walkway sagged above the engineering court, its floor canted toward the glass. Students waited at the far end beneath green exit lights. Several had sat down. One instructor stood in front of them with both hands raised, speaking too calmly.

Below the bridge, rain blew through the open court and collected around a sculpture no one had ever been able to explain.

The south mount made a deep metallic sound.

Everyone heard it.

The students at the far end became one body trying to stand.

“Keep them down,” Sera ordered over comms.

The instructor repeated it.

This time calm was not enough.

Kade saw the crowd compress toward the doors behind them.

Another bad shape.

“Give them something to do,” he said.

Sera looked at him once, understood, and pointed through the glass.

“Engineering side, count off by six. First person in each group stands. Everyone else stays seated until your number is called.”

The instructor began counting.

Students answered.

Not all of them.

Enough.

The crowd became six smaller groups instead of one frightened thing.

Mara lay flat beside the damaged mount and pressed both palms to the floor.

The bridge trembled beneath her.

“The north support is taking the extra load,” she said. “It will hold people if they stay spread. It will not hold a rush.”

Sera crouched beside her.

“Can we move them?”

“Six at a time.”

“Can you stabilize it?”

Mara closed her eyes.

The answer took four seconds.

“I can keep the damaged mount from moving farther. I cannot carry the bridge.”

Limit.

Not lack of courage.

Not lack of skill.

Physics refusing negotiation.

Sera opened the first group’s channel.

“When your instructor says go, walk on the center line. Do not run. Do not touch the glass. At this end, turn left and keep moving until you reach the blue doors.”

A student asked, “What if it moves?”

Sera looked at Mara.

Mara's jaw had tightened with strain.

“It will move,” Sera said. “You will keep walking.”

The first six crossed.

The floor dipped under the fourth student.

Mara made a sound through her teeth.

The mount stopped pulling.

The students kept walking.

Kade received them at the Meridian end and sent them left.

Second six.

Third.

By the fourth group, Kade knew which floor panel flexed and told them to step over it. By the fifth, the instructor on the engineering side no longer needed Sera's count. By the sixth, the students were helping each other maintain the spacing.

The last group included the instructor.

He crossed backward, watching the support instead of the exit.

“Turn around,” Sera called.

“I can see them from here.”

“They are safe. Turn around.”

The mount tore another centimeter free.

Mara's left hand slipped.

Kade felt the bridge begin to become one object in his mind.

Deck.

Glass.

Mounts.

People.

The cold line appeared behind his eyes.

He could define the walkway against the buildings.

He could tell the bridge to keep belonging to both sides.

And if he reached wrong, every person on it would become part of the thing he tried to hold.

Mara looked at him.

She knew exactly what he was considering.

“Do not.”

The instructor turned and ran the final four steps.

As his shoes crossed the expansion joint, Mara released her field.

The damaged end dropped.

The bridge fell half a meter and stopped against an emergency catch beneath the wall.

Glass burst outward into the empty court.

The route turned red on Sera's map.

No one was on it.

Sera counted the evacuees herself.

Forty-six students.

Three staff.

“People?” Kade asked.

She looked at him.

He had used her order.

“Forty-nine safe.”

“Route?”

“Closed.”

“Failure?”

Sera looked through the broken glass at the bridge hanging crooked above the court.

“Already happened.”

That was one way to survive a failure condition.

They returned to the engineering junction by the interior lab corridor.

Julian had turned it into a checkpoint.

The Directorate operative sat behind two restraint fields under the supervision of campus security. Sol students moved west in groups of twenty. A pair of fourth-years checked every maintenance uniform against a live faculty roster.

Nobody had told Julian how to build the checkpoint.

He had borrowed laboratory tables for cover, left the center open for evacuation, and stationed the strongest students where they could reinforce walls instead of meeting an attack head-on.

Arena strategy with the audience removed.

He handed Sera a tablet.

“North shelter is accepting another hundred. West shelter lost backup power. The medical team wants the Meridian dining hall because it has an independent circuit.”

Three needs.

Three different routes.

Sera studied the map.

Kade saw the collision first.

“If medical comes through Central Bridge, they hit the last Meridian floors going north.”

Julian pointed to the lower ring.

“Locked.”

“Not all of it.”

Eli had shown them the old maintenance layer.

Kade enlarged the eastern section. A short service passage connected the landing-complex clinic to Meridian's kitchen receiving area without joining lower transit.

The passage looked gray rather than red.

Unknown, not compromised.

Sera asked, “Can your source open it?”

Kade switched to the private channel.

“Eli?”

“I heard.”

“Can you?”

“The receiving door uses a local mechanical interlock.”

“That sounds like no.”

“It sounds like Navarro gets to leave the underground room everyone believes contains his corpse.”

Kade stopped.

“Where are you?”

“South service junction. I can reach the interlock in four minutes.”

“You are supposed to be medically cleared for walking.”

“This is walking with urgency.”

“That is called running.”

“Clinically disputed.”

The connection clicked as Eli moved.

Julian watched Kade's face.

“Navarro?”

The lie had outlived its usefulness among the people in this corridor.

Kade nodded.

Julian exhaled once.

Not surprise.

Relief he did not have time to express.

“Tell him to open the route.”

“Already doing it.”

“Of course he is.”

Four minutes later, the receiving door opened.

Medics brought the first stretchers through Meridian's kitchen while cooks shoved preparation tables against the walls. The smell of disinfectant mixed with bread left from the night shift. House dinner tables were pushed together beneath the common-room windows to make treatment stations.

The building changed use room by room.

At 03:42, Kade crossed Meridian Commons again.

The ranking board where he had once stared at 10,482 showed blood supply and shelter capacity. Each incoming patient changed the numbers.

An injured first-year lay on the table where Eli had once complained about potatoes.

Sera pressed a seal against the student's arm and kept talking calmly while a medic worked.

“North shelter has room for sixty-two more,” she told Julian. “West academic remains open but we need the center lane kept clear. East bridge is closed. Medical enters through receiving.”

Julian updated the route board.

“House Sol reports one student unaccounted for.”

Sera's hands did not pause on the seal.

“Last confirmed location?”

“South gate during the split.”

“Name?”

Julian gave it.

Sera put the name on the board under **SEARCH**, not **MISSING**.

The distinction mattered.

Kade stopped beside the table.

Sera looked up.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

She gave him the exact look she had given him months ago.

“Careful.”

He almost laughed.

Some patterns survived everything.

Then the lights over the treatment stations dimmed.

Every medic looked up.

The independent circuit had not failed.

Something had taken priority over it.

Eli's voice came through Kade's comm, breathless now.

“I have temporarily become infrastructure.”

“Explain.”

“The automatic controller is diverting Meridian's backup power toward lower transit. I am holding the kitchen circuit open locally.”

Kade pictured Eli somewhere inside a service cabinet, injured, recently dead, and arguing with a building.

“You were already emotionally infrastructure.”

There was a pause.

“Was that nice?” Eli asked.

“Emergency conditions.”

“Understood.”

The lights steadied.

Kade opened the service map.

Doors changed state as Eli reached them.

One camera loop went dark.

Another came back with its archive access removed.

Power rerouted around the compromised lower ring in short segments rather than one vulnerable line.

Eli was not controlling the whole island.

He was making hundreds of small choices faster than the people below could reverse them.

Open this door because twelve students waited behind it.

Close that one because a red marker approached.

Keep the dining hall lit.

Let an empty corridor go black.

The campus above and the hidden infrastructure below stopped being separate maps.

For the first time, Kade understood what Eli had been trying to show them in Lab Six.

Old systems did not remain harmless because everyone had forgotten them.

They waited for someone to remember first.

At 04:07, the attack changed.

Every compromised system on campus activated at once.

Training drones lifted from storage.

Lower transit locked.

Power to the Spire flickered.

The ground beneath central Asterion produced a vibration Kade felt through his shoes.

Eli’s voice sharpened.

“They reached precursor control.”

Then Sen’s message arrived on every secure channel.

**SUBLEVEL NINE BREACHED. CONTAINMENT AT RISK.**

Sera stared at Kade.

“What is in Sublevel Nine?”

A second message followed.

**THE REASON ASTERION EXISTS.**

Julian read it once.

“Of course it is.”

Kade laughed despite himself.

The laugh lasted less than a second.

The central access to legacy sublevels lay beneath the oldest administration wing, west of the Spire. Their current position was east-central campus. Under normal conditions it was a seven-minute transit ride.

Transit was gone.

Central Bridge offered the shortest walking route.

It also showed six moving drone markers.

The lower ring was black. The east academic bridge hung broken over its court. The west academic route remained open, but it curved north of the Spire before returning south through the old lecture halls.

Sera studied the three bad choices.

The ranking board behind her updated north-shelter capacity again.

“West,” she said. “Central is exposed. The last evacuation group clears the west bridge in two minutes.”

Julian looked toward Priya.

“You have Meridian?”

Priya glanced at the treatment stations, route board, and security teams.

“I have the building. Nessa has shelter movement. Anton has triage supply.”

Sera gave her the response tablet.

Priya did not take it immediately.

“Where are you going?”

“To keep the reason Asterion exists from leaving through the floor.”

Priya looked as though she wanted a less alarming answer.

“Will you be reachable?”

Sera checked the sublevel map.

“Probably not.”

“Then give me your failure condition.”

Sera looked at her.

Priya held out her hand for the tablet.

People.

Route.

Failure.

Sera gave it to her.

“If Central Bridge loses structural green, move everyone inside Meridian and the north towers. Do not reroute south. If lower-transit doors open without Sen's authorization, seal the interior stairs.”

Priya took the tablet.

“And if you do not come back?”

Sera's expression did not change.

“The plan remains good.”

Priya hated that.

Kade could tell because he hated it too.

She nodded anyway.

Authority moved without ceremony.

The four of them left Meridian through the west stair: Sera first, Mara beside Kade, Julian behind them long enough to make sure nobody followed.

The bridge was nearly empty now.

Rain still hammered the sealed outer wall. Emergency arrows ran blue along the floor toward the north shelter. On the far side, two faculty marshals escorted the final nine students into the library underpass.

Sera checked the count.

“That is the last group.”

Above the inner windows, the Spire lights flickered again.

One dark floor at its base became three.

Then a training drone rose into view.

It came from below the bridge with its field vanes open.

Mara saw the load build first.

“Down.”

The pulse struck the glass where Kade's head had been.

Safety laminate whitened but held.

The faculty marshals pushed the last students through the far doors.

A second drone appeared above the courtyard.

Then a third.

They did not attack the people nearest them.

They took positions around the bridge supports.

Kade understood.

“They are closing the route.”

Julian looked through the glass at the drone below.

“By dropping it?”

“Efficient.”

The lower drone fired into the north mounting point.

The floor kicked under them.

Sera glanced toward the last students. The far doors had not closed. One student had stopped in the threshold to look back.

“Keep moving,” she shouted.

The student moved.

The drone above charged again.

Mara could redirect one pulse.

Not three.

Julian saw that too.

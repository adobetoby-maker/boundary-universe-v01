# Chapter 4 — Ten Thousand Four Hundred Eighty-Two

At six fifteen the next morning, Kade discovered that Asterion believed breakfast was something people earned after needles.

He sat on an examination table in the intake clinic while a technician filled four vials with his blood and explained, with offensive cheerfulness, that the academy’s baseline protocol was “comprehensive.”

“That word keeps finding me,” Kade said.

The technician removed the needle and pressed a seal over the puncture.

“You’re the Zero candidate, right?”

Kade looked at her.

She looked at his wrist band.

“I’m going to pretend that was a medical question.”

“Sorry.”

“You’re not.”

“No.”

At least she was honest.

By six forty-five, they had measured nearly everything medical, sensory, or mechanical about him: blood chemistry, breathing, hearing, balance, reflexes, joint stability, sleep debt, reaction time, and Conduit compatibility. Several remaining tests seemed designed solely to justify expensive equipment.

He received a protein bar after the third machine.

Apparently breakfast could be earned incrementally.

At six fifty-eight, a training aide escorted him underground.

The elevator descended long enough that Kade stopped pretending it was taking them to a basement.

“How far?” he asked.

“Forty-two meters.”

“Why?”

“Field containment.”

“Comforting answer.”

The aide did not respond.

The doors opened onto a corridor of dark composite panels and glass thick enough to make Kade wonder what the academy expected to hit it.

Every few meters, a wall symbol showed concentric circles crossed by a diagonal line.

**RESONANCE TESTING — CONTROLLED ACCESS**

This part of Asterion did not appear in recruitment videos.

No marble.

No ocean views.

No carefully chosen students laughing on green lawns.

Just sealed doors, quiet corridors, embedded sensors, and people carrying tablets without looking at one another.

The aide delivered Kade to Dr. Lian Park.

Park was small, silver-haired, and moved like someone who had decided years ago that wasted motion was a personal insult.

“Shoes off,” she said.

Kade looked down at his shoes.

“Did they fail a background check?”

“Conductive floor.”

He removed them.

“Shirt too.”

He paused.

Park looked at him over the rim of her glasses.

“Medical sensors, Mr. Mercer. I have no interest in your fashion decisions.”

Kade took off the shirt.

Thirty adhesive contacts later, he stood in the center of a circular room twenty meters across.

The floor beneath his bare feet was marked with concentric rings. The walls were glass. Behind them sat a control room containing six technicians, two banks of equipment, and one person Kade recognized immediately.

Dr. Amara Sen.

Director of Asterion Academy.

One of eleven publicly known Standard Eight Resonants alive.

She had appeared on magazine covers, disaster footage, tournament broadcasts, and one government campaign encouraging teenagers not to modify their own Conduits.

In person, she looked less impressive.

That was almost more intimidating.

No ceremonial uniform.

No medals.

Just a dark jacket over a plain shirt and a cup of tea in one hand.

She watched him through the glass.

Kade lifted one hand.

Sen lifted hers back.

Park said, “Try not to entertain the director.”

“Wasn’t planning to.”

“You were absolutely planning to.”

Kade decided he liked Park.

A speaker clicked on.

“Baseline stage one,” Park said. “Passive perception.”

A metal sphere rose from the floor ten meters away.

“Tell me the instant you perceive any change not attributable to normal senses.”

Kade waited.

Nothing.

The sphere began vibrating.

Still nothing.

Lights around the room dimmed.

The vibration increased until he could hear it.

“Anything?”

“My left foot is cold.”

Park checked a monitor.

“That is unrelated.”

“Then no.”

The sphere stopped.

A second apparatus rose.

Two blocks of black material floated in a rigid frame with empty air between them. The space shimmered faintly.

Kade watched.

Nothing.

A pressure developed in his ears.

Not field perception.

Just pressure.

“Nothing.”

The equipment cycled through gravity, electromagnetic force, kinetic storage, heat transfer, and structural cohesion. Each time Park asked the same kind of question, and each time Kade gave the same answer: nothing.

At first the technicians watched every screen.

Then one started checking another workstation between trials.

Another whispered to the person beside him.

Kade knew that process.

He had watched it happen in classrooms.

At first, adults expected something interesting.

Then he disappointed them.

Then they looked at the clock.

Park did not look at the clock.

Sen did not move.

That helped less than he expected.

“Again,” Park said.

Kade frowned.

“The last three were identical.”

“Yes.”

“Is this where science becomes persistence?”

“This is where science confirms identical results are actually identical.”

“Less poetic.”

“More useful.”

The ninth field came on.

Nothing.

Park recorded it.

Then she said, “Control pass.”

The inner door opened before Kade could ask what that meant. One of the technicians entered carrying a narrow case and a yellow floor marker. She had been behind the glass all morning, the one who kept returning to the same screen even after everybody else’s attention began to wander.

She set the marker at the fourth ring.

Park lowered a clear partition between Kade and the other half of the room.

“You are not being tested now,” Park said. “The equipment is.”

“That distinction seems designed to protect the equipment.”

“Stand still.”

The technician opened the case. Inside lay six ceramic tiles, each smaller than Kade’s palm. She arranged them in a line on the conductive floor, stepped back, and touched two fingers to the silver plate at her wrist.

The first tile rose.

No vibration. No arm beneath it. It simply lifted ten centimeters and stayed there.

The second followed. Then the third.

Behind the glass, all six monitoring stations drew clean green curves.

Kade kept his attention on the tiles.

Park’s voice came through the speaker. “Report anything beyond visual observation.”

He waited for pressure. Tingling. Heat. The sense of a line moving through space.

Nothing arrived.

The technician rotated her wrist. Three tiles slid apart while the others remained fixed, a precise fan opening across the ring.

Kade could see the result.

He could see her tendons tighten and the pale indicator along her Conduit brighten. He could hear one of the tiles scrape when it settled too close to the floor.

The action between those things remained absent.

“Nothing,” he said.

Park changed the test.

The room lights went out.

For two seconds, the only illumination came from the technician’s wrist and the green lines behind the glass.

“Now?” Park asked.

Kade could no longer see the tiles.

One clicked softly against another.

He pointed toward the sound.

“Two moved. Maybe three.”

The lights returned.

All six tiles were suspended in a vertical column on the opposite side of the partition.

The technician lowered them.

Her display read **STANDARD I — FORCE TRANSFER**.

Not spectacular. Not a student throwing a vehicle or crossing a courtyard in one leap. A woman moving six pieces of ceramic a few meters under perfect control.

The monitors had seen all of it.

Kade had seen only what ordinary senses left behind.

The technician packed the tiles away. She did not look bored now. That should have helped.

It did not.

Park raised the partition.

“Control response is within tolerance,” she said.

“Congratulations to the room.”

“The room was never in doubt.”

“Good for it.”

Park studied him through the glass.

“The control pass matters, Mr. Mercer. A null result from a broken sensor is not information. A null result from a functioning sensor is.”

Kade looked at the empty floor where the tiles had floated.

“So we have proven I am definitely not doing something.”

“We have proven the tests can distinguish absence from equipment failure.”

“You found a longer version.”

“I found the accurate one.”

She waited until he looked back at her.

“Accuracy is not always comfort.”

He almost told her he had not asked for comfort.

That would have been a lie, and Park seemed unusually well equipped to measure those.

“Stage two.”

A waist-high table rose in front of Kade. On it sat a one-kilogram steel puck.

“Move it,” Park said.

“With what?”

“Resonance.”

“That clarification did not improve the instructions.”

“However feels natural.”

“Nothing about this feels natural.”

Park waited.

Kade stared at the puck. It continued being a puck.

He concentrated, imagined pushing it, pictured it sliding, and ran through acceleration, friction, weight—every physical thing that should matter. Nothing changed.

He thought about the examination.

A sphere hanging in a chamber.

The floor beneath the chamber.

The foundation beneath the floor.

A line he had drawn through objects that were supposed to be whole.

Something tingled at the base of his skull.

The puck moved.

Kade’s heart jumped.

Then a technician opened the testing-room door.

Air pressure had shifted the puck half a centimeter.

Kade stared at it.

Park said, “Environmental artifact.”

“Of course it was.”

They reset and changed the object. A training bead designed for first-time Resonants stayed still. A suspended ribbon sensitive to microscopic field interaction stayed still too.

By nine thirty, Kade’s official output remained zero.

Not `000`.

Actual zero.

The distinction did not improve his mood.

He sat on the floor while Park reviewed the data.

Through the glass, a technician whispered to another.

Kade could not hear the words.

He did not need to.

Their faces were familiar.

Park came into the room and handed him a bottle of water.

“You are dehydrated.”

“I’m fascinating.”

“You are mildly dehydrated.”

“Put it on the recruitment poster.”

She sat on the floor across from him.

That surprised him.

“You expected more,” she said.

“No.”

Park waited.

Kade unscrewed the bottle.

“I expected something.”

“That is different.”

He drank.

The water tasted faintly metallic.

“Does Zero Protocol ever select people with no Resonance?”

Park’s expression changed so little that someone else might have missed it.

“Above my clearance.”

“You run the test.”

“I run today’s tests.”

“What happened thirty-one years ago?”

Park’s eyes sharpened.

Kade watched her.

“You know.”

“I know your questions have become less random.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No.”

She stood.

“Stage three.”

Kade sighed.

“What’s stage three?”

“Stress.”

“Finally. Something I’ve trained for.”

Before the next test, they gave him fifteen minutes and breakfast.

A real breakfast.

Eggs, toast, fruit, yogurt, and a bowl of oatmeal the size of a structural component.

Kade ate in a small observation room with a glass wall looking into the testing chamber.

A boy about his age occupied the other end of the table.

He wore a Standard Three candidate band.

The boy glanced at Kade’s provisional lanyard.

Then his face.

“You’re him.”

Kade pointed to the oatmeal.

“I’m eating.”

“Zero.”

“Less specific.”

The boy laughed.

“Mateo Ruiz. Amplification.”

“You say that like a last name.”

“At Asterion it basically is.”

Mateo held up one hand. A small silver Conduit plate curved along his wrist.

“Family knew when I was fourteen. Spent three years in a feeder program.”

Kade looked at the device.

“Three years.”

“Yep.”

“And you’re still doing baseline.”

“Everyone does baseline.”

“Why?”

Mateo shrugged.

“Because half the people who arrive thinking they’re amazing are wrong about what they’re amazing at.”

That sounded useful enough to be suspicious.

Kade said, “What rank?”

“Preliminary? Four seventy-two.”

Kade paused with the spoon halfway to his mouth.

Mateo noticed.

“Rankings are stupid for the first month.”

“You’re number four hundred seventy-two out of ten thousand and calling them stupid.”

“Yes.”

“Convincing.”

Mateo smiled.

“Last year a guy baselined thirty-seven and finished eight hundred. Another started nine thousand and ended two hundred.”

“Still sounds like numbers.”

“They’re numbers with time attached.”

Kade looked through the glass at the test chamber.

That phrase stayed with him.

Numbers with time attached.

Not verdicts.

Maybe.

Stage three put him on a moving platform while light, sound, acceleration, projected obstacles, and false alerts attacked his attention from six directions.

He did better.

Not because of Resonance.

Because chaos made sense to him.

A static classroom demanded that he hold still and accept somebody else’s framing.

A collapsing simulation gave him problems that actually needed solving.

The platform lurched.

A training arm swung at shoulder height.

Kade ducked, caught a dropped tool with his left hand, and stepped through a gap that appeared too narrow until the last instant.

The room rebuilt itself around him.

Projected walls divided the circular floor into three corridors. Smoke rolled low across all of them. At the far end, an orange rescue case flashed beneath a countdown.

**00:43**

“Retrieve the case and return to the center mark,” Park said.

“Any rules?”

“The environment supplies consequences.”

“That is not a rule.”

“Forty seconds.”

The left corridor offered the shortest route. Its floor pulsed red in a repeating pattern.

The middle looked clear.

The right contained two moving barriers and no visible exit.

Kade chose the middle.

Three steps in, the floor dropped beneath his right foot.

He caught the wall, but the wall vanished when his palm struck it. Projection. No support.

His knee hit the platform hard enough to send pain up his thigh.

A buzzer sounded.

**ROUTE FAILURE — 5 SECOND PENALTY**

“The environment is smug,” Kade said.

“Thirty-one seconds.”

He backed out instead of forcing the false corridor.

The left route was not randomly dangerous. The red pattern traveled from the outside wall toward the center, three floor panels at a time. A moving load. Predictable once he stopped treating the color as a warning and asked what it described.

Kade stepped onto the first panel after the pulse passed.

The next pulse began before he reached the safe gap.

He jumped sideways, planted one foot on the base of the projected wall, and discovered that the base was real even if the upper surface was not. The pulse crossed beneath him.

He dropped behind it and ran.

**00:19**

The orange case waited on a pedestal.

Kade grabbed the handle.

The case did not move.

**LOAD: THIRTY-EIGHT KILOGRAMS**

“You could have mentioned that.”

“The environment did.”

Small print appeared beneath the countdown.

**ASSISTED EXTRACTION POINT: CORRIDOR C**

The right corridor.

Kade looked back the way he had come. He could drag thirty-eight kilograms across the pulsing floor. Maybe. The timer would expire first.

The right corridor’s two barriers passed each other every four seconds. What had looked like a sealed route opened for less than one second between them.

He pulled the case off the pedestal and let its weight carry it onto the floor. Wheels unfolded from the base.

That part had also been in the environment.

He shoved it toward the first barrier, ran beside it, and slowed instead of chasing the first gap.

The barrier closed.

The countdown reached eight.

Kade waited.

Every instinct told him to move because time was leaving.

He waited through the next false opening too. The second barrier would have cut him off from the case.

At four seconds, both panels separated.

Kade pushed the case between them, threw himself after it, and caught the handle as it rolled onto the center ring.

The clock reached zero.

A red warning appeared.

**TIME EXPIRED**

Then a green one beneath it.

**OBJECTIVE COMPLETE — LOAD SECURE**

Kade lay on his back beside the case, breathing hard.

“That seems inconsistent.”

Park said, “The clock measured ideal response. The objective measured acceptable response.”

“So I lost correctly.”

“You rejected two unsafe openings under time pressure.”

Kade sat up.

The green line remained.

Nobody at Northline had ever put *waiting* on a scoreboard. Waiting usually meant refusing, failing, or making an adult repeat the question.

Here it had counted as judgment.

The case sank into the platform.

Before Kade finished standing, a new display flashed three route options toward a target above the far wall.

He ignored all three and used the maintenance rail.

A red warning appeared.

**UNAUTHORIZED PATH**

Then a green one.

**OBJECTIVE COMPLETE**

Kade smiled.

Park’s voice came over the speaker.

“Do not look so pleased.”

“The machine and I disagree about authority.”

“Shocking.”

His reaction metrics climbed from the seventy-third percentile to the eighty-first, then reached the ninety-sixth in adaptive route selection. The technicians became interested again.

Kade hated that he liked it.

Then the field portion resumed.

Nothing.

Every time the system measured him as quick, adaptive, spatially unusual, or good under pressure, the next Resonance metric returned to zero.

The pattern began to feel almost deliberate.

At eleven forty-seven, Dr. Sen entered the testing room.

Everyone else left.

Kade noticed that too.

He sat on the edge of the platform wearing his shirt again and pretending not to be tired.

Sen carried no tablet.

“You look disappointed,” she said.

“People keep saying that.”

“Are they wrong?”

“Yes.”

Sen smiled faintly.

“Good. Then we can skip reassurance.”

Kade looked at her.

“That might be the nicest thing anyone has said today.”

She walked to the center ring.

“Do you know why rankings exist?”

“To make teenagers emotionally stable.”

“Obviously.”

He blinked.

Sen continued before he could decide whether she had made a joke.

“Rankings make performance legible. Students understand them. Coaches understand them. Sponsors understand them. Governments especially understand them.”

“That sounded critical.”

“It was observational.”

“Those are often the same thing.”

Sen looked pleased.

“Your baseline data is being entered now.”

Kade’s stomach tightened.

“I’m not enrolled.”

“Provisional students are ranked.”

“Why?”

“Because everyone is.”

“That is a terrible reason.”

“It is also the true one.”

A wall display illuminated behind her.

Kade’s profile appeared.

**MERCER, KADE A.**

**YEAR: 1 — PROVISIONAL**

**PERCEPTION: UNCONFIRMED**

**OUTPUT: 0.00**

**CONTROL: UNRATED**

**COMBAT: UNRATED**

For a moment, nothing else happened.

Then the final line resolved.

**FIRST-YEAR RANK: 10,482 / 10,482**

Kade stared.

Sen sipped her tea.

He waited for the sting.

It arrived precisely on schedule.

Of course.

He had reached the most selective academy on Earth through a classified back door and immediately proven himself the least capable person there.

“That seems efficient,” he said.

“You are last.”

“I understood the fraction.”

“Good.”

Kade looked at her.

“You’re enjoying this.”

“A little.”

“Director quality.”

She set the tea on the floor.

“Last place is useful.”

“People in first say that.”

“You cannot fall.”

The sentence landed differently than she intended.

Or perhaps exactly as she intended.

Kade looked again at the number.

Ten thousand four hundred eighty-two.

The last name in a cohort larger than his entire hometown high school district.

He imagined the number on every public board he had walked past yesterday.

His phone started vibrating on the bench.

Then again.

Then continuously.

The wall display flashed.

**PUBLIC LADDER UPDATED**

Kade looked at Sen.

“What did you do?”

“I did nothing.”

His phone showed seventeen messages.

Darius first.

**DUDE**

Then:

**YOU ARE ACTUALLY LAST**

Then:

**THIS IS THE FUNNIEST POSSIBLE OUTCOME**

Kade closed his eyes.

Sen said, “Friend?”

“Formerly.”

Another message appeared from an unknown number.

**ZERO = 10,482. WELCOME TO ASTERION.**

Another.

A link to a campus forum already titled:

**ZERO MERCER: CLASSIFIED PRODIGY OR ADMINISTRATIVE ERROR?**

Kade stared at the screen.

For one second, Northline seemed very far away.

At Northline everybody already had a file.

Nobody could pretend theirs made them better.

Here, ten thousand four hundred eighty-one people had a number proving they were ahead of him.

His chest tightened.

The old reflex arrived instantly.

*Doesn’t matter.*

He almost said it.

Almost laughed.

Almost turned the whole thing into a joke before the number could become real.

Instead he looked back at the wall.

10,482.

He thought of Ms. Alvarez.

*Try.*

He thought of Elena at the kitchen table.

*Do not get scared now that somebody finally asked the right question.*

Sen watched him.

“What?” Kade said.

“I was waiting.”

“For what?”

“To see whether you would leave.”

The accusation landed close enough to something true that Kade had to choose his next breath.

“You think I’m going to?”

“I think you have a history of leaving things before they can judge you.”

“You read my file.”

“I read all admitted students’ files.”

“I’m not admitted.”

Sen picked up her tea.

“Keep telling yourself that.”

Kade stood.

Not to leave.

Just because sitting suddenly felt too much like being observed.

“What do you think Zero means?” he asked.

Sen’s face went still.

“Today?”

He frowned. “What does that mean?”

“It means today your measured Resonant output is zero, your conventional perception is unconfirmed, and your adaptive cognition is unusual enough to justify further evaluation.”

“That was very careful.”

“Yes.”

“You know more.”

“Yes.”

The admission surprised him.

“Will you tell me?”

“Not yet.”

“Why?”

“Because incomplete explanations can become instructions.”

Kade stared at her.

“That sounds like something people say before doing something suspicious.”

“Probably.”

She walked toward the door.

Kade looked at the ranking again.

“Director.”

Sen stopped.

“What happens next?”

“Tomorrow you move into House Meridian.”

“I thought House assignment followed testing.”

“It does.”

“Why Meridian?”

“Because its faculty lead volunteered.”

“For last place?”

“For you.”

That was worse.

Sen opened the door.

“And Mercer?”

“Yes?”

“Do not confuse being unusual with being important.”

He frowned.

“Comforting.”

“Good.”

She left.

Park entered carrying his shoes and a thin glass tablet.

She placed both beside him.

The tablet showed the categories from his profile, but this version had more columns. Every test was listed with time, confidence interval, equipment status, and the initials of the person who had verified it.

Nine passive-perception trials.

Six active-output trials.

One control pass.

Zero was no longer a large empty space on a wall. It was a row of small, completed things.

“You have ten minutes to review before certification,” Park said.

Kade picked up the tablet.

“You let candidates challenge the result?”

“Candidates may challenge procedure, equipment, interpretation, or identity. Disliking the number is not a challenge category.”

“Your system lacks imagination.”

“Your system has nine minutes.”

He opened the adaptive metrics.

Route selection sat in the ninety-sixth percentile. Hazard discrimination, ninety-first. Recovery after false cue, eighty-eight. His time had been mediocre. His objective score was high. His compliance score had acquired a small red triangle.

“Why doesn’t any of this affect the rank?”

“It does.”

“I am last.”

“You have no rated control or combat result, and the first-week ladder weights demonstrated Resonant function heavily. Adaptive metrics break ties and inform placement. They cannot substitute for categories you have not performed.”

Kade scrolled to the formula.

It was visible.

That annoyed him more than if it had been hidden.

The weighting was brutal, but it was not broken. His best category could not carry five empty ones. Every student ahead of him had produced something the academy could measure and he had not.

“So the result is accurate.”

Park folded her arms.

“Within the model being used.”

He looked up.

“That sounded almost critical.”

“It was precise.”

The answer belonged beside Sen’s explanation of rankings. Performance made legible. A model did not need to hate him to put him last. It only needed to value what he had failed to do.

Kade returned to the passive-perception records.

Trial seven showed a brief noise event at the exact time the pressure had developed behind his ears.

He opened it.

**PHYSIOLOGICAL VARIANCE: 0.7%**

**FIELD CORRELATION: BELOW REPORTING THRESHOLD**

“That one.”

Park came closer.

“What about it?”

“I had pressure here.” Kade touched the base of his skull. “You called the trial negative.”

“You reported no non-ordinary perception.”

“I thought it was ordinary pressure.”

“Then the recorded answer is correct.”

“But the variance happened with the field.”

“It also happened during two inactive intervals.”

She expanded the record. There they were: smaller changes before the test and while the apparatus reset.

Not proof.

Not nothing either.

“Can I challenge interpretation?”

“On what basis?”

Kade read the three events again.

“I don’t know yet.”

Park nodded once.

“That is not a basis.”

She did not sound pleased about it.

Kade closed the challenge form without submitting.

“Can I get the data?”

“Your medical and performance records will appear in your student account after provisional enrollment.”

“All of it?”

“Everything you have clearance to see.”

“That was a dangerous phrase.”

“It was an accurate one.”

He reached the certification page.

At the bottom were two options.

**ACKNOWLEDGE RESULT**

**DECLINE CERTIFICATION AND REPEAT BASELINE**

“What happens if I repeat?”

“Tomorrow’s result replaces today’s only if procedural fault is established. Otherwise both remain in the record.”

“And I still move to Meridian?”

“House placement is independent of whether you enjoy your rank.”

Kade stared at the acknowledgement box.

The number hurt because it was public.

It hurt more because, for today, it was earned.

He pressed his thumb to the glass.

**RESULT ACKNOWLEDGED — NOT WAIVER OF APPEAL**

Park took the tablet.

“Most candidates do not read that long.”

“Most candidates probably have better numbers.”

“Higher numbers.”

Kade put on his shoes.

“You do that deliberately.”

“Words are equipment, Mr. Mercer. Poor calibration causes accidents.”

She moved toward the door.

“Dr. Park.”

She turned.

“Did Director Sen tell you to keep testing after the first result?”

Park considered him.

“Director Sen authorized a full baseline.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“No.”

He waited.

Park looked through the glass at the room, now empty except for the steel puck.

“I kept testing because a result becomes useful only after you understand where it stops being true.”

Then she opened the door.

The public corridor waited on the other side.

Kade remained beneath his ranking.

Ten thousand four hundred eighty-two.

Last.

A minute passed.

Then two.

He took a picture of the display and sent it to Darius.

**Enjoy it while you can.**

Darius responded immediately.

**IS THAT A THREAT**

Kade looked at the number again.

The shame was still there.

So was anger.

So was the small, deeply inconvenient thing he had felt looking at the island from the air.

Want.

For once, he did not make any of those feelings cancel the others.

He typed:

**No.**

Then:

**It’s a plan.**

Kade hit send.

For the first time since arriving at Asterion, he smiled without using it to hide anything.

---

The plan became more difficult at lunch.

Kade had not expected the ranking to follow him physically.

It did.

The temporary-residence dining hall projected first-year rankings on a narrow strip above the food stations. Most students ignored it with the intense concentration of people who absolutely knew where their own names were.

Kade entered carrying a tray.

The strip refreshed.

**10,482 — MERCER, KADE — PROVISIONAL**

Conversation changed around him.

Not stopped.

Changed.

A fraction quieter.

A fraction more directional.

Kade considered turning around.

Then Imani Bell waved from a table near the window.

“Last place.”

Several people looked at her.

Kade walked over.

“Medical review.”

She pointed to the seat across from her.

“You’re famous.”

“I noticed.”

“Congratulations.”

“On what?”

“Having the easiest rank to remember.”

Kade sat.

Imani’s own preliminary rank showed on her wrist display.

6,771.

“You did better than me.”

She looked offended.

“I have functional Resonance.”

“Show-off.”

“It can move approximately one paper clip.”

“Terrifying.”

A boy at the next table leaned over.

“Are you actually Zero?”

Kade took a bite of sandwich.

“No. I’m Kade.”

The boy blinked.

Imani laughed.

The questioner went back to his table.

Kade looked at her.

“Thanks.”

“For what?”

“Being more annoying than they are.”

“You’re welcome.”

They ate.

Imani told him the cardiology team had cleared her implant for low-output field exposure and scheduled a second test for the following week.

Kade listened.

Not because he understood half of it.

Because nobody at the table cared that she was ranked six thousand seven hundred seventy-one.

They cared whether her heart implant let her do the thing she had trained years to do.

Rankings were everywhere.

They were not everything.

Mateo Ruiz passed their table and raised his water bottle.

“Numbers with time attached.”

Kade pointed at him.

“I have not agreed to that philosophy.”

“You will.”

“Arrogant for four seventy-two.”

Mateo grinned and kept walking.

Kade looked at the ranking strip again.

Ten thousand four hundred eighty-two.

It still hurt.

Good.

Maybe pain was information too.

---

After lunch, every baseline candidate attended a ladder briefing in a lecture room built around a screen three stories high.

Kade took a seat beside Imani near the back.

Mateo sat one row ahead and turned around when Kade arrived.

“You acknowledged it.”

Kade stopped.

“How do you know that?”

Mateo pointed at the screen.

Names with uncertified results were gray. Kade’s was now white.

“You can all see that?”

Imani looked at him.

“You thought the public ranking was private?”

“I was hoping for inconsistent design.”

An instructor walked onto the stage before she could answer. He wore a green sash with **LADDER OFFICE** printed along one edge and had the exhausted posture of someone assigned to explain numbers to ten thousand ambitious teenagers.

“Your first rank is a baseline, not a prophecy,” he said.

Around the room, several students began recording.

“It determines initial training bands, not class access. It may affect challenge eligibility after orientation. It does not determine housing, meals, medical care, equipment safety standards, or the value of your continued existence, despite last year’s forum discussion.”

Mateo raised one hand without waiting.

“What was the forum position?”

“Incorrect.”

The room laughed.

Kade did too, then caught himself looking at the white name on the enormous screen.

The instructor explained recalculation windows. A full baseline at enrollment. Weekly updates after rated instruction began. Immediate changes only after sanctioned challenges or corrected procedural errors. Performance decayed in weight as newer evidence replaced it.

Numbers with time attached.

The phrase was not Mateo’s philosophy. It was system design.

Then the instructor displayed a sample student record.

“Every rank compresses unlike categories. Output, control, perception, tactical judgment, safety, recovery, and rated application are not interchangeable. The number makes comparison possible. It does not make comparison complete.”

Kade leaned forward.

On the screen, the sample student ranked twelve hundred overall and below eight thousand in raw output.

Imani whispered, “Paper clip empire.”

“Your reign begins soon.”

“I’ll spare you.”

“Politically wise.”

The instructor moved to questions.

A girl near the front asked whether family-sponsored training hours were adjusted for in the initial ladder.

“Recorded,” he said. “Not adjusted.”

The girl’s mouth tightened.

Someone else asked why students with medical restrictions appeared beside unrestricted candidates.

“Because restrictions describe permitted exposure, not ability. Safety limits are shown to instructors and challenge officials. They are not public medical labels.”

Imani stopped joking.

Her fingers closed around the sleeve covering her implant.

The answer protected something the rank did not show.

Kade understood then why she had told the stranger in the terminal about her heart and why she might not want ten thousand other people told for her.

The screen could expose a score without owning the reason beneath it.

Mateo asked, “What happens when someone has a category the ladder doesn’t recognize?”

The instructor glanced toward the back.

Not at Mateo.

At Kade.

Half the room followed his gaze.

“The academy evaluates demonstrated effects,” the instructor said. “A category is not created for a claim.”

Kade raised his hand.

The instructor looked resigned. “Mr. Mercer.”

“What if the effect is real and the model is wrong?”

The room went quiet enough that Kade could hear someone’s stylus tap the floor.

“Then evidence must show where the model fails.”

“And until then?”

“Until then, the model reports what it can support.”

Not fair.

Not unfair either.

A condition.

Kade looked up at his name.

10,482.

The system had made a claim about today. If he wanted it to make a different claim, anger would not be evidence.

He would need to produce something the room could not mistake for an open door moving a puck.

The instructor dismissed them.

Students stood in clusters, comparing numbers and training bands. Nobody left without looking once at the screen, including the ones who pretended to check messages while facing it.

Imani remained seated.

“You asked my question,” she said.

“I thought it was mine.”

“It can be both.”

Kade glanced at her sleeve.

He did not ask what the medical restriction hid. She had already decided what to tell him.

“Next week,” he said, “one paper clip.”

“Two.”

“Reckless.”

She stood.

“Tomorrow, Meridian.”

He looked at her.

“How do you know my House?”

“Also public.”

“This place is hostile to mystery.”

Above them, his certified last-place rank remained on display.

Kade walked out beneath it instead of waiting for the screen to change.

That evening he called Elena.

She answered from their kitchen table.

The black box containing Aaron’s things was no longer visible.

The Asterion folder was.

“How bad?” she asked.

Kade frowned. “Why do you assume bad?”

“You called exactly on time.”

“That is an accusation.”

“Kade.”

He turned the camera toward the public ranking board across the courtyard.

Elena read the number.

Her eyebrows rose.

“Oh.”

“That reaction lacked support.”

“You are very last.”

“I am aware.”

She pressed her lips together.

“Are you laughing?”

“No.”

“You are.”

“I love you.”

“That is unrelated.”

She did laugh then.

Kade tried to be offended.

It lasted five seconds.

Then he laughed too.

When it passed, Elena said, “Do you want to come home?”

The question was simple.

No challenge hidden inside it.

No disappointment.

No expectation.

Kade looked at the island outside his window.

At students crossing the bridge below.

At the arena roofs.

At the first-year ranking board with his name at the bottom.

“No,” he said.

The answer surprised him with how quickly it came.

Elena noticed.

“Okay.”

“That’s it?”

“That’s it.”

“You’re not going to say you told me so?”

“I am storing it for a more irritating moment.”

“Good parenting.”

“Thank you.”

Kade looked back at the number.

Last place was humiliating.

It was public.

It was unfair in at least four ways he could already identify.

And tomorrow he was moving into House Meridian.

He had no idea what that meant.

He wanted to find out.

That was enough of a plan for one day.

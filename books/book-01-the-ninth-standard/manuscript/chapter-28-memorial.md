# Chapter 28 — Memorial

Asterion held memorials in the Hall of Names.

Kade had walked past it dozens of times.

He had never gone inside.

The doors were dark wood instead of glass.

That alone made the building feel older than the rest of campus.

Most of Asterion announced itself through transparency—walls you could see through, rankings everybody could read, training footage available before students had finished showering.

The Hall of Names hid everything until someone opened the door.

Kade stood outside with Meridian and did not move.

Sera looked at him.

“You coming?”

“In theory.”

“That is not movement.”

“I know.”

Mara stood on his other side.

No one touched him.

Kade appreciated that.

The blue strip from Eli’s bedpost had been tied around Kade’s wrist for the ceremony. Every Meridian student attending wore one.

Nobody had explained that ritual either.

Kade was beginning to suspect House culture was mostly accumulated things nobody remembered deciding.

Taren stood ten meters away under supervision.

Two academy security officers remained near him—not touching, not pretending he was simply another student.

He wore the same blue strip.

Sera had objected.

Then changed her mind without explaining why.

Kade had not asked.

Some decisions needed room.

The doors opened.

They went inside.

The Hall was larger than it looked from the courtyard.

Names covered the walls in narrow bronze lines.

Not only students.

Faculty.

Staff.

Graduates killed in field disasters.

People lost during the early Resonance years.

Names from incidents Kade recognized from textbooks and names that meant nothing to him at all.

The building did not rank them.

That was the first thing he noticed.

No Standards.

No ladder positions.

No awards.

Just names and dates.

People flattened into the one measurement Asterion could not turn competitive.

At the front of the room, Eli’s photograph stood beneath the older names.

He had apparently submitted a serious academy portrait.

That alone seemed dishonest.

Someone had placed his battered drone rotor beneath the photograph beside the formal academy crest.

Kade almost laughed when he saw it.

That felt wrong for half a second.

Then it felt exactly right.

The room was full.

That surprised him.

Not because Eli had not been liked.

Because Kade had somehow imagined grief should be private.

A mistake, apparently.

Students from engineering filled three rows.

House Meridian took another two.

Priya Shah stood near the back.

Owen Park sat with Vector Analysis students.

Professor Amadi was there.

Instructor Hale came in late and stood by the door.

Two maintenance technicians Kade did not recognize sat together in work gray.

One of them carried a repaired sensor housing in both hands like an offering.

Kade leaned toward Sera.

“Who are they?”

“Facilities.”

“Eli knew facilities?”

Sera gave him a look.

“Eli knew every person who could unlock a panel.”

Fair.

A woman in a municipal-response uniform sat in the third row.

Kade recognized her only after Sera whispered, “He emailed her about the stabilizer.”

Kade looked at Eli’s photograph again.

He had known Eli lived outside Room 814.

Knowing was different from seeing the evidence fill a hall.

Asterion began memorials with silence.

Not a recorded tone.

Not a formal statement.

The doors closed and everybody stood for one minute.

Kade counted the first fifteen seconds.

Then stopped.

The silence did what silence always did when he let it.

It filled with everything he had been avoiding.

Eli laughing at his own spreadsheet labels.

Eli carrying the rescue capsule for eight seconds and telling the story like it had been eight hours.

Eli saying civilian rescue hardware should not cost more than the people it was supposed to save.

Eli on the wrong side of the pressure door.

Kade opened his eyes.

The minute ended.

No one looked relieved.

Dr. Sen spoke first.

She did not talk about classified work or brilliance or potential.

She talked about a student who submitted safety paperwork only after being asked twice and who had once corrected a faculty equipment specification during a disciplinary meeting.

The room laughed softly.

Sen continued.

“Elias Navarro believed rules deserved reasons. This created work for many of us.”

Another small laugh.

“He also believed that if a system failed the people most dependent on it, the system was not finished.”

Kade looked down.

That sounded like Eli.

An engineering professor followed.

Professor Dae Kim, head of first-year systems labs.

He described Eli’s fellowship proposal.

Low-cost field stabilization.

Civilian response.

Rural clinics.

Rescue teams.

Then he held up a prototype connector no larger than a coin.

“Mr. Navarro rejected this component because the material cost was forty-three credits higher than an equivalent part made from industrial ceramic.”

Someone in engineering laughed.

Kim smiled faintly.

“I told him forty-three credits was irrelevant at laboratory scale.”

He looked at Eli’s photograph.

“He told me laboratory scale was how expensive mistakes became policy.”

Kade swallowed.

The municipal-response officer spoke next.

Her name was Captain Janel Ortiz.

“I never met Elias in person,” she said. “He sent me nine messages.”

A few people laughed already.

“The first was a question about bridge-collapse stabilization. The next seven were corrections to my answer.”

More laughter.

“The ninth was an apology for the seven corrections.”

Kade closed his eyes briefly.

That was painfully plausible.

Ortiz held up the repaired sensor housing the maintenance technician had brought.

“He wanted emergency equipment ordinary departments could afford. He asked good questions about who gets protected first when technology is scarce.”

She set it beside the drone rotor.

“I hope somebody finishes that question.”

Kade looked at Eli’s unfinished fellowship model.

Not literally.

He could see it anyway.

Sera spoke next.

She stood behind the lectern and looked irritated that everyone was looking at her.

“Eli believed every problem was technical until proven otherwise,” she said.

A few people smiled.

“He was wrong about that. Constantly.”

More smiles.

“But he also believed systems could be made better if someone cared enough to understand why they failed.”

Sera paused.

Her hand closed around the blue strip at her wrist.

“I am applying to Disaster Response because I want to be the person who knows what to do when a building becomes a problem.”

Kade looked at her.

She had not planned to say that publicly.

He could tell.

Sera looked at Eli’s photograph.

“He wanted to build the things that person would need.”

Her voice broke once.

Barely.

“I hope I get good enough to break all of them for him.”

The room laughed through tears.

Sera sat down fast.

Kade did not look at her for several seconds.

Sometimes kindness was privacy.

Mara spoke after her.

She talked about Eli’s refusal to accept hidden assumptions in models.

About how he had once spent three hours proving a training algorithm was unfair to left-handed users simply because faculty called the difference statistically insignificant.

“He did not accept insignificant easily,” Mara said.

Her voice remained steady.

Her hands did not.

Kade noticed.

Mara continued.

“He also did not understand when to stop explaining.”

A laugh moved through engineering.

“So I will stop.”

She sat.

Julian did not speak.

He had organized the memorial logistics instead.

That felt like him.

Kade had not planned to speak either.

He told himself that repeatedly while other people went to the front.

A maintenance technician described Eli returning a tool two weeks late but cleaner than when he borrowed it.

Owen Park said Eli had once spent an entire lunch proving Owen’s calculator was rounding a third decimal incorrectly.

Priya told the story of Eli trying to explain combat strategy to her after the Forty-Seven Second Fight despite being ranked thousands of places below her.

“He was wrong,” she said.

A beat.

“Mostly.”

People laughed.

Each story made Eli more present and less reachable.

Kade almost left.

He recognized the instinct.

Door.

Hallway.

Air.

Distance.

Then Sen said, “If anyone else wishes to say something—”

Kade stood.

His legs moved before the rest of him agreed.

The walk to the lectern felt longer than any arena.

He looked at Eli’s photograph.

Serious portrait.

Ridiculous person.

Kade gripped the edge of the lectern.

For several seconds, nothing came.

The room waited.

Normally he would fill silence with a joke before it became dangerous.

This silence was already dangerous.

So he left it alone.

“Eli broke things,” Kade said.

A small laugh moved through the room.

“He usually called it testing. That was generous.”

Another laugh.

Kade swallowed.

“He was the first person at Asterion who didn’t care why I was here.”

The room went quiet.

“He cared whether I ate. Whether I studied. Whether my footwork was terrible.”

Mara’s mouth twitched.

“It was.”

A few people laughed again.

Kade looked at the photograph.

“He made a spreadsheet about me before he knew me well enough to know whether I would be offended by that.”

Sera muttered, “He absolutely knew.”

The room laughed.

Kade almost lost his voice.

He waited until it came back.

“I spent a long time thinking people mattered less if you didn’t need them.”

The sentence came easier than he expected.

“Because if you don’t need anyone, nobody gets leverage. Nobody gets to leave a hole.”

He looked at the empty seat beside Mara.

“I was wrong.”

His throat tightened.

“Needing people is not weakness. It is just expensive.”

A tiny, painful laugh moved through the Hall.

Kade breathed.

“Eli was worth it.”

He almost stopped there.

Then Captain Ortiz’s sentence came back.

I hope somebody finishes that question.

Kade looked toward the engineering students.

“He wanted to build things for people who could not afford Asterion answers.”

His eyes found Sera.

“He was not finished.”

The words hurt.

Kade let them.

“So if any of you engineers are thinking about turning his fellowship proposal into a memorial project where everybody makes speeches and nobody actually ships anything—he would hate that.”

The room laughed louder.

Professor Kim covered his mouth.

Kade almost smiled.

“Build something useful.”

That was all.

It was enough.

He returned to his seat.

Mara took his hand for the final minute of the ceremony.

This time Kade did not pretend not to notice.

---

Afterward, the Hall did not empty quickly.

That was another ritual.

People stayed.

They ate small sandwiches in the courtyard and told less formal stories.

The stories became progressively worse for Eli’s reputation.

A second-year engineer described the time he borrowed a thermal camera to prove a toaster was inefficient.

One maintenance worker claimed Eli had named three service robots and then denied it when staff started using the names.

Captain Ortiz found Sera.

They talked for almost twenty minutes.

Kade watched Sera listen more than speak.

When Ortiz left, Sera stared at the contact card in her hand.

“You going to call?” Kade asked.

“Probably.”

“That is almost enthusiasm.”

“Careful.”

Kade smiled.

Professor Kim approached him next.

“Mercer.”

Kade braced for another condolence.

Instead Kim handed him a data wafer.

“Navarro’s fellowship proposal.”

Kade did not take it.

“Why me?”

“Because his family controls the formal disposition, but his working group has technical access rights. You are listed as a collaborator.”

Kade blinked.

“I am not an engineer.”

“No.”

“Then why did he list me?”

Kim’s mouth moved slightly.

“Under contribution, he wrote: ‘asks annoying system-boundary questions.’”

Kade laughed despite himself.

He took the wafer.

“I do not know what to do with this.”

“Good. Do nothing today.”

Professor Kim walked away.

Kade held the wafer in his palm.

Even dead, Eli had found a way to assign homework.

Taren remained at the edge of the courtyard under supervision.

Kade had not spoken to him since Lab Six.

Taren looked toward him once.

Then away.

For the first time, Kade understood that forgiveness was not required simply because grief had made anger exhausting.

He could carry both later.

Not today.

After fifteen minutes more, he needed air.

He walked back to Meridian alone.

The campus felt wrong in memorial quiet.

Classes had resumed around the edges. Students crossed walkways. Training continued in distant arenas. The world had the poor manners to keep functioning.

At Meridian, the blue strip still marked Eli’s bed.

Day four of seven.

Kade opened Room 814.

The empty bed was still empty.

The Hall of Names had not changed that.

He sat at Eli’s desk.

Not his own.

For the first time.

The fellowship model rotated above the surface.

Kade placed Professor Kim’s wafer beside it.

He did not open it.

Today was not for turning grief into a project.

He looked at the note already visible on the model.

**Version 7 still too expensive.**

Then at the line from Eli’s notebook.

*People should not need to be rich, military, famous, or lucky to survive the weird future we are building.*

“Annoying,” Kade whispered.

His tablet chimed.

A maintenance notification.

He ignored it.

The tablet chimed again.

Same channel.

Kade frowned.

Eli had once hijacked an obsolete maintenance subnetwork to test drone routing because, in Eli’s words, nobody respected abandoned infrastructure enough.

Kade opened the message.

Sender identification:

**NULL**

One line.

**DUDE.**

Kade stopped breathing.

He stared until the letters blurred.

No.

Grief did strange things.

He had not slept.

Somebody could be cruel.

A system error could reuse an old packet.

NULL-7 had existed before the blast.

The investigators had taken Eli’s tablet.

A second message appeared.

**DO NOT FREAK OUT.**

Kade stood so quickly the chair rolled backward.

His hands started shaking.

Not the Boundary tremor.

Different.

A third message.

**I AM SERIOUS ABOUT THE FREAKING OUT PART. THERE ARE SENSORS.**

Kade looked toward the ceiling.

The room sensors were standard housing hardware.

He had never thought about who could see them.

A fourth message arrived.

**ALSO PLEASE DO NOT TELL SERA I USED “DUDE” IN A FUNERAL WINDOW.**

Kade sat down very slowly.

His pulse hammered in his throat.

He typed with both thumbs.

**WHO IS THIS**

The answer appeared instantly.

**WOW. GRIEF HAS MADE YOU SUSPICIOUS.**

Kade’s vision blurred.

He laughed.

Then covered his mouth because the sound came out broken.

**ELI?**

A pause.

Long enough to hurt.

Then:

**YES.**

Kade put both hands on the desk.

Hope was not gentle.

It hit harder than grief because grief had already taught his body how much could be lost.

He stood.

Sat again.

Stood.

His brain seemed unable to choose a physical response.

He typed:

**PROVE IT**

A reply came.

**PANIC GEOMETRY IS A VALID ANALYTICAL CATEGORY AND YOU KNOW IT.**

Kade laughed again.

No one else knew that column name except Kade and Eli.

Probably.

Another message:

**ALSO YOU STILL OWE ME 14 CREDITS FROM MERIDIAN NIGHT.**

Kade typed:

**I DO NOT**

**YOU ABSOLUTELY DO**

His hands shook harder.

Then the next line appeared.

**TELL NO ONE YET. SEN KNOWS. MARA CAN KNOW. SERA WILL MURDER ME BUT SHE CAN KNOW AFTER WE MOVE.**

Kade read it twice.

Sen knows.

Of course Sen knew.

Anger and relief arrived at exactly the same time.

He had just stood in front of hundreds of people and said needing Eli had been worth the cost.

Apparently the universe had decided to test whether he meant it before lunch fully digested.

Kade typed:

**WHERE ARE YOU**

The reply:

**CLASSIFIED ALIVE. VERY INCONVENIENT.**

**WHY**

A longer pause.

Then:

**BECAUSE THE PEOPLE WHO BLEW LAB SIX ARE STILL WATCHING.**

Kade stopped.

The memorial courtyard.

The room sensors.

The mirrored status traffic.

The unresolved device identifier.

Pieces moved without becoming a full picture.

**DID THEY TRY TO KILL YOU**

Three dots.

Gone.

Back.

**MAYBE. WE NEED TO TALK WHERE THIS CHANNEL ISN’T A TERRIBLE IDEA.**

Kade stared.

Then another message came.

**AND KADE?**

**WHAT**

**I HEARD ABOUT THE SPEECH.**

Kade’s throat closed.

**HOW**

**CLASSIFIED ALIVE, REMEMBER?**

Then:

**WORTH IT IS A LOT OF PRESSURE.**

Kade wiped his eyes with one sleeve.

**GET USED TO IT**

The response came instantly.

**THAT’S THE SPIRIT**

Kade laughed.

This time he let himself.

Then he stood and went to find Mara.

The blue strip remained tied to the empty bed.

For the first time, empty did not mean gone.

It meant Kade did not yet understand the system.

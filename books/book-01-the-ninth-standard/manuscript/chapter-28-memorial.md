# Chapter 28 — Memorial

Asterion held memorials in the Hall of Names.

Two hours before Eli’s, House Meridian gathered around the common-room table to decide what the Hall would be allowed to keep.

The academy had sent a checklist.

One formal portrait.

One House object.

One printed program biography, limited to two hundred words.

Julian had already reduced Eli’s life to one hundred ninety-eight words.

Sera stood over his shoulder reading it with the concentration she usually reserved for structural failure.

“Delete *promising*.”

Julian looked up from the tablet. “Why?”

“It means adults expected him to become useful later.”

“He was useful now,” Priya said from the other end of the table.

She had arrived ten minutes earlier, taken the chair Julian had been using, and informed him that helping Meridian did not make him Meridian.

Julian had moved one chair to the left.

Kade sat with Eli’s broken drone rotor in front of him.

Beside it lay the ceramic connector from the fellowship prototype and the mug that said **WORLD’S MOST ADEQUATE ENGINEER**.

The Hall allowed one object.

Asterion could apparently quantify even this.

“The connector,” Julian said. “It represents the work.”

“The work is not finished,” Kade replied.

“That may be why it represents him.”

Kade turned the little ceramic piece between two fingers.

It had no markings. No visible reason to matter. Anyone walking into the Hall would need a paragraph explaining why Eli had rejected a component that cost forty-three credits more.

Eli would have supplied six paragraphs and an appendix.

The mug required no explanation.

That made it feel like a joke the room would understand and nobody else would.

The rotor was bent from an ordinary failed repair on an ordinary day.

It belonged to no fellowship committee. It made no claim about what Eli might have become. It was only proof that he had lived in Room 814 long enough to leave something unfinished there.

Kade pushed the other two objects aside.

“Rotor.”

Sera read the biography again.

“Delete *sacrifice* too.”

Julian’s hand stopped above the screen.

“He went back into Lab Six to close the vent.”

“That was his decision. *Sacrifice* turns the decision into something the academy gets to praise itself for surviving.”

The room went quiet.

Kade looked at her.

Sera did not look away from the program.

Julian deleted the word.

“Elias Navarro died after preventing a larger systems failure,” he read.

“Presumed dead,” Kade said.

Priya’s chair creaked as she leaned forward.

Nobody corrected him.

The investigation had used the phrase for three days. Everyone else had begun dropping the first word because the second was easier to print.

Julian put it back.

**Elias Navarro is presumed dead after preventing a larger systems failure in Lab Six. He believed technical excellence was incomplete if ordinary people could not afford its protection.**

He turned the tablet so the House could read it.

“One hundred ninety-four.”

“You can remove the sentence about his ranking,” Kade said.

Julian found it near the bottom.

“The Hall biography office requires academy standing.”

Priya held out her hand.

Julian gave her the tablet.

She deleted the sentence, opened the Hall request, and entered **NOT RELEVANT** in the required field.

The form turned amber.

“There,” she said. “Now it can argue with me.”

At the bell cabinet, Sera cut blue cloth from the faded House roll.

One strip for every Meridian student attending.

Not new cloth. The same roll that had supplied the marker still tied to Eli’s bedpost. Its color changed every few centimeters where older knots had protected it from light.

Sera passed the strips around.

Kade tied his badly. Mara, seated beside him, waited until he held out his wrist before fixing the knot.

The common-room door opened.

Taren stood outside with two security officers.

He had been permitted to attend under escort. Apparently permission to grieve and permission to move through campus now required separate forms.

His gaze dropped to the cloth in Sera’s hand.

“May I wear one?”

Sera closed her fist around the roll.

“You do not get to make that strip evidence that Meridian has forgiven you.”

Taren’s face remained still.

“I wouldn’t.”

“You wrote reports about him.”

“I wrote reports about all of you.”

“That does not improve the answer.”

“I know.”

Taren could have explained that his reports had become less useful over time. He could have mentioned the credentials he burned or the locked door he opened while Lab Six came apart.

He left every defense unused.

“I am not asking the House to make me innocent,” he said. “I am asking whether I can carry the person who is missing.”

Sera’s fingers tightened.

Kade could have decided for her. Taren had helped save them. Taren had also made the attack easier for the people who had sent him.

Both facts remained true without Kade turning either into a verdict.

Priya rose and crossed to the bell cabinet.

“The strip marks an absence,” she said. “Not innocence.”

She did not touch the roll.

The choice stayed with Sera.

For several seconds, the only sound in the common room came from Julian correcting the Hall’s punctuation.

Then Sera cut one more length.

She placed it on the table rather than handing it to Taren.

“You tie it yourself.”

Taren crossed the room between his escorts and picked it up.

“Thank you.”

“This changes nothing.”

“I understand.”

He tied the strip around his wrist.

Nobody helped with the knot.

One security officer recorded the exchange on her tablet. The other shifted half a step to keep Taren inside arm’s reach.

The blue cloth changed neither distance.

That mattered.

Meridian could make room for his grief without pretending trust had returned. Taren could carry Eli’s absence without using it to purchase absolution.

Julian sent the biography back to the Hall with the ranking field still amber.

Priya carried the rotor.

Kade carried nothing.

Meridian left together.

---

Kade had walked past it dozens of times.

He had never gone inside.

The doors were dark wood instead of glass.

That alone made the building feel older than the rest of campus.

Most of Asterion announced itself through transparency—walls you could see through, rankings everybody could read, training footage available before students had finished showering.

The Hall of Names hid everything until someone opened the door.

Kade stood outside with Meridian and did not move.

Sera looked at him.

“You coming?”

“In theory.”

“That is not movement.”

“I know.”

Mara stood on his other side.

No one touched him.

Kade appreciated that.

The blue strip from the same faded roll as Eli’s bedpost marker had been tied around Kade’s wrist for the ceremony. Every Meridian student attending wore one.

Nobody had explained that ritual either.

Kade was beginning to suspect House culture was mostly accumulated things nobody remembered deciding.

Taren stood ten meters away under supervision.

Two academy security officers remained near him—not touching, not pretending he was simply another student.

He wore the same blue strip.

The knot Taren had tied himself sat crooked against his wrist.

The doors opened.

They went inside.

The Hall was larger than it looked from the courtyard.

Names covered the walls in narrow bronze lines.

Not only students.

Faculty.

Staff.

Graduates killed in field disasters.

People lost during the early Resonance years.

Names from incidents Kade recognized from textbooks and names that meant nothing to him at all.

The building did not rank them.

That was the first thing that mattered.

No Standards.

No ladder positions.

No awards.

Just names and dates.

People flattened into the one measurement Asterion could not turn competitive.

At the front of the room, Eli’s photograph stood beneath the older names.

He had apparently submitted a serious academy portrait.

That alone seemed dishonest.

Below the photograph, a narrow bronze name strip rested on blue cloth.

**ELIAS NAVARRO**

No dates.

The mounting pins had not been cut.

Kade leaned toward Julian.

“Why is it loose?”

“The Hall does not mount a name until the recovery finding is final.”

Kade looked at him.

“Then why hold the memorial?”

“Because his family chose not to make everybody wait for the investigation before they could gather.”

“But they made the bronze wait.”

“They asked the Hall to keep the record accurate.”

The answer should have sounded like procedure.

Instead it sounded like the professor who had kept Eli’s fellowship suspended rather than rejected. Like the seven-day strip on his bed. Like every careful person refusing to let certainty outrun what they knew.

Kade wanted the loose name to mean something.

He wanted the unresolved device identifier from Lab Six to mean something.

Hope tried to assemble a system from two incomplete measurements.

He stopped it there.

An unmounted name meant the investigation was open.

Nothing more.

Nothing less.

Sera read the strip over his shoulder.

“Good,” she said.

Kade glanced at her.

“The Hall waits for evidence,” she said. “Somebody around here should.”

Then she faced forward.

Kade did too.

Someone had placed his battered drone rotor beneath the photograph beside the formal academy crest.

Kade almost laughed at the sight.

For half a second, laughter seemed wrong.

Then it was exactly right.

The room was full.

That surprised him.

Not because Eli had not been liked.

Because Kade had somehow imagined grief should be private.

A mistake, apparently.

Students from engineering filled three rows.

House Meridian took another two.

Priya Shah stood near the back.

Owen Park sat with Vector Analysis students.

Professor Amadi was there.

Instructor Hale came in late and stood by the door.

Two maintenance technicians Kade did not recognize sat together in work gray.

One of them carried a repaired sensor housing in both hands like an offering.

Kade leaned toward Sera.

“Who are they?”

“Facilities.”

“Eli knew facilities?”

Sera gave him a look.

“Eli knew every person who could unlock a panel.”

Fair.

A woman in a municipal-response uniform sat in the third row.

Kade recognized her only after Sera whispered, “He emailed her about the stabilizer.”

Kade looked at Eli’s photograph again.

He had known Eli lived outside Room 814.

Knowing was different from seeing the evidence fill a hall.

Asterion began memorials with silence.

Not a recorded tone.

Not a formal statement.

The doors closed and everybody stood for one minute.

Kade counted the first fifteen seconds.

Then stopped.

The silence did what silence always did when he let it.

It filled with everything he had been avoiding.

Eli laughing at his own spreadsheet labels.

Eli carrying the rescue capsule for eight seconds and telling the story like it had been eight hours.

Eli saying civilian rescue hardware should not cost more than the people it was supposed to save.

Eli on the wrong side of the pressure door.

Kade opened his eyes.

The minute ended.

No one looked relieved.

Dr. Sen spoke first.

She did not talk about classified work or brilliance or potential.

She talked about a student who submitted safety paperwork only after being asked twice and who had once corrected a faculty equipment specification during a disciplinary meeting.

The room laughed softly.

Sen continued.

“Elias Navarro believed rules deserved reasons. This created work for many of us.”

Another small laugh.

“He also believed that if a system failed the people most dependent on it, the system was not finished.”

Kade looked down.

That sounded like Eli.

An engineering professor followed.

Professor Dae Kim, head of first-year systems labs.

He described Eli’s fellowship proposal.

Low-cost field stabilization.

Civilian response.

Rural clinics.

Rescue teams.

Then he held up a prototype connector no larger than a coin.

“Mr. Navarro rejected this component because the material cost was forty-three credits higher than an equivalent part made from industrial ceramic.”

Someone in engineering laughed.

Kim smiled faintly.

“I told him forty-three credits was irrelevant at laboratory scale.”

He looked at Eli’s photograph.

“He told me laboratory scale was how expensive mistakes became policy.”

Kade swallowed.

The municipal-response officer spoke next.

Her name was Captain Janel Ortiz.

“I never met Elias in person,” she said. “He sent me nine messages.”

A few people laughed already.

“The first was a question about bridge-collapse stabilization. The next seven were corrections to my answer.”

More laughter.

“The ninth was an apology for the seven corrections.”

Kade closed his eyes briefly.

That was painfully plausible.

Ortiz held up the repaired sensor housing the maintenance technician had brought.

“He wanted emergency equipment ordinary departments could afford. He asked good questions about who gets protected first when technology is scarce.”

She set it beside the drone rotor.

“I hope somebody finishes that question.”

Kade looked at Eli’s unfinished fellowship model.

Not literally.

He could see it anyway.

Sera spoke next.

She stood behind the lectern and looked irritated that everyone was looking at her.

“Eli believed every problem was technical until proven otherwise,” she said.

A few people smiled.

“He was wrong about that. Constantly.”

More smiles.

“But he also believed systems could be made better if someone cared enough to understand why they failed.”

Sera paused.

Her hand closed around the blue strip at her wrist.

“I am applying to Disaster Response because I want to be the person who knows what to do when a building becomes a problem.”

Kade looked at her.

She had not planned to say that publicly.

He could tell.

Sera looked at Eli’s photograph.

“He wanted to build the things that person would need.”

Her voice broke once.

Barely.

“I hope I get good enough to break all of them for him.”

The room laughed through tears.

Sera sat down fast.

Kade did not look at her for several seconds.

Sometimes kindness was privacy.

Mara spoke after her.

She talked about Eli’s refusal to accept hidden assumptions in models.

About how he had once spent three hours proving a training algorithm was unfair to left-handed users simply because faculty called the difference statistically insignificant.

“He did not accept insignificant easily,” Mara said.

Her voice remained steady.

Her hands did not.

Mara continued.

“He also did not understand when to stop explaining.”

A laugh moved through engineering.

“So I will stop.”

She sat.

Julian did not speak.

He had organized the memorial logistics instead.

That was like him.

Kade had not planned to speak either.

He told himself that repeatedly while other people went to the front.

A maintenance technician described Eli returning a tool two weeks late but cleaner than when he borrowed it.

Owen Park said Eli had once spent an entire lunch proving Owen’s calculator was rounding a third decimal incorrectly.

Priya told the story of Eli trying to explain combat strategy to her after the Forty-Seven Second Fight despite being ranked thousands of places below her.

“He was wrong,” she said.

A beat.

“Mostly.”

People laughed.

Each story made Eli more present and less reachable.

Kade almost left.

He recognized the instinct.

Door.

Hallway.

Air.

Distance.

Then Sen said, “If anyone else wishes to say something—”

Kade stood.

His legs moved before the rest of him agreed.

The walk to the lectern was longer than any arena.

He looked at Eli’s photograph.

Serious portrait.

Ridiculous person.

Kade gripped the edge of the lectern.

For several seconds, nothing came.

The room waited.

Normally he would fill silence with a joke before it became dangerous.

This silence was already dangerous.

So he left it alone.

“Eli broke things,” Kade said.

A small laugh moved through the room.

“He usually called it testing. That was generous.”

Another laugh.

Kade swallowed.

“He was the first person at Asterion who didn’t care why I was here.”

The room went quiet.

“He cared whether I ate. Whether I studied. Whether my footwork was terrible.”

Mara’s mouth twitched.

“It was.”

A few people laughed again.

Kade looked at the photograph.

“He made a spreadsheet about me before he knew me well enough to know whether I would be offended by that.”

Sera muttered, “He absolutely knew.”

The room laughed.

Kade almost lost his voice.

He waited until it came back.

“I spent a long time thinking people mattered less if you didn’t need them.”

The sentence came easier than he expected.

“Because if you don’t need anyone, nobody gets leverage. Nobody gets to leave a hole.”

He looked at the empty seat beside Mara.

“I was wrong.”

His throat tightened.

“Needing people is not weakness. It is just expensive.”

A tiny, painful laugh moved through the Hall.

Kade breathed.

“Eli was worth it.”

He almost stopped there.

Then Captain Ortiz’s sentence came back.

I hope somebody finishes that question.

Kade looked toward the engineering students.

“He wanted to build things for people who could not afford Asterion answers.”

His eyes found Sera.

“He was not finished.”

The words hurt.

Kade let them.

“So if any of you engineers are thinking about turning his fellowship proposal into a memorial project where everybody makes speeches and nobody actually ships anything—he would hate that.”

The room laughed louder.

Professor Kim covered his mouth.

Kade almost smiled.

“Build something useful.”

That was all.

It was enough.

He returned to his seat.

Mara took his hand for the final minute of the ceremony.

This time Kade did not pretend not to notice.

---

Afterward, the Hall did not empty quickly.

That was another ritual.

People stayed.

They ate small sandwiches in the courtyard and told less formal stories.

The stories became progressively worse for Eli’s reputation.

A second-year engineer described the time he borrowed a thermal camera to prove a toaster was inefficient.

One maintenance worker claimed Eli had named three service robots and then denied it when staff started using the names.

Captain Ortiz found Sera beside the courtyard wall.

She still carried the repaired sensor housing from the ceremony.

Up close, it looked older than Kade had assumed. One corner had been rebuilt in gray polymer. The manufacturer seal had faded until only half the name remained.

Ortiz held it out.

“Navarro said you break emergency equipment.”

Sera accepted the housing carefully.

“Only when the design deserves it.”

“This one deserves it.”

Sera turned the unit over. “What happened?”

“Bridge collapse outside Denver. The field sensor survived the first impact and failed during aftershock monitoring.”

“Battery?”

“Connector.”

Sera found the replacement seam with one thumbnail.

“Proprietary.”

“Discontinued. The department had six housings and four working cables.”

Sera’s attention sharpened.

“So you moved cables between positions.”

“Until one locking collar cracked.”

“Then you had three sensors.”

“We had two. One cable disappeared under the west slab with a responder attached to it.”

The courtyard noise receded around them.

Sera ran her thumb once over the cracked collar.

“Was the responder recovered?”

“Alive.”

Her shoulders lowered by a fraction.

Ortiz tapped the ceramic connector on the repaired corner.

“Elias wanted to standardize the replacement and publish the fabrication pattern. I told him departments could buy new housings faster than we could certify an unofficial part.”

“He hated that answer.”

“He sent me a nineteen-line cost comparison.”

“Only nineteen?”

Ortiz smiled.

“I asked whether he had ever stood beside a responder trusting a part that might fail under load.”

Sera’s smile vanished.

“What did he say?”

Ortiz rotated the housing. A tiny mark had been etched into the replacement seam.

**TEST TO FAILURE. THEN TEST THE FAILURE.**

“He asked what our certification rig needed.”

Sera read the words twice.

“Did he build it?”

“He sent plans. We did not have the budget.”

There was the second half of Eli’s question.

Not whether Asterion could make a cheap part.

Whether anyone outside Asterion could afford to prove the cheap part was safe.

Sera handed the housing back.

“I applied to Disaster Response.”

“I heard.”

“From Eli?”

“From your assessment lead. Navarro only said you would identify everything wrong with my department in under a week.”

“That is unrealistic.”

“A full week?”

“I would need to inspect it first.”

Ortiz laughed.

Then she took a plain paper card from her uniform pocket.

No academy seal. No Directorate authentication. Just a name, a municipal number, and an address on the mainland.

“Call when you are ready to spend a shift with people who cannot replace a cracked housing by opening a laboratory account.”

Sera did not take the card immediately.

“Observation?”

“Work. Supervised. Unpaid.”

“Can I bring the connector plans?”

“If Navarro’s family and fellowship committee permit it.”

Sera nodded.

The answer mattered. Eli’s work did not become public property because he was gone.

She took the card.

“Then I will ask.”

Ortiz left her with the housing’s failure report open on her tablet.

Sera read the report before she read the contact card.

That, more than anything, made Kade believe she would call.

“You going to call?” Kade asked.

“Probably.”

“That is almost enthusiasm.”

“Careful.”

Kade smiled.

Professor Kim approached him next.

“Mercer.”

Kade braced for another condolence.

Instead Kim handed him a data wafer.

“Navarro’s fellowship proposal.”

Kade did not take it.

“Why me?”

“Because his family controls the formal disposition, but his working group has technical access rights. You are listed as a collaborator.”

Kade blinked.

“I am not an engineer.”

“No.”

“Then why did he list me?”

Kim’s mouth moved slightly.

“Under contribution, he wrote: ‘asks annoying system-boundary questions.’”

Kade laughed despite himself.

“What does collaborator mean if I cannot build any of it?”

“It means the committee cannot remove his cost assumptions without notifying you.”

Kade looked at the wafer again.

“Why would they?”

Kim’s expression became very tired.

“Because the fastest way to make his design pass is to replace affordable parts with certified ones and redefine the users as agencies with Asterion procurement access.”

“That would pass by deleting the point.”

“Elias used nearly those words.”

Kade turned the wafer over. One edge carried a green access stripe. The data inside belonged partly to Eli’s family, partly to a working group, partly to an academy that could afford every shortcut the project rejected.

“Captain Ortiz has a connector he designed.”

“A prototype fabricated from plans he released to her department. She may keep and test that unit. She may not distribute the pattern yet.”

“So everyone owns a different piece.”

“Everyone carries a different obligation,” Kim said. “Ownership is what the review panel will spend three meetings arguing about.”

That sounded more accurate.

“How long do they wait?”

“Thirty days before any disposition. Longer if his family requests it. The fellowship remains suspended, not closed.”

Suspended.

The same careful word the professor in Room 814 had used.

Kade could not tell whether the academy was preserving a future or refusing to admit the absence yet.

It could be both.

He took the wafer.

“I do not know what to do with this.”

“Good. Do nothing today.”

“I am not promising to finish it.”

“You are not qualified to promise that.”

Kade looked up.

Kim’s mouth moved slightly.

“You are qualified to ask whether the people who finish it preserved the problem Elias intended to solve.”

Professor Kim walked away.

Kade held the wafer in his palm.

Even dead, Eli had found a way to assign homework.

Taren remained at the edge of the courtyard under supervision.

Kade had not spoken to him since Lab Six.

Taren looked toward him once.

Then away.

For the first time, Kade understood that forgiveness was not required simply because grief had made anger exhausting.

He could carry both later.

Not today.

After fifteen minutes more, he needed air.

He walked back to Meridian alone.

Memorial quiet made the campus wrong.

Classes had resumed around the edges. Students crossed walkways. Training continued in distant arenas. The world had the poor manners to keep functioning.

At Meridian, the blue strip still marked Eli’s bed.

Day four of seven.

Kade opened Room 814.

The empty bed was still empty.

The Hall of Names had not changed that.

He sat at Eli’s desk.

Not his own.

For the first time.

The fellowship model rotated above the surface.

Kade placed Professor Kim’s wafer beside it.

He did not open it.

Today was not for turning grief into a project.

He looked at the note already visible on the model.

**Version 7 still too expensive.**

Then at the line from Eli’s notebook.

*People should not need to be rich, military, famous, or lucky to survive the weird future we are building.*

“Annoying,” Kade whispered.

His tablet chimed.

A maintenance notification.

He ignored it.

The tablet chimed again.

Same channel.

Kade frowned.

Eli had once hijacked an obsolete maintenance subnetwork to test drone routing because, in Eli’s words, nobody respected abandoned infrastructure enough.

Kade opened the message.

Sender identification:

**NULL**

One line.

**DUDE.**

Kade stopped breathing.

He stared until the letters blurred.

No.

Grief did strange things.

He had not slept.

Somebody could be cruel.

A system error could reuse an old packet.

NULL-7 had existed before the blast.

The investigators had taken Eli’s tablet.

A second message appeared.

**DO NOT FREAK OUT.**

Kade stood so quickly the chair rolled backward.

His hands started shaking.

Not the Boundary tremor.

Different.

A third message.

**I AM SERIOUS ABOUT THE FREAKING OUT PART. THERE ARE SENSORS.**

Kade looked toward the ceiling.

The room sensors were standard housing hardware.

He had never thought about who could see them.

A fourth message arrived.

**ALSO PLEASE DO NOT TELL SERA I USED “DUDE” IN A FUNERAL WINDOW.**

Kade sat down very slowly.

His pulse hammered in his throat.

He typed with both thumbs.

**WHO IS THIS**

The answer appeared instantly.

**WOW. GRIEF HAS MADE YOU SUSPICIOUS.**

Kade’s vision blurred.

He laughed.

Then covered his mouth because the sound came out broken.

**ELI?**

A pause.

Long enough to hurt.

Then:

**YES.**

Kade put both hands on the desk.

Hope was not gentle.

It hit harder than grief because grief had already taught his body how much could be lost.

He stood.

Sat again.

Stood.

His brain seemed unable to choose a physical response.

He typed:

**PROVE IT**

A reply came.

**PANIC GEOMETRY IS A VALID ANALYTICAL CATEGORY AND YOU KNOW IT.**

Kade laughed again.

No one else knew that column name except Kade and Eli.

Probably.

Another message:

**ALSO YOU STILL OWE ME 14 CREDITS FROM MERIDIAN NIGHT.**

Kade typed:

**I DO NOT**

**YOU ABSOLUTELY DO**

His hands shook harder.

Then the next line appeared.

**TELL NO ONE YET. SEN KNOWS. MARA CAN KNOW. SERA WILL MURDER ME BUT SHE CAN KNOW AFTER WE MOVE.**

Kade read it twice.

Sen knows.

Of course Sen knew.

Anger and relief arrived at exactly the same time.

He had just stood in front of hundreds of people and said needing Eli had been worth the cost.

Apparently the universe had decided to test whether he meant it before lunch fully digested.

Kade typed:

**WHERE ARE YOU**

The reply:

**CLASSIFIED ALIVE. VERY INCONVENIENT.**

**WHY**

A longer pause.

Then:

**BECAUSE THE PEOPLE WHO BLEW LAB SIX ARE STILL WATCHING.**

Kade stopped.

The memorial courtyard.

The room sensors.

The mirrored status traffic.

The unresolved device identifier.

Pieces moved without becoming a full picture.

**DID THEY TRY TO KILL YOU**

Three dots.

Gone.

Back.

**MAYBE. WE NEED TO TALK WHERE THIS CHANNEL ISN’T A TERRIBLE IDEA.**

Kade stared.

Then another message came.

**AND KADE?**

**WHAT**

**I HEARD ABOUT THE SPEECH.**

Kade’s throat closed.

**HOW**

**CLASSIFIED ALIVE, REMEMBER?**

Then:

**WORTH IT IS A LOT OF PRESSURE.**

Kade wiped his eyes with one sleeve.

**GET USED TO IT**

The response came instantly.

**THAT’S THE SPIRIT**

Kade laughed.

This time he let himself.

Then he stood and went to find Mara.

The blue strip remained tied to the empty bed.

For the first time, empty did not mean gone.

It meant Kade did not yet understand the system.

# Chapter 7 — The Suppression Order

Kade learned that Asterion could make silence feel louder than an arena.

The morning after the Forty-Seven Second Fight, nobody mentioned the final six seconds.

That was how he knew everyone had seen them.

Eli talked about breakfast.

Sera complained about a biomechanics lecture.

Taren delivered a seven-minute argument about why the coffee in South Hall was better than the coffee in Meridian Commons and why this mattered to civilization.

Mara passed Kade on the stairs, looked once at his left hand, and said, “Still shaking?”

Kade put it in his pocket.

“No.”

“It is.”

“Then my pocket is nervous.”

Her eyes almost smiled.

By eight thirty, three students had asked whether he had broken Priya Shah’s shoulder.

He had not.

At eight forty-seven, a second-year student asked whether Kade could redirect bullets.

Kade told him he had trouble redirecting laundry.

At nine sixteen, his training schedule disappeared.

Not changed.

Gone.

His tablet displayed an empty white block where the next six days of field instruction had been.

**TRAINING STATUS: RESTRICTED**

Below it:

**ACTIVE FIELD AUTHORIZATION: SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW**

**RANKED CHALLENGE AUTHORIZATION: SUSPENDED**

He stared.

Eli leaned over his shoulder.

“That seems bad.”

“Thank you, systems engineer.”

“I contain multitudes.”

A message arrived before Kade could decide whether to be angry.

**REPORT TO DIRECTOR SEN — 09:30.**

No explanation.

Kade looked at Eli.

“Definitely bad,” Eli said.

“Your support remains invaluable.”

“Do you want me to come?”

Kade nearly said no.

The answer arrived so automatically he heard it before he spoke.

He stopped.

“No,” he said more carefully. “But thanks.”

Eli nodded as though the distinction mattered.

It did.

---

Director Sen’s office occupied the upper floor of Asterion’s oldest administration building, where the walls were stone instead of glass and nobody had bothered pretending the furniture was comfortable.

Kade arrived two minutes early because being summoned by a Standard Eight Resonant had temporarily improved his relationship with clocks.

Sen stood at the window.

Dr. Park sat at the conference table. Instructor Hale, head of first-year field training, sat beside her.

Three adults.

One empty chair.

Kade sat in it.

“Good morning,” Sen said.

“That depends.”

“On?”

“Whether I’m being expelled before lunch.”

Hale’s mouth twitched.

Sen did not.

“You are not being expelled.”

Kade relaxed approximately three percent.

“The provisional restrictions I gave you in medical are being formalized.”

Three percent vanished.

Park turned a tablet toward him.

Priya’s final charge played in silence.

Impact.

Six missing telemetry frames.

The wall flexing.

Kade’s knees giving out.

Park froze the image.

“Measured field output from your Conduit: effectively zero.”

“I remember.”

“Measured excess structural transfer at the western support system: four-point-eight kilonewtons above model prediction.”

Kade looked at the graph.

“Is that a lot?”

“For someone who registered no measurable field activity?” Hale said. “It is approximately four-point-eight kilonewtons too many.”

“How?”

“That,” Park said, “remains the question.”

Kade looked at Sen.

“So because something strange happened, I’m not allowed to learn how it happened.”

“We are reducing variables,” Sen said.

“That sounds better if you don’t translate it.”

“Translate it.”

“You are afraid I will do it again.”

Hale shifted.

Park looked toward Sen.

Sen held Kade’s gaze.

“Yes.”

The answer stole the joke he had prepared.

Sen crossed the room and sat opposite him.

“We do not know whether what occurred was harmless, repeatable, or stable. We do know you developed a headache, tremor, pupil asymmetry, and short-term temporal disorientation.”

“I got the day wrong once.”

“Your brain is the organ we would prefer you not to experiment on.”

“That seems judgmental toward science.”

“Kade.”

He stopped.

Sen’s voice had not risen.

That made it harder to push against.

“You are not being punished.”

“It feels similar.”

“I understand why.”

He almost laughed.

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

Something in the answer made him study her.

She looked tired.

Not morning tired.

Older than that.

Then the expression was gone.

Hale slid a revised schedule across the table.

“Physical conditioning. Standard academics. Tactical theory. Combat observation. Non-field footwork and grappling. Conduit theory. No unsupervised Resonance drills. No deliberate attempt to reproduce the Forty-Seven event. No ranked challenges until medical and field clearance.”

Kade stared at the last line.

“No ranked challenges.”

“Correct.”

“I’m ten thousand four hundred eighty-two.”

“We are aware.”

“So I’m not allowed to move.”

“Temporarily.”

The heat finally arrived.

Kade stood.

The chair scraped hard enough that Hale looked up.

“Anything else?”

Sen’s eyes narrowed slightly.

“Yes.”

Kade waited.

“Do not confuse restriction with judgment.”

“That advice gets less useful every time somebody restricts me.”

He turned toward the door.

Park said, “Mercer.”

He stopped.

“You can improve every measurable thing you currently do badly without using Resonance.”

Kade looked back.

Park’s expression was dry, almost challenging.

“Your footwork is poor. Your conditioning is below cohort median. Your defensive timing is inconsistent. Your formal physics is incomplete. You have almost no trained combat habits.”

Kade stared at her.

“Did you prepare that list?”

“No. It is easy to remember.”

Hale looked down, possibly to hide a smile.

Park continued.

“If you decide the interesting part of you is the only part worth training, you will remain easy to beat.”

That landed harder than Kade wanted it to.

Sen said nothing.

Kade left.

---

The order was private for approximately forty-seven minutes.

By lunch, half of Meridian knew.

By dinner, the whole first-year cohort did.

The rumor changed every twenty minutes.

Kade had overloaded an arena.

Kade had almost killed Priya.

Kade had failed a neurological screen.

Kade was secretly a government experiment.

Kade had cheated.

The last one bothered him most.

He learned about it because someone had written **ZERO NEEDS TRAINING WHEELS** across the digital message wall outside Meridian Commons.

Eli reached for the controls.

Kade stopped him.

“Leave it.”

“Why?”

“Because deleting it makes them think it worked.”

Eli looked at him.

“Did it?”

Kade looked at the message.

“Yes.”

Eli blinked.

Kade shrugged.

“Still leave it.”

A group of students passed behind them. One lowered her voice just enough to make it obvious she wanted him to hear.

“Convenient. He breaks something and suddenly nobody can challenge him.”

Kade’s shoulders tightened.

Eli turned.

Kade caught his arm.

“Don’t.”

“I was just going to explain—”

“That is how fights begin when engineers are involved.”

“I can fight.”

“You are ranked 8,911.”

“Cruel.”

“Accurate.”

Eli looked at the students disappearing down the corridor.

Then at Kade.

“Okay.”

Kade hated the gratitude that moved through him.

Not because Eli had offered to defend him.

Because Kade had trusted him to stop when asked.

That evening, mandatory physiology lab assigned Kade a partner who spent fifteen minutes pretending not to know who he was.

During conditioning, Hale prohibited him from field-assisted movement and made him run the same course as everyone else without support.

Kade finished in the bottom third.

He wanted to dismiss the result.

Park’s list returned instead.

*Conditioning below cohort median.*

Fine.

He wrote down his time.

That was new.

At dinner, Priya Shah sat across from him without invitation.

A few conversations nearby quieted.

Priya ignored them.

“My shoulder remains tragically unbroken,” she said.

Kade looked up from his food.

“Congratulations.”

“I thought I should make a public appearance.”

“For rumor control?”

“For lunch.”

“It’s dinner.”

“Then I failed.”

Kade smiled.

Priya stabbed a piece of broccoli.

“They restricted you.”

“Yes.”

“That seems reasonable.”

Kade’s smile disappeared.

Priya continued eating.

“You went gray after the wall hit. Your hands shook for an hour.”

“You were there for four minutes.”

“I asked Park later.”

“She answered?”

“No. Which answered enough.”

Kade looked at her.

Priya shrugged.

“I want a rematch eventually. I would prefer your brain intact.”

Then she went back to dinner.

It was difficult to build a satisfying persecution narrative when the person he had supposedly almost injured kept being reasonable.

Kade resented her for it.

A little.

---

That night he went to the roof.

Mara was already there.

Of course she was.

She stood near the east railing with a training tablet in one hand, replaying footwork patterns. The ocean below the island was black except where the breakwater lights cut white lines into the waves.

“You have a room,” Kade said.

“So do you.”

“Mine contains Eli.”

“Fair.”

He leaned on the railing.

For a while neither spoke.

It was one of the first silences at Asterion that did not make Kade feel like he was supposed to fill it.

Mara closed her tablet.

“They restricted you.”

“That rumor made it to rank seven?”

“It made it to the kitchens.”

“Good. I was worried about reach.”

She watched him.

“Do you think they’re wrong?”

Kade opened his mouth.

Closed it.

“I think they don’t know what I am.”

“Neither do you.”

“That is less comforting when you say it.”

“Truth usually is.”

He looked at her.

“You practice being irritating?”

“No. Natural talent.”

Kade laughed.

Mara turned toward the water.

“My father used to say restrictions reveal what you actually want.”

“Your father sounds exhausting.”

“He is.”

Kade waited.

Mara did not elaborate.

“What’s that supposed to mean?”

“It means when something becomes difficult, most people discover they only wanted the idea of it.”

“And if I still want it?”

“Then the restriction is information.”

“Meaning?”

“Find what is not restricted.”

She lifted her tablet.

“Your first eighteen seconds against Shah were terrible.”

“Thank you.”

“Your next nine were less terrible.”

“Growth.”

“You started reading her weight before she moved.”

Kade frowned.

“So?”

“You are restricted from field training. Not from learning to fight.”

She walked toward the roof door.

“Mara.”

She looked back.

“Why are you helping me?”

Her face became very still.

“I am not helping you.”

“You just—”

“I dislike wasted variables.”

Kade stared.

Mara disappeared through the door.

He smiled despite himself.

---

At 22:14, Kade opened the academy challenge rules.

At 22:19, he opened first-year combat recordings.

At 22:31, he opened Hale’s conditioning benchmarks.

At 22:47, he found the first useful distinction.

Ranked challenges required active-field clearance.

Unranked tactical simulations did not.

Neither did footwork drills.

Neither did physical conditioning.

Neither did match analysis.

Neither did grappling fundamentals under zero-field conditions.

Neither did academic study.

Park had not taken away his path.

She had removed the part he wanted to skip toward.

Kade did not enjoy realizing that.

At 23:03, Eli woke to the light from Kade’s desk.

“What are you doing?”

“Learning.”

Eli sat up.

“That sounds dangerous when you say it.”

Kade dragged three fight recordings onto the wall display.

“I need your model.”

Eli blinked sleep from his eyes.

“What model?”

“The one you do not have because somebody removed your telemetry access.”

Eli stared.

Kade waited.

A grin slowly appeared.

“I may have a legal personal-use tactical analysis system that contains no restricted sensor data.”

“Of course you do.”

“It is extremely legal.”

“That adjective made it worse.”

Eli climbed out of bed and crossed to the desk.

The ranking display hovered in the corner of Kade’s tablet.

**10,482 / 10,482**

For the first time since the number had appeared, it did not feel like a verdict.

It felt like a measurement.

Measurements could change.

“They want me last?” Kade said.

Eli rubbed his eyes.

“I don’t think anyone said that.”

Kade looked at the empty field-training blocks on his schedule.

Then at the conditioning times Park had embarrassed him with.

Then at Priya’s first strike.

“Fine.”

Eli blinked.

“Fine what?”

Kade opened a blank training log.

“I’ll climb without the thing they’re afraid of.”

He typed his first entry.

**Conditioning baseline: bad.**

Eli leaned over.

“That is not a number.”

Kade sighed and replaced it with his actual time.

At 23:11, they started reviewing Priya Shah’s footwork frame by frame.

Kade did not go to sleep until 01:36.

The next morning, he got up at six anyway.

Restriction, he was beginning to discover, was not the same thing as being stopped.

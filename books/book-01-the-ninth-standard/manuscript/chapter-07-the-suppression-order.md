# Chapter 7 — The Suppression Order

Kade learned that Asterion could make silence feel louder than an arena.

The morning after the Forty-Seven Second Fight, nobody mentioned the final six seconds.

That was how he knew everyone had seen them.

Eli talked about breakfast. Sera complained about a biomechanics lecture. Taren explained why the coffee in South Hall was better than the coffee in Meridian Commons and why this mattered to civilization. Mara passed Kade on the stairs, gave him one measured look, and said only, “Your left hand still shaking?”

Kade tucked it into his pocket.

“No.”

“It is.”

“Then my pocket is nervous.”

She did not smile, but her eyes almost did.

By eight-thirty, three separate people had asked whether he had broken Priya Shah’s shoulder.

He had not.

By nine, a student he had never met asked whether he could redirect bullets.

Kade told him he had trouble redirecting laundry.

At nine-sixteen, his training schedule disappeared.

Not changed.

Gone.

His tablet showed a white block where the next six days of field instruction had been.

**TRAINING STATUS: RESTRICTED**

He stared at it.

Eli leaned over his shoulder.

“That seems bad.”

“Thank you, systems engineer.”

“I contain multitudes.”

A message arrived before Kade could decide whether to be angry.

**REPORT TO DIRECTOR SEN — 09:30.**

No explanation.

Kade looked at Eli.

“Definitely bad,” Eli said.

Director Sen’s office occupied the upper level of Asterion’s oldest administration building, where the walls were stone instead of glass and nobody had bothered pretending the furniture was comfortable.

Sen stood at the window when Kade entered.

Dr. Park sat at the conference table. Beside her was a man Kade recognized from the arena control booth: Instructor Hale, head of first-year field training.

Three adults.

One chair.

Kade sat in it.

“Good morning,” Sen said.

“That depends.”

“On?”

“Whether I’m being expelled before lunch.”

Hale’s mouth twitched.

Sen did not.

“You are not being expelled.”

Kade relaxed approximately three percent.

“Your active Resonance training is being limited,” she continued.

Three percent vanished.

“Why?”

Park turned a tablet toward him. The screen showed a replay of the final six seconds of his fight.

Priya lunged.

Kade shifted.

Her momentum vanished sideways.

The wall flexed.

The recording froze.

Park tapped a graph.

“Measured field output from your Conduit: effectively zero.”

“I remember.”

“Measured force transfer into the west arena wall: four-point-eight kilonewtons above model prediction.”

Kade looked at the frozen image.

“Is that a lot?”

“For someone who registered no field output?” Hale said. “Yes.”

“How?”

“That,” Park said, “is the question.”

Kade looked at Sen.

“So because something strange happened, you’re stopping me from learning how it happened.”

“We are reducing variables,” Sen said.

“That sounds better if you don’t translate it.”

“Translate it.”

“You’re afraid I’ll do it again.”

Hale shifted.

Park looked at Sen.

Sen held Kade’s gaze.

“Yes.”

The answer surprised him enough to take the next joke out of his mouth.

Sen crossed the room and sat opposite him.

“We do not know whether what occurred was harmless, repeatable, or stable. We do know you experienced neurological symptoms afterward.”

“A headache.”

“And tremor. Pupil asymmetry. Temporal disorientation.”

“Everybody sounds more alarming in medical language.”

“Kade.”

He stopped.

Sen’s voice had not risen.

That made it harder to ignore.

“You are not being punished.”

“Feels similar.”

“I understand why.”

He almost laughed.

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

Something in the answer made him study her.

She looked tired.

Not tired from this morning.

Older than that.

Then it was gone.

Hale slid a revised schedule toward him.

“Physical conditioning. Standard academics. Non-field tactical work. Observation sessions. No unsupervised Resonance drills. No ranked challenges until cleared.”

Kade stared at the last line.

“No ranked challenges.”

“Correct.”

“I’m ten thousand four hundred eighty-two.”

“We know.”

“So I’m not allowed to move.”

“Temporarily.”

Kade stood.

The chair scraped hard enough to make Hale look up.

“Anything else?”

Sen’s eyes narrowed slightly.

“Yes.”

He waited.

“Do not confuse restriction with judgment.”

“That advice gets less useful every time somebody restricts me.”

He left before she could answer.

By lunch, half of Meridian knew.

By dinner, the whole first-year cohort did.

The rumor changed every twenty minutes.

Kade had overloaded an arena.

Kade had almost killed Priya.

Kade had failed a neurological screen.

Kade was secretly a government experiment.

Kade had cheated.

The last one bothered him most.

He found out because someone had written **ZERO BOY NEEDS TRAINING WHEELS** across the digital message wall outside Meridian Commons.

Eli reached for the panel controls.

Kade stopped him.

“Leave it.”

“Why?”

“Because deleting it makes them think it worked.”

“Did it?”

Kade looked at the words.

“Yes.”

Eli blinked.

Kade shrugged.

“Still leave it.”

That night, he went to the roof.

Mara was already there.

She stood near the east railing with a training tablet in one hand, replaying footwork patterns.

“You have a room,” Kade said.

“So do you.”

“Mine contains Eli.”

“Fair.”

He leaned on the railing.

Far below, the ocean struck Asterion’s breakwater in white lines.

Mara closed her tablet.

“They restricted you.”

“That rumor made it to rank seven?”

“It made it to the kitchens.”

“Good. I was worried about reach.”

She watched him.

“Do you think they’re wrong?”

Kade opened his mouth.

Closed it.

“I think they don’t know what I am.”

“Neither do you.”

“That’s less comforting when you say it.”

“Truth usually is.”

He looked at her.

“You practice being irritating?”

“No. Natural talent.”

This time he did laugh.

Mara turned toward the water.

“My father used to say restrictions reveal what you actually want.”

“Your father sounds exhausting.”

“He is.”

Kade waited.

She said nothing more.

“What’s that supposed to mean?” he asked.

“It means when something becomes difficult, most people discover they only wanted the idea of it.”

“And if I still want it?”

“Then find another way to get better.”

She picked up her tablet.

Before she left, she looked back.

“You saw Priya’s weight shift before she moved.”

Kade frowned.

“So?”

“You’re restricted from field training. Not from learning.”

Then she was gone.

Kade stayed on the roof another twenty minutes.

At 22:14, he opened the academy challenge rules.

At 22:19, he opened first-year combat recordings.

At 22:47, he found a loophole.

Ranked challenges required field clearance.

Unranked tactical simulations did not.

He smiled.

At 23:03, Eli woke to the light from Kade’s desk.

“What are you doing?”

“Learning.”

Eli sat up.

“That sounds dangerous when you say it.”

Kade dragged three fight recordings onto the screen.

“They want me last?”

Eli rubbed his eyes.

“I don’t think anyone said that.”

“Close enough.”

Kade looked at the rankings displayed in the corner.

**10,482 / 10,482**

For the first time since the number appeared, it did not feel like a verdict.

It felt like a starting line.

“Fine,” he said.

Eli blinked.

“Fine what?”

Kade smiled.

“I’ll climb without them.”

# Chapter 22 — Midyear: Mercer v. Vey

Kade knew he was in trouble when Mara smiled.

Not a large smile.

That would have been reassuring.

This was the small, precise smile she wore when a problem had finally become interesting.

They met in the quarterfinal arena under lights bright enough to erase the crowd beyond the barrier.

Mara entered ranked seventh.

Kade entered with a live tournament projection of two hundred eighty-six.

The announcer spent far too much time on the story.

Zero.

Meridian teammates.

House Trial partners.

Rising anomaly versus established elite.

Kade ignored all of it.

Mara did too.

The only difference was that Mara looked comfortable.

Kade looked at her stance.

Center balanced.

Front foot light.

Hands relaxed.

Nothing obvious.

Which meant everything was obvious and he simply had not found it yet.

The signal sounded.

Kade attacked first.

That was the mistake.

Mara gave him exactly the opening he expected.

A narrow lane inside her right guard.

He took it.

She was already moving to punish the response she knew would follow.

Kade changed angle.

She had changed before he did.

Her forearm redirected his shoulder, not hard, just enough to spoil his base.

Point Mara.

He reset.

She did not smile this time.

That worried him more.

Second exchange.

Kade waited.

Mara waited too.

He had spent months becoming more patient.

She had apparently planned for that.

She stepped forward with a deliberately imperfect entry.

Kade saw the flaw.

He also saw Julian in his head telling him not to solve the problem currently offered.

So he did nothing.

Mara withdrew.

No point.

The crowd murmured.

Kade smiled.

“Disappointed?” he asked.

“Not yet.”

Third exchange.

She attacked his rear side.

Kade countered.

Mara turned the counter into the next problem.

Then the next.

Three decisions later, Kade was on the mat.

He stared upward.

The ceiling lights were offensively calm.

Mara offered him a hand.

“Two-zero.”

“I can count.”

“You sometimes approximate.”

Kade took the hand and stood.

Now he understood.

Mara was not fighting him.

She was fighting the version of him three decisions from now.

Julian had trained Kade to make plans.

Mara had studied what those plans looked like once Kade started believing in them.

That was deeply unfair.

It was also excellent.

The next exchange lasted almost a minute.

Kade gave ground deliberately.

Mara refused to overextend.

He changed tempo.

She changed distance.

He planted a false retreat and caught her weight slightly forward.

For half a second, the arena opened.

He scored.

Two-one.

The Meridian section erupted.

Eli’s voice reached him through the noise even though that should not have been physically possible.

Mara reset.

This time she looked pleased.

“Better,” she said.

Kade breathed.

“That was praise.”

“Do not make it unpleasant.”

The fourth exchange began.

Mara attacked harder.

Kade absorbed one clean contact and circled.

The arena network flickered at the edge of his awareness.

Floor.

Anchors.

Walls.

Transfer paths.

The cold line started behind his eyes.

He could reach for Boundary architecture.

He knew the shape now.

Not well.

Enough.

Mara drove him toward the edge.

The crowd rose.

Kade felt the wall behind him as part of the system.

Asterion expected the strange thing.

He could almost feel thousands of people waiting for him to become interesting in the way they preferred.

He let it go.

The cold line faded.

Mara saw the hesitation.

Not the mechanism.

The choice.

She struck.

Kade hit the mat.

Point.

Three-one.

He sat up.

Mara remained several steps away.

“You could have used it,” she said quietly.

Kade glanced toward the crowd.

“You trying to get us both medically detained?”

“No.”

“Then I made the right choice.”

Mara nodded.

Something in her face changed.

Respect, maybe.

Or relief.

The final required margin was four points.

Kade returned to center.

If he lost the next exchange, it was over.

He tried something Julian had taught him.

A plan with a deliberate flaw.

He feinted high.

Mara saw the opening.

Of course she did.

Kade rolled under her counter and finally reached the line he had wanted since the first exchange.

Her balance shifted.

His hand found her forearm.

He turned.

For one beautiful fraction of a second, Mara Vey was exactly where he needed her.

Then she changed the future.

She released the arm Kade expected her to protect, stepped through the direction he had planned to send her, trapped his lead wrist against his own centerline, and redirected his momentum across her hip.

Kade understood the counter while he was airborne.

That was not useful.

He hit the mat flat on his back.

The buzzer sounded.

**WINNER — VEY, 4–1**

For half a second there was silence inside Kade’s head.

Then he laughed.

He could not help it.

Mara looked down at him.

“What?”

“That was awful.”

“You lost.”

“I noticed.”

“You sound happy.”

“I almost had the plan.”

“You almost had the wrong plan.”

“That is less encouraging.”

She offered him a hand.

Kade took it.

“You planned all of that?” he asked.

“Most.”

“How far ahead?”

“Far enough.”

“That is deeply irritating.”

“I know.”

They stood close for half a second.

Not romantic enough to deserve the noise the crowd made.

The crowd made it anyway.

Mara stepped back immediately.

Kade looked toward Meridian.

Eli was making a face Kade intended to remember during future opportunities for revenge.

Sera looked delighted by his suffering.

Taren was applauding with suspicious enthusiasm.

Julian, waiting for his semifinal, gave Kade a single approving nod.

When Kade left the arena, Julian intercepted him in the corridor.

“You finally trusted a plan,” Julian said.

“And lost.”

“You trusted one plan.”

Kade stared.

Julian smiled.

“Welcome to the next problem.”

Then he walked toward his semifinal.

Kade hated useful people.

They multiplied.

Mara went on to lose the semifinal to Julian by one point.

Kade watched from Meridian’s section.

She did not sulk afterward.

She watched the replay twice before sitting down.

He found that attractive.

He immediately decided not to examine why.

The final ranking recalculation posted that evening.

Tournament weighting included opponent rank, bracket depth, clean wins, penalty rate, and performance against elite competition. Reaching the quarterfinal from #1,904 produced a substantial adjustment even with the loss.

Kade’s tablet vibrated.

**KADE MERCER — RANK #211**

He stared.

Eli stared over his shoulder.

“That is a normal response to losing.”

“This ranking system is absurd.”

Mara sat across the Meridian common room with an ice wrap on one shoulder, reviewing Julian’s semifinal counter.

“You beat me,” Kade said.

“Yes.”

“I moved up seventeen hundred places.”

“Yes.”

“That does not bother you?”

“Your prior ranking lagged your demonstrated performance.”

“That sounded almost like a compliment.”

“It was mathematics.”

Kade looked at the screen again.

Two hundred eleven.

The number should have felt enormous.

Instead it felt strangely distant.

He had spent months believing the visible ladder was the mystery.

Now Aaron Mercer’s name existed in a classified archive under it.

Mara closed her replay.

“You’re thinking about your father.”

Kade looked at her.

“You are irritating.”

“I know.”

He sat across from her.

“Do you ever wonder if the rankings are partly camouflage?”

“For what?”

“All of this.”

He gestured vaguely at the campus beyond the windows.

“Ten thousand students. Constant testing. Every fight recorded. Every mistake categorized.”

Mara considered it.

“Measurement can have more than one purpose.”

Kade thought of Sen saying rankings made performance legible.

Students understood them.

Coaches understood them.

Governments especially understood them.

His new rank remained on the screen.

#211.

A visible answer.

Somewhere beneath it, someone had erased his father from history.

Kade was beginning to understand that Asterion had always contained two ladders.

One everyone could see.

And one people had gone to extraordinary lengths to hide.

# Chapter 1 — The Kid in Room Four

Kade Mercer had been at Northline Alternative for forty-three school days, which was long enough to know that Room Four had three reliable clocks and none of them told time.

The first was the white plastic clock above the door. Its second hand twitched twice for every second and stopped whenever the heater kicked on.

The second was Ms. Alvarez’s coffee. Full meant first period. Half meant lunch was getting close. Empty meant nobody should ask her anything unless someone was bleeding.

The third was Darius Bell.

At exactly 10:17 every morning, Darius decided he had endured enough education for one day, stood up, announced a destination that was obviously false, and walked out.

Today he made it to 10:19.

“Bathroom,” Darius said.

Ms. Alvarez did not look up from the district tablet in her hands.

“You went fourteen minutes ago.”

“Different bathroom.”

“There is one bathroom.”

Darius considered that.

“Then I forgot something in it.”

Across the room, Kade kept his eyes on his own tablet and moved his thumb over the blank answer field without touching it.

The question read:

A cargo tram with a mass of 18,000 kilograms accelerates from rest to 12 meters per second over 8 seconds. What average force is required?

It was a stupid question.

Not mathematically. Mathematically it was almost insulting.

The problem was the word required.

Required by what?

If the tram was on rails, friction mattered. If it was magnetic, the field geometry mattered. If twelve meters per second was measured relative to the track, fine, but if the track itself was moving—

Kade exhaled through his nose and typed the answer they wanted.

27,000 N

Green check.

A cartoon rocket ship appeared and performed a tiny victory loop.

Kade hated the rocket ship.

“Mercer.”

He looked up.

Ms. Alvarez pointed at Darius without moving anything but her eyes.

Darius had reached the door.

Kade looked at him.

“You know she has to log it if you leave again.”

Darius’s hand stopped on the push bar.

“Don’t start.”

“I didn’t.”

“You got that face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you think you’re smarter than everybody.”

Kade leaned back in his chair.

“I don’t have enough facial muscles for that.”

A laugh escaped from somewhere behind him.

Darius did not smile.

He was sixteen, two inches taller than Kade, and built like the district had accidentally enrolled a linebacker in remedial geometry. He had been suspended from Canyon Ridge High after putting another kid through the glass panel beside a classroom door. The other kid needed eleven stitches.

Darius needed none.

Northline had taught Kade that none of those facts meant what people assumed they meant.

“I’m leaving,” Darius said.

“Okay.”

“You gonna stop me?”

“No.”

That irritated him more.

Kade turned his tablet around just enough for Darius to see the red banner in the upper corner.

ATTENDANCE CONTRACT: 4/5 UNEXCUSED EXITS

Darius stared at it.

“One more,” Kade said, “and Russell sends you back to review.”

“So?”

“So your review is Friday.”

Darius folded the attendance contract until the warning disappeared inside the crease.

Kade knew about Friday because Darius had spent most of the previous afternoon pretending not to care whether his mother could get off work for the meeting.

“If they push it another week,” Kade said, “your mom misses another shift for nothing.”

Darius looked toward Ms. Alvarez.

She was studying her tablet with the intense concentration of someone who had absolutely not heard the conversation.

“Sit down,” Kade said. “Walk out at lunch and call it rebellion.”

Darius stared at him for another two seconds.

Then he came back to his chair.

“Still hate you,” he muttered.

“I know.”

Ms. Alvarez lifted her coffee and drank.

Still half full.

Not lunch yet.

She set the cup beside her tablet.

“Since Mr. Bell has decided to remain enrolled for another eleven minutes, we are going to use them.”

Darius looked offended.

“I remained enrolled all morning.”

“Physically.”

Ms. Alvarez touched her screen. The cargo-tram problem appeared on the wall display, enlarged until the cartoon tram was nearly the size of an actual small car.

Three students groaned.

Kade looked down at the green check on his tablet.

Ms. Alvarez said, “Who got twenty-seven thousand newtons?”

Almost every hand went up.

“Who got something else?”

Darius raised his hand halfway.

“I got a red box.”

“That is not a unit of force.”

“It is what the program gave me.”

Ms. Alvarez waited.

Darius sighed and turned his tablet around. He had multiplied eighteen thousand by twelve and divided by eight, but entered the number as twenty-seven hundred.

Kade said, “You dropped a zero.”

“I had plenty.”

“Apparently not.”

Darius pulled his tablet back. “This is why people leave.”

“Correct the answer first,” Ms. Alvarez said. “Then you may blame the institution.”

While Darius fixed it, she tapped the wall display again. A second line appeared under the question.

WHAT INFORMATION IS MISSING?

The raised hands came down.

Kade stopped moving his thumb.

Ms. Alvarez looked directly at him.

He kept his face empty.

“Mercer.”

“The software says I’m right.”

“The software also celebrates basic arithmetic with a rocket ship. I am asking you.”

He glanced at the clock above the door. Its second hand twitched uselessly.

“Friction,” he said.

Ms. Alvarez wrote it on the display.

“What else?”

“Track grade. Drive efficiency. Whether twelve meters per second is relative to the track. Whether the cargo shifts under acceleration.”

More words appeared as she wrote.

Kade wished she would stop making the list visible.

“Wheel condition,” he continued. “Wind, if this is outside. Maximum force versus average force. Whether the tram is self-propelled or being pulled. Whether the eighteen thousand includes the vehicle.”

Darius looked from the list to the cartoon tram.

“That thing has wheels?”

“It has something pretending to be wheels.”

“Then the load moves.” Darius pointed at the cargo boxes stacked in the picture. “You hit speed too fast, all that comes through the front.”

Kade looked at him.

Darius shrugged. “You ever watch a shopping cart stop?”

Ms. Alvarez added LOAD SECUREMENT beneath Kade’s list.

“Good,” she said.

Darius sat a little straighter.

Kade looked back at the display.

The problem had seemed stupid because it ignored everything that could make the answer useful. Darius had found one of those things without needing the equation at all.

Ms. Alvarez drew a box around twenty-seven thousand.

“This is the answer the question requests. Under the stated simplification, it is correct.”

Then she drew a larger box around the missing-information list.

“This is the answer that keeps someone from loading a real tram badly and blaming mathematics when it fails.”

One of the students near the windows said, “Which answer is on the test?”

“The small box.”

“Then why do we need the big one?”

Ms. Alvarez capped the stylus.

“Because sometimes passing a test and understanding a system are different jobs.”

Kade stared at the two boxes.

That sounded suspiciously like something a school was not supposed to admit.

Darius tapped the missing-information list.

“So he did all that in his head and still put the same answer as everybody else?”

“Yes,” Ms. Alvarez said.

“That seems unhealthy.”

“It is occasionally exhausting.”

Kade leaned back.

“I’m sitting here.”

“Physically,” Darius said.

Two people laughed.

Ms. Alvarez assigned the next problem. This one involved a water tank, an outlet valve, and a flow rate that assumed water behaved politely.

Kade entered the requested answer.

Then, before the rocket ship could finish its loop, he opened the notes field and typed the assumptions the question had left out.

He did not look toward Ms. Alvarez.

She did not say anything.

Darius leaned across the gap between their desks.

“What did the water do wrong?”

Kade angled the screen away.

“Nothing.”

“Then why are you writing a complaint?”

Kade glanced at the drawing. “The tank has no height. The outlet has no diameter. They gave a flow rate but not whether it stays constant as the level drops.”

Darius considered the stained ceiling tile above them.

“If it’s Northline water, the pipe is clogged.”

“Probably.”

Kade added PIPE CONDITION.

Darius nodded as though this settled the matter and returned to his own tablet.

For the first time since the rocket ship appeared, Kade did not want to close the problem.

Northline Alternative occupied an old county records building between a tire warehouse and a self-storage facility. Someone had painted the exterior blue three years earlier, but the desert sun had bleached the south wall to a color best described as disappointed gray.

The district website called it a personalized transitional learning campus.

Students called it Northline.

Parents called it whatever they needed to call it when relatives asked where their kid went to school now.

There were forty-two students enrolled, divided among six classrooms. Some had been expelled. Some had been arrested. Some had stopped showing up at regular school until the district stopped pretending they would. A few had panic disorders, complicated families, pregnancies, probation officers, jobs, addictions, or combinations of all six.

Northline did not have a mascot.

It had a metal detector, two social workers, one security officer who spent most afternoons fixing bicycles, and a vending machine that occasionally dispensed three bags of pretzels for the price of one.

Nobody knew why.

Darius believed the machine respected him.

Kade had none of the obvious reasons for being there.

That was, according to Vice Principal Russell, part of his problem.

At seventeen, Kade had collected three suspensions, two school transfers, a semester of failed classes he could have passed, and enough teacher comments to assemble a personality entirely from administrative adjectives.

Bright but inconsistent.

Argumentative.

Fails to demonstrate appropriate investment.

Challenges authority.

Does not utilize available opportunities.

His favorite came from ninth grade.

Kade often appears disengaged even when he understands the material.

As if understanding it should have made pretending to enjoy it easier.

He had read the whole file once while Russell was out of the office.

Not hacked it.

That was an important distinction.

Russell had left the file open on his desk while he went to make a phone call, which in Kade’s opinion transformed unauthorized access into poor information security.

Ms. Alvarez disagreed.

They had argued about that for eleven minutes.

Kade had enjoyed the argument.

She had not.

Probably.

At 10:31, the classroom speaker clicked.

Every head lifted.

Northline’s speaker system usually worked only when nobody wanted it to.

“Attention, students,” Russell’s voice said. “All seventeen-year-old students scheduled for the National Aptitude Ascension Examination should report to Multipurpose Two at eleven hundred hours. Bring your district identification card. Testing begins promptly at eleven fifteen.”

Darius groaned.

“You’re sixteen,” Kade said.

“I’m groaning in solidarity.”

“That’s new.”

“I’m growing.”

The speaker clicked off.

Ms. Alvarez set down her tablet.

“Mercer.”

Kade looked at her.

“You have your ID?”

“Somewhere.”

“Find somewhere.”

He patted the pocket of his hoodie.

The card was there.

“Miracle,” she said.

“You set your expectations correctly.”

A couple students laughed.

Ms. Alvarez did not.

That was unusual.

Kade watched her.

“You nervous?”

“For you?”

“For the national integrity of standardized testing.”

“No.”

“Good.”

“I am, however, concerned you’ll decide the instructions are philosophically inconsistent and refuse to answer question twelve.”

“That happened once.”

“Twice.”

“The second one really was inconsistent.”

Her mouth twitched.

Kade counted that as a win.

“Just take the test,” she said.

He shrugged.

The Aptitude Ascension Examination had been around longer than Kade had been alive. Every seventeen-year-old in the country took some version of it. The official explanation was that it measured academic reasoning, spatial cognition, reaction speed, systems intuition, stress adaptation, and a dozen other things useful for university admissions, technical apprenticeships, military programs, and Resonance screening.

That last category was why people cared.

Thirty-one years after Resonance entered the world, civilization had reorganized itself around the tiny percentage of human beings who could do things everyone else could only watch.

They stopped collapsing bridges.

Redirected aircraft during emergencies.

Worked orbital construction.

Stabilized coastal barriers during storms.

Competed in arenas watched by hundreds of millions.

Wore Conduits worth more than Northline’s entire building.

And every year, a few teenagers walked into testing centers as ordinary kids and walked out with numbers that changed their lives.

Kade did not expect to be one of them.

He had learned to distrust any institution promising to reveal what he was capable of.

Institutions had been explaining what he was capable of for years.

Mostly they seemed disappointed.

A soft chime sounded from the teacher’s station.

Ms. Alvarez checked the message and looked around Room Four.

“All right. Ten-minute break before testing. Stay on campus. Nobody set anything on fire.”

Darius raised a hand.

“Clarification.”

“No.”

“You didn’t hear the question.”

“I know you.”

Chairs scraped back.

Northline breaks had rules nobody had written down.

Students with jobs checked schedules.

Students on probation checked whether their officers had messaged.

People who wanted to be alone went behind the old records annex where the cameras covered the door but not the wall.

Everybody else migrated toward the vending machines.

Darius grabbed Kade’s sleeve.

“Come witness history.”

“I have testing.”

“In twenty minutes.”

“Exactly.”

“The machine knows.”

Kade allowed himself to be dragged into the hall.

Mr. Boyd sat behind the security desk with the front wheel removed from a student’s bicycle.

The bicycle belonged to a fourteen-year-old named Miguel whose family did not own a car and whose rear brake had failed on the ride to school.

Boyd looked up as Kade and Darius passed.

“Testing day.”

Kade nodded.

“Try not to overthrow anything federal.”

“No promises.”

Boyd returned to the brake cable.

Northline had more adults doing things outside their job descriptions than any school Kade had attended.

Sometimes that meant nobody knew who was responsible.

Sometimes it meant a security officer fixed a kid’s bicycle because the kid needed to get home.

Kade had not decided which version of the system was more honest.

The vending machine waited near the multipurpose hallway.

Darius approached it with ceremonial seriousness.

“One pretzel.”

“You say that like it understands language.”

“It understands respect.”

Darius inserted two credits and pressed B7.

The machine hummed.

One bag fell.

Darius’s face collapsed.

“Betrayal.”

A second bag dropped.

Then a third.

Darius pointed at Kade without looking away.

“Witness.”

“I withdraw every criticism.”

Tessa arrived in time to steal one bag.

Darius looked offended.

“The machine chose me.”

“The machine chose community,” she said.

“That sounds socialist.”

“You don’t know what that means.”

“I know I have fewer pretzels.”

Kade took none.

His stomach had begun feeling strange in a way he refused to label nervousness.

Tessa opened her bag.

“What do you think you’ll score?”

“Between zero and whatever the maximum is.”

“That confident?”

“I like to leave room for outcomes.”

“My cousin got seven-twelve last year.”

“Is that good?”

She stared at him.

“You know it is.”

He did.

Everyone did.

Scores above seven hundred triggered scholarship calls. Eight hundred brought recruiters. Nine hundred became local news.

The highest score ever recorded was supposedly nine sixty-eight, though there were arguments online about whether the scoring system had changed since then.

Northline students did not generally contribute to that debate.

Tessa leaned closer.

“My aunt says anything over six fifty and they test you for Conduit compatibility.”

“Your aunt also says the moon landing was filmed in Nevada.”

“That was my other aunt.”

“You have a complicated family.”

She gave him a look.

Darius ate pretzels.

“What’d your mom score?” he asked Kade.

Kade frowned.

“How would I know?”

“Parents tell you that stuff.”

“Your mother tells you her standardized test scores?”

“She tells me everything I do is genetic when she is mad.”

Tessa said, “My dad got five-ninety-eight and still talks about it.”

“That explains your family more than the moon thing,” Kade said.

She threw a pretzel at him.

Darius looked back at Kade.

“What about your dad?”

The question was ordinary.

That was why it landed.

Kade shrugged.

“He died when I was a baby.”

Darius’s face changed.

Not pity.

A quick recalculation.

“Oh.”

“Industrial accident.”

“You know his score?”

“No.”

Kade had never wondered.

Aaron Mercer existed in family history as a photograph, a broken watch, and a short list of stories Elena told carefully enough that Kade had stopped asking for new ones years ago.

A national test score had never been part of the story.

For some reason, standing outside Multipurpose Two on the day of his own test, that absence felt briefly strange.

Then Darius held out the remaining pretzel bag.

Kade took one.

Conversation over.

That was one of the useful things about food. People thought they were sharing snacks.

Mostly they were also giving you an exit.

Tessa checked the hall clock.

“You’re going to be late.”

Kade looked.

10:54.

He handed Darius the pretzel bag.

“Try not to get expelled while I’m testing.”

“No promises.”

At 10:56, Ms. Alvarez stopped him at the classroom door while he retrieved his hoodie.

The others filed past toward Multipurpose Two.

“Kade.”

He turned.

She leaned against the teacher’s desk with her arms folded.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That’s usually not why people say my name like that.”

She studied him.

“You know what I think your problem is?”

“Alphabetical or ranked?”

“You decide something doesn’t matter before you find out whether you’re good at it.”

His smile stayed in place.

“Efficient.”

“No.”

The word landed harder than it should have.

Ms. Alvarez nodded toward the hallway.

“Go take the test.”

He turned away.

“And Kade?”

He looked back.

“Try.”

He almost made a joke.

For some reason, he didn’t.

Multipurpose Two had once been the county archive room.

The shelves were gone, replaced by twenty testing stations separated by collapsible privacy panels. Eleven Northline students sat in assigned seats while two district proctors checked identification cards.

Kade took Station Seven.

The testing tablet was larger than the ones used in class. A black sensor band rested beside it, thin enough to look harmless and expensive enough that Kade resisted the urge to ask what would happen if he dropped it.

A proctor stopped beside him.

“Temple contacts here and here,” the man said, tapping the band. “Rear contacts behind the ears. Do not remove it during a section unless instructed.”

“I’ve used one.”

“Then this should go beautifully.”

Kade looked at him.

The proctor smiled.

Kade decided he liked him.

He fitted the band around his head.

Cool contacts settled against his temples.

The screen illuminated.

NATIONAL APTITUDE ASCENSION EXAMINATION

Candidate: MERCER, KADE A.

Age: 17

Identity verified.

Below it, a block of legal text appeared.

Kade scrolled.

And scrolled.

And scrolled.

“Does anybody read this?” he asked.

The proctor did not look up.

“No.”

“Comforting.”

“Government efficiency.”

Kade pressed ACCEPT.

A countdown appeared.

Five.

Four.

Three.

He rubbed his thumb against the edge of the desk.

Two.

Ms. Alvarez’s voice returned unexpectedly in his head.

Try.

One.

The first section was ordinary.

Pattern recognition.

Language.

Mathematics.

Kade moved quickly.

He had always been good at tests when he cared enough to finish them.

Sentence relationships. Rotating shapes. Conditional logic. Probability.

A few questions were difficult enough to be interesting.

Most were not.

Section four switched to dynamic systems.

A simulated suspension bridge oscillated in crosswind. Kade had to choose where to add damping.

Easy.

A municipal water network ruptured. He had to isolate the failure while preserving pressure to a hospital district.

Better.

A transport grid lost three substations during an evacuation. He had ninety seconds to reroute power and traffic simultaneously.

He forgot to be bored.

That annoyed him a little.

The questions stopped feeling like school.

For once, the test seemed to be asking what he actually saw when he looked at a problem.

Not whether he remembered an equation.

What failed first?

What could be sacrificed?

What looked important but wasn’t?

Where was the load really going?

He leaned closer to the screen.

In one simulation, three emergency routes converged at a bridge rated below projected capacity.

The obvious answer was to reroute traffic.

Kade left the traffic alone and shut down two commercial substations instead, darkening several blocks and freeing enough grid capacity to accelerate a rail evacuation line.

The simulation paused.

A message appeared.

CIVILIAN IMPACT PENALTY: -8.3%

Then:

SURVIVAL PROJECTION: +14.7%

Kade stared at it.

“Rude,” he whispered.

He accepted the score.

At some point, the first students began finishing.

Kade noticed because a chair scraped behind him.

Then another.

He kept going.

The room thinned.

Section five was reaction timing.

Section six was adaptive reasoning.

Kade expected the system to become harder.

Instead it became stranger.

Questions stopped presenting all the information at once.

A collision simulation changed mass after he committed to an answer.

A pressure network hid two sensors.

A structural problem gave him three plausible assumptions and penalized him for choosing any of them too early.

That last one made him smile.

Finally.

A test that admitted the problem might be the problem.

Then Section Seven loaded.

There was no title.

Only one sentence.

Identify the boundary of the active system.

A diagram appeared.

A steel sphere hung from a cable inside a transparent chamber. The chamber sat on a platform. The platform rested on a larger floor grid. Colored arrows indicated force vectors.

Kade frowned.

There was no question beyond the instruction.

He waited.

Nothing happened.

He tapped the sphere.

The tablet chimed.

Incomplete.

He tapped the chamber.

Incomplete.

The platform.

Incomplete.

Kade leaned back.

The sensor contacts at his temples felt colder now.

He looked at the diagram again.

The drawing wanted him to pretend the lines around the objects mattered.

Sphere.

Cable.

Chamber.

Platform.

Floor.

Nice clean boxes.

But the cable attached to the chamber ceiling. The chamber transferred load to the platform. The platform transferred force into the floor. The floor into the foundation. The foundation into the earth.

And if the sphere moved, energy went into all of it.

“That’s not a system,” Kade whispered.

The proctor glanced over.

Kade ignored him.

He dragged his finger around the sphere, cable, chamber and platform.

The selection glowed.

Incomplete.

He expanded it to include the floor grid.

The screen flickered.

Just once.

Kade stopped.

He removed his finger.

The diagram returned to normal.

He drew the same boundary again.

Another flicker.

Not random.

Kade’s pulse changed.

He expanded the selection beyond the floor grid.

The interface refused.

He tried again.

Nothing.

Then he noticed a tiny change at the edge of the diagram.

The selection handle had moved.

Before, it snapped to objects.

Now it floated between them.

Kade touched it.

The boundary moved.

Anywhere.

He sat up.

The room seemed quieter.

He drew a line that cut diagonally through the platform.

The screen accepted it.

He drew upward, excluding one support and including the other.

Accepted.

Across the chamber wall.

Accepted.

Through the cable.

Accepted.

Around half the sphere.

The screen went black.

Kade froze.

For one full second, he saw only his own reflection.

Then a single word appeared in white.

REDEFINE

His heartbeat kicked.

“What?”

The word vanished.

A new simulation loaded.

No section header.

No instructions beyond:

Minimize occupant impulse.

A vehicle struck a barrier.

Kade selected the crumple zones.

83.1%

He reset it.

Included the barrier.

91.4%

Again.

Included a section of roadway.

96.0%

Again.

This time he excluded the vehicle’s rear axle from the active transfer network.

The screen hesitated.

For half a second, the numbers dissolved into static.

Then:

99.98%

Kade stared.

A pressure began behind his eyes.

Not pain.

Not yet.

More like the sensation of remembering a word he had never learned.

The next problem loaded before he touched anything.

A pressure vessel.

Prevent rupture.

He changed the boundary.

A power grid.

Preserve critical load.

Boundary.

A falling structure.

Minimize transferred impulse.

Boundary.

Heat.

Charge.

Fluid flow.

Collisions.

The questions came faster.

At first Kade answered them.

Then he started anticipating them.

The interface seemed to anticipate him back.

Every problem asked the same hidden question in different clothes.

Where did the thing end?

And every time, Kade’s answer became simpler.

It ended wherever you stopped counting.

The pressure behind his eyes sharpened.

He blinked hard.

A timer in the corner showed 87:13.

That could not be right.

The standard exam block was ninety minutes total.

He had already been testing longer than that.

He looked toward the proctor.

The man was standing very still.

Not bored-still.

Watching-still.

Kade looked back at the screen.

The timer disappeared.

A new problem appeared.

No diagram this time.

Only a blank gray field and one sentence.

Define the active system.

Kade waited.

Nothing appeared.

No objects.

No vectors.

No boundaries.

“Seriously?” he whispered.

His first instinct was to tap the whole screen.

He stopped himself.

What active system?

The tablet?

The testing station?

The room?

Him?

He looked at his hand resting on the desk.

At the cable running from the tablet into the floor.

At the privacy panels.

At the proctor.

At the sensor band touching his skin.

Something cold moved down his back.

Kade placed two fingers on the blank screen and drew a boundary around nothing.

The tablet vibrated once.

The gray field vanished.

SECTION COMPLETE

He sat there for several seconds.

Then the normal testing interface returned as if nothing had happened.

At the top:

SCORING…

Kade looked around.

Multipurpose Two was almost empty.

One student remained at the far end, packing her bag.

A proctor stood by the door.

The other stood behind Kade’s station, three meters away.

“How long was I in that section?” Kade asked.

The closer proctor looked at the wall clock.

Then at him.

“Long enough.”

“That sounds official.”

The man did not smile.

Kade removed the sensor band.

His skin felt cool where the contacts had rested.

A drop of sweat moved from his hairline toward his temple.

For the first time all morning, Kade wanted the score before he had a joke prepared for it.

He thought of Ms. Alvarez saying *try*.

Darius asking whether he ever imagined getting picked.

Tessa’s cousin getting seven-twelve.

His mother at St. Vincent, probably moving from one emergency-room problem to another without thinking about national aptitude scores at all.

And, unexpectedly, the question from the vending machine hall.

What did your dad score?

Kade still did not know.

It was a stupid detail to think about now.

He looked at the spinning word SCORING and realized something more uncomfortable.

If the number was good, he wanted to tell Elena.

If it was terrible, he wanted to tell Darius first so Darius could make it funny.

The test had become real enough to create an order of operations for who got the truth.

That meant he cared.

Annoying.

The door opened.

Darius leaned his head inside.

“You alive?”

“Debatable.”

“Food’s gone.”

“Tragedy.”

“Alvarez said I had to come get you because apparently abandoning people in government testing facilities is frowned upon.”

“Authoritarian.”

Darius stepped into the room.

The proctor opened his mouth.

Darius pointed behind himself.

“She said.”

The proctor apparently knew Ms. Alvarez well enough to accept that as documentation.

Darius stopped beside Kade.

“What’d you get?”

“It’s scoring.”

“You think you’re Resonant?”

“No.”

“That sounded fast.”

“Because I’ve had seventeen years to think about it.”

“You’d be unbearable with powers.”

“That’s fair.”

“You’d stop bullets and explain why everybody was standing wrong.”

“I’d probably start with your posture.”

Darius laughed.

The scoring icon vanished.

Kade turned back to the screen.

For a moment he thought it had failed to load.

The page was almost empty.

No percentile.

No section breakdown.

No recommendations.

Then three digits appeared in the center.

000

Darius went silent.

Kade stared at the number.

The proctor behind him inhaled sharply.

That was interesting.

“Wow.”

Kade nodded slowly.

“Honestly, kind of impressive.”

“You got zero.”

“I noticed.”

“Like zero zero.”

“There are three of them. It’s a strong commitment.”

Darius barked a laugh.

Kade smiled too.

Because that was what you did.

You made the joke before somebody else could.

You made failure funny enough that nobody had to ask whether it hurt.

Three schools.

Three suspensions.

A semester he could have passed.

Forty-three days at Northline.

And now the grand national machine designed to identify hidden potential had looked at Kade Mercer and returned the cleanest possible answer.

Nothing.

He waited for the embarrassment.

It came.

Not hard.

Just familiar.

A little pressure beneath the ribs.

A little heat at the back of the neck.

The old instinct arrived with it.

Doesn’t matter.

He could already hear himself telling Ms. Alvarez.

Stupid test.

Bad scoring.

Government software.

He could make the whole thing irrelevant before he ever had to decide whether he had cared.

He looked at the three zeros.

Ms. Alvarez’s voice came back again.

You decide something doesn’t matter before you find out whether you’re good at it.

Kade’s smile faded.

For once, he had tried.

That was the annoying part.

He had actually tried.

Behind him, the proctor said, very quietly, “Don’t touch anything.”

Kade turned.

“What?”

The lights went out.

Not dimmed.

Out.

The ventilation stopped.

Every tablet in Multipurpose Two turned black at the same instant.

Darius’s laugh died.

Emergency lights should have come on.

They didn’t.

For two seconds, the room was completely dark.

Kade heard somebody inhale.

Then every testing screen illuminated white.

No logos.

No controls.

Just a single line of black text.

SESSION TERMINATED

The proctor muttered something sharp.

His wrist terminal began vibrating.

Then the other proctor’s.

Then Darius’s phone.

Then Kade’s.

All at once.

In the hallway, someone shouted.

The wall display rebooted.

A district network map appeared.

One red indicator flashed over Northline.

Then another.

Then five.

The map zoomed out without anyone touching it.

City level.

Red.

County level.

More red.

State.

The screen paused.

Then zoomed again.

Kade stopped breathing for a second.

Red indicators multiplied across the western states like infection spreading through tissue.

The proctor moved to the wall display.

“What is that?” Darius asked.

Nobody answered.

The map widened to the whole country.

Testing centers vanished one after another.

Not offline.

Terminated.

Kade’s phone buzzed again.

A government testing notification filled the screen.

NATIONAL APTITUDE SERVICE INTERRUPTION

RESULTS TEMPORARILY UNAVAILABLE

He looked back at Station Seven.

His screen had changed.

It was the only terminal in the room not showing SESSION TERMINATED.

It still displayed his score.

000

The proctor saw it.

His face lost color.

Kade noticed because noticing people was easy when they were trying very hard not to react.

The man took one step backward.

Not from the tablet.

From Kade.

Darius noticed too.

“What?” he said.

The proctor looked toward the door.

His wrist terminal vibrated again.

This time he answered immediately.

“Northline testing center,” he said.

Pause.

“Yes.”

Another pause.

His eyes moved to Kade.

“No, the candidate is still here.”

Kade’s stomach tightened.

Darius looked at him.

The proctor turned slightly away, as though privacy still mattered.

“Yes. Mercer. Kade Mercer.”

Silence.

The voice on the other end said something Kade could not hear.

The proctor’s face changed.

Not confusion.

Recognition.

Then fear.

“Yes, sir.”

He ended the call.

Nobody spoke.

Kade looked from the proctor to his screen.

Three zeros.

He tried to make the joke.

Nothing came.

Outside Multipurpose Two, doors opened up and down the hallway. Teachers called students back into rooms. Phones chimed. Somewhere, Russell was arguing loudly with someone about district emergency procedures.

Inside, Station Seven continued glowing.

000

Kade had spent most of his life being told that numbers measured things.

Grades.

Attendance.

Suspensions.

Risk.

Potential.

Success.

Failure.

This was the first number he had ever received that seemed to frighten the people who had given it to him.

And for the first time that day, Kade wondered if zero meant something other than nothing.

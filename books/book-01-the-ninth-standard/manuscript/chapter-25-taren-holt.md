# Chapter 25 — Taren Holt

Taren was the one who knew how to get into places he was not supposed to be.

That should have worried Kade sooner.

In hindsight, there were many things that should have worried him sooner.

Taren knowing which upper-year trainer had an opening before the trainer posted it.

Taren knowing when Kade’s restricted footage moved between departments.

Taren appearing at Meridian Night with information from three different Houses and no explanation for where any of it came from.

Taren texting Mara within minutes of her skipping dinner.

Taren knowing which lift would be closed for maintenance before the maintenance notice appeared.

At the time, it was simply Taren being Taren.

Useful.

Connected.

Annoying in ways that made life easier.

Now Kade had learned about the Continuity Directorate.

Old observations rearranged themselves.

That was the problem with new information.

It made the past unstable too.

Three days after Sen’s Contact Zero disclosure, Taren found Kade alone on the Meridian roof.

Kade had gone there to avoid everybody.

This worked for approximately four minutes.

Taren appeared carrying two canned drinks.

“You look suspiciously reflective.”

Kade did not take the offered drink.

“Why do you know so much?”

Taren stopped.

The question had arrived without preamble.

Good.

Kade was tired of preambles.

Taren looked toward the ocean.

“About what?”

“Everything.”

“That is a broad accusation.”

“You knew my combat schedule before I did. You knew where restricted footage went. You know people’s training injuries, calendar changes, faculty appointments.”

“People tell me things.”

“Why?”

Taren smiled faintly.

“Because I listen.”

“That is your answer every time.”

“It remains true.”

Kade studied him.

Taren did not look away.

For months, Kade had treated Taren’s social ease as almost the opposite of secrecy.

Somebody that open could not be hiding much.

That assumption embarrassed him now.

“What is the Continuity Directorate?” Kade asked.

Taren’s face changed.

Barely.

Enough.

Kade’s next breath stopped halfway.

Taren recovered quickly.

“You heard Sen mention it?”

“Answer.”

“A government continuity group.”

“That is the public sentence.”

“That is all most people get.”

“You know another one.”

Taren looked down at the unopened drink in his hand.

“Probably.”

“Probably?”

“Kade.”

Kade set the unopened drink on the ledge between them.

“Not this time.”

Taren looked up.

The easy expression was gone.

For one second someone much more tired showed through.

Then footsteps sounded near the roof door.

Two Meridian students emerged laughing.

Taren’s face changed back instantly.

“Kade wants to know whether the Directorate runs secret assassins,” he said loudly.

One student laughed.

“Obviously.”

The other said, “That’s just tax auditors.”

They walked past.

Taren took a drink.

Kade stared at him.

“You did that on purpose.”

“I did.”

“Why?”

“Because not every conversation belongs in public.”

“That sounds familiar.”

Taren winced.

The wince came before Taren could hide it.

Then Taren said, very quietly, “Do not go looking for Directorate files alone.”

Kade’s anger sharpened.

“That is extremely specific.”

“I know.”

“What are you not telling me?”

Taren looked toward the roof door.

“More than I should.”

Then he left.

Kade did not follow.

He should have.

---

The opportunity arrived the next afternoon.

Mara had found references to a restricted archive collection under old eastern administration. Sen’s limited historical clearance let them access Contact Zero summaries and precursor technical records.

It did not permit the Continuity Directorate application files.

Eli considered this an invitation to complain about database design.

Mara considered it evidence that the files mattered.

Sera considered the entire plan unwise.

Kade considered all three positions compatible.

They met in Meridian’s small strategy room after classes.

Mara put the search list on the wall.

Aaron Mercer.

Continuity Directorate.

Project Consensus.

Asterion precursor site.

“We have legal access to the historical index,” Mara said. “Not all attached records.”

Eli raised a hand.

“How illegal are we willing to become?”

Sera looked at him.

“None.”

“That was not one of the quantitative options.”

“We use Sen’s authorization,” Mara said. “If the system blocks us, we stop.”

Kade looked at her.

“You believe that?”

“I expect us to stop. Belief is different.”

“Good.”

The door opened.

Taren entered.

Nobody had invited him.

Sera’s expression changed first.

Taren looked at the search list.

Then at Kade.

“You’re going into eastern archive.”

It was not a question.

Kade said, “How did you know?”

Taren sighed.

“Because that is where those collections are.”

“Most students do not know that.”

“I am not most students.”

“Not helping.”

Taren closed the door behind him.

“I can get you through the outer access.”

Silence.

Sera folded her arms.

“Why?”

“Because your clearance will fail at the biometric bridge.”

Eli frowned.

“How do you know that?”

Taren looked at him.

“Because the archive was built before the current student-access model.”

“That explains the system, not your knowledge.”

Taren's automatic smile reached for the space between them and failed before it formed.

Kade remembered the roof.

*Do not go looking for Directorate files alone.*

“Who are you?” Kade asked.

Taren smiled automatically.

The smile failed halfway.

“Your friend.”

“You know what I’m asking.”

“It was not.”

Sera stepped between Taren and the door.

“You do not come with us unless you tell us why you can open it.”

Taren looked at all four of them.

For several seconds, Kade thought he might leave.

Instead Taren sat.

“My family works around international security.”

“That is public,” Mara said.

“My mother is diplomatic security. My uncle worked continuity analysis.”

The word struck harder than the family history around it.

“Directorate.”

Taren nodded once.

“Formerly.”

“And you have access because of him?”

Taren hesitated.

Kade caught it.

“Not exactly.”

Sera said, “We’re done.”

She reached for the door.

Taren stood.

“Wait.”

Nobody moved.

He looked at Kade.

“If you go without me, the outer archive logs your clearance before you know the deeper section rejects it.”

“And if we go with you?” Mara asked.

“I can route you through a legacy maintenance entrance that does not touch student access control.”

Eli’s eyes narrowed.

“That is not information you inherit from an uncle.”

“It is not.”

There it was.

A truth shaped like an opening.

Kade stared at Taren.

“Tell me now.”

Taren looked away.

“I can’t.”

“Then we don’t go.”

The words surprised everyone.

Including Kade.

A few months ago curiosity would have beaten caution in under a second.

Now he could see the system around the choice.

Mara’s father.

Directorate interest.

Sen’s warning.

Taren’s impossible access.

Not enough information.

Taren looked back at him.

Something like relief passed across his face.

Then fear.

“They already know you found the twelfth identifier,” he said.

The room went still.

Kade’s voice became quiet.

“How?”

Taren closed his eyes.

“I don’t know.”

“You’re lying.”

“I’m not lying about that.”

“How do you know they know?”

Taren opened his eyes.

“Because somebody asked me what you were looking for.”

Sera moved closer.

“Who?”

Taren did not answer.

Mara said, “Directorate.”

Taren nodded.

The last months rearranged themselves around that nod.

Not yet a full shape.

Enough to hurt.

“You talk to them.”

Taren looked at him.

“I do.”

The room changed.

Eli stopped moving.

Sera’s hands relaxed at her sides in the way they did before she expected trouble.

Mara’s expression became unreadable.

Kade stared at Taren.

“How long?”

Taren’s voice was almost inaudible.

“Too long.”

“Since when?”

“I will tell you.”

“Now.”

Taren looked at the search list.

“Not here.”

Kade laughed once without humor.

“Convenient.”

“It is.”

Taren swallowed.

“If you want the whole answer, we need a place they cannot listen.”

“And your solution is their archive?” Sera asked.

“Old eastern sublevel has local shielding.”

Eli said, “That is either brilliant or catastrophically suspicious.”

“Both,” Taren said.

Kade should have walked away.

Instead he thought of his father’s blank portrait space.

The Directorate knew they were searching.

Taren had been talking to them.

Whatever truth remained, they were already inside the consequences.

Kade looked at Mara.

She did not decide for him.

Good.

He looked at Sera.

She clearly hated the plan.

Also good.

“Show us the route first,” he said.

Taren glanced toward the wall display.

“The archive route?”

“Every part you expect us to trust because you say so.”

The automatic answer almost reached Taren’s face. Some light remark about his wounded credibility. He stopped it before it became a smile.

“All right.”

He moved the search list to one side and drew a rough plan beneath it.

The old eastern lecture hall occupied the top level. Two public stairs descended to faculty storage. Below that, a maintenance spine ran south behind the archive rooms and joined the engineering utility tunnels.

Taren marked the official archive entrance in red.

Then he marked a narrower door on the maintenance spine.

“We enter here. The outer door reads Sen’s historical authorization. The inner bridge still expects a pre-unification faculty biometric, so it will reject her current credential even though the permission is valid.”

Eli leaned closer.

“That is absurd.”

“It is also useful to people who prefer the archive inaccessible.”

Mara pointed to the maintenance door.

“And this?”

“Built for technicians who had to service both sides without being granted document access. It checks a local infrastructure identity instead of a faculty biometric.”

“Whose identity?” Sera asked.

Taren’s finger remained on the door.

“Mine will work.”

The answer changed the drawing.

What had looked like a route became a confession that had not finished speaking.

Sera took the stylus from him.

“Two exits.”

She circled the maintenance spine and the nearest public stair.

“We test both before anybody opens a file. If either locks, we leave.”

“Agreed,” Taren said, the word arriving without its usual ease.

“Eli keeps the map,” Sera continued. “Mara owns the search. Kade does not touch an unknown system because it looks interesting.”

Kade looked at her.

“That last rule seems unusually personal.”

“Experience made it efficient.”

Eli copied the route to his tablet, then disconnected the device from Meridian’s network.

“How do we communicate if the archive blocks House comms?”

Taren drew three short lines beside the maintenance spine.

“Hardline call points. One at each junction. They reach campus security.”

“And the Directorate?” Mara asked.

Taren did not move the stylus.

“If that layer is active, assume every fixed system reaches them.”

“Including the doors you open,” Kade said.

“Possibly.”

“You left that out upstairs.”

Taren’s mouth tightened.

“I should have said it upstairs.”

Sera added a final mark where the maintenance spine met the archive corridor.

“Abort point. We stop there and verify the return path. Nobody separates. If Taren changes the route without explaining why, he stops leading.”

Taren studied the conditions.

He could have argued that delay increased their exposure. He could have asked for the trust he had spent months collecting.

Instead he gave Eli the old infrastructure code for the route and stepped away from the map.

“You should also know the local alarm does not sound like a normal campus alarm,” he said. “Three low notes means fire suppression. One continuous tone means archive security. Red lights without a tone means someone has isolated the room remotely.”

Kade looked at him.

“How many times have you rehearsed this?”

“Enough to know I should have told you sooner.”

Not an answer.

Closer to one than before.

“Outer archive only,” Kade said. “If anything changes, we leave.”

Taren nodded.

“Those are the limits.”

Sera said, “I reserve the right to hit him.”

Taren looked at her.

“Also agreed.”

---

The route to the restricted archive began beneath the old eastern lecture hall.

Getting there required them to pass through the lecture hall while an evening policy seminar was still ending.

Students spilled into the corridor in clusters, arguing about emergency powers as though the phrase belonged safely to case studies. A faculty assistant held the door for Sera. Taren thanked her and kept walking at the exact pace of someone with no reason to hurry.

Kade stayed two steps behind him.

Taren had always moved through Asterion like that. Never sneaking. Never needing to. People remembered the ease and forgot to ask what door he had used.

At the faculty-storage stair, Taren stopped beside a public directory.

The archive did not appear on it.

“First return check,” Sera said.

They climbed down one level and tried the public stair from the inside. The bar released. Eli held it open long enough for the sensor to register each of them, then let it close.

“One exit,” he said.

The maintenance spine began behind a cabinet labeled **LECTURE SUPPORT — ANALOG EQUIPMENT**. Taren moved the cabinet without strain by releasing two recessed floor catches that Kade had mistaken for screws.

Behind it, a narrow service door carried no handle.

Taren pressed two fingers to the frame.

Nothing happened.

Sera’s attention shifted to him.

Taren tried again, this time holding his hand lower. A line of blue light traveled beneath his fingertips.

**LOCAL IDENTITY ACCEPTED**

The door opened onto a concrete passage barely wide enough for two people abreast.

Eli compared it to the plan.

“The drawing omitted the drop.”

The passage floor descended sharply toward the archive level.

“The drawing was not to scale,” Taren said.

“Gravity remains to scale.”

Sera went first. The soles of her shoes slid once on dust, and she caught the wall before anyone could reach for her.

“Single file. Use the pipe rail.”

Kade followed. Cold metal vibrated beneath his palm. Machinery somewhere below them ran with the slow regularity of a building breathing in its sleep.

At the first junction, Eli tested the hardline call point.

The screen woke.

**CAMPUS SECURITY AVAILABLE**

Beneath the public message, a smaller icon flashed once: three vertical lines closing a broken circle.

Then it vanished.

Kade caught Taren looking at the empty corner.

“What was that?”

Taren’s answer took half a second too long.

“Legacy routing mark.”

“Directorate?” Mara asked.

“The archive inherited some of their infrastructure.”

Sera reached past him and terminated the call before it connected.

“Does the mark mean the line notified them?”

“It means it could.”

“You knew that upstairs.”

“I suspected it.”

Kade looked back up the sloping passage. The service door remained open, a pale rectangle three turns away.

They could leave.

Taren’s route had produced exactly the uncertainty he had warned them about.

Mara waited beside the next junction rather than urging anyone forward.

Kade examined the facts they had.

The Directorate already knew about the twelfth identifier.

The fixed systems might be reporting them.

The exit was still available.

“Second return check,” he said.

They continued only far enough to reach the utility-tunnel hatch. Eli opened it with the local code Taren had surrendered in Meridian, then closed it again.

Two exits.

Both usable for now.

Sera took the lead for the remaining distance. Taren let her.

The credentialed entrance waited beyond the final turn. Its panel demanded two faculty credentials and a biometric confirmation.

Taren had none of those.

The door still opened.

Eli stared.

“How?”

Taren smiled weakly.

“Charm.”

“That was encryption.”

“I’m charming to machines.”

Sera did not laugh.

Neither did Mara.

The archive corridor smelled like cold metal and old air. Unlike the student-access legacy systems, this section had been maintained. New sensors. New seals. New cameras that turned away when Taren touched a wall panel.

Kade slowed.

“You’ve done this before.”

Taren kept walking.

“I know the infrastructure.”

“I asked whether you’d used it.”

“I keep doing that.”

Sera’s gaze sharpened.

Taren looked back.

“For people about to enter a classified archive, you are all suddenly very respectful of rules.”

Eli said, “I am respectful of technical explanations.”

“That is not true.”

“Fair.”

They passed two sealed side doors.

One carried an old Continuity Directorate emblem, barely visible beneath a newer Asterion access plate.

Kade stopped.

Taren did too.

The emblem was simple: a broken circle closed by three vertical lines.

Kade had seen it before.

Not consciously.

On one of the black government folders at Northline.

His stomach tightened.

“You knew them before Asterion.”

Taren did not answer.

Kade did not need one anymore.

They reached the terminal room.

Mara carried the search list.

Aaron Mercer.

Continuity Directorate.

Project Consensus.

Asterion precursor site.

Sen had granted limited historical clearance after the Contact Zero disclosure, but gaps remained inside the authorization large enough to hide decades.

Taren stayed near the door, apart from the search he had brought them to.

“You’re not searching?”

“I’m keeping watch.”

“For what?”

“People more responsible than us.”

Sera positioned herself where she could see both Taren and the corridor.

Eli logged into the archive bridge.

The first useful record appeared under Project Consensus.

Twelve identifiers.

A network diagram.

Then a file header.

**CONTINUITY DIRECTORATE — CONSENSUS APPLICATION STUDY**

Kade leaned closer.

Mara opened the document.

The first paragraph was enough.

**Objective: determine feasibility of coordinated multi-operator Boundary control under centralized command authority.**

Kade’s stomach drew tight.

“Centralized command.”

Mara read farther.

“Operator autonomy degradation acceptable within operational tolerances…”

Sera’s expression hardened.

“Autonomy degradation.”

Eli said, “They were measuring loss of individual control as a design variable.”

Kade looked at the Twelve diagram.

Sen had said Aaron opposed weaponization.

Now he understood why.

The point had never merely been twelve people working together.

Somebody wanted one person—or one command structure—to make twelve people function like components.

Kade thought of the House Trial.

The plan becoming stronger once it stopped being his.

This was the opposite.

Mara opened a later memorandum.

**Recommendation: prioritize command-node stability over participant subjective continuity.**

Kade read the phrase twice.

“Subjective continuity means…”

“The person still feeling like themselves,” Eli said quietly.

Sera stepped toward the screen.

“And they were willing to sacrifice that.”

Taren said from the door, “Some of them.”

Everyone turned.

Kade’s anger snapped into focus.

“You knew.”

Taren shook his head.

“Not details.”

“You knew what they wanted.”

“I knew they believed centralized Consensus was a survival tool.”

“When?”

Taren opened his mouth.

The screen went black.

The lights changed.

White to red.

A heavy lock engaged behind them.

Kade turned.

A transparent security partition had dropped across the corridor.

Taren stood on the other side.

His face looked wrong.

Not triumphant.

Not cold.

Ashamed.

“Taren,” Kade said.

Taren closed his eyes once.

“I’m sorry.”

Sera moved first.

She hit the partition hard enough to make it ring.

“You open this door.”

“I can’t.”

“You just did.”

“That was before.”

Kade stepped closer.

“Before what?”

Taren looked at him.

“Before they knew you were here.”

The wall panel beside Taren lit white.

Not an alarm.

An incoming connection.

He stepped away from it.

The connection opened anyway.

“Cadet Holt,” a woman said.

The voice was low, precise, and unsurprised.

Taren went rigid.

Kade had heard instructors use that tone when a drill ended exactly as expected. The person on the other end had been waiting for this version of Taren.

“Your observation status remains recoverable,” the woman continued. “Do not touch the partition controls. Proceed north to the staff stair.”

Taren looked through the barrier.

Sera stood nearest him, one hand still against the transparent material. Mara had moved beside the terminal, blocking the screen from the camera. Eli held the data slate against his chest.

Kade waited.

The panel displayed a route in green.

It led away from them.

“A security team will assume custody of the students,” the woman said. “You will not be identified in the incident report.”

Taren’s throat moved.

Months of access remained on the wall in one green line.

His family’s version of the future.

All he had to do was walk toward it.

“Was this the assignment?” he asked.

The voice paused.

“This is containment of an unauthorized disclosure.”

Taren kept his gaze on the green route.

“You assigned me to Mercer. Was this always the endpoint?”

“You were assigned to report instability around Mercer. His decision to enter a restricted collection validates the concern.”

Kade stared at the panel.

There it was.

Not friend.

Observation status.

Taren had been given a category for staying near him before they had shared a table.

“He entered because I offered the route,” Taren said.

“Then your corrective obligation is clear.”

“Meaning leave them locked in.”

“Meaning do not compound an error out of personal attachment.”

The last two words carried no anger.

That made them crueler.

Taren lifted his hand toward the panel.

For one second Kade thought he would accept the route.

Instead Taren pressed his thumb to the white connection icon.

“My attachment is not your error to correct.”

He terminated the call.

The green route disappeared.

The panel turned red.

**FIELD IDENTITY REVIEW INITIATED**

Taren stared at the message until the first line of his name began to vanish.

Then he faced Kade again.

No joke arrived to make the choice smaller.

Mara said, “Who is they?”

Taren’s jaw tightened.

“The Directorate.”

Silence.

The red lights pulsed once.

Kade waited for anger.

Nothing came.

That frightened him.

“How long?”

Taren looked away.

“How long have you been reporting on me?” Kade asked.

“Since before you arrived.”

Eli’s arms cinched around the data slate.

Kade thought of the Meridian roof.

The first message.

*I’ll explain why half the school already hates you.*

He had thought it was funny.

“How?” Kade asked.

Taren looked back.

“What?”

“How were you assigned before I arrived?”

Taren breathed in.

“My family has Directorate connections.”

“Family connections didn’t put you at our table.”

“They didn’t.”

Taren looked at the floor.

“My mother works diplomatic security. My uncle was Directorate analysis. I grew up around people who talk about stability like it is the only moral word.”

Sera’s expression hardened.

“And you joined them.”

“I was sixteen.”

“You are seventeen now,” Mara said.

“A year did not make it better.”

His voice cracked at the edge.

Kade had never heard that happen.

“They approached me after my academy ranking posted. Said I had the right social profile. High enough rank to move between groups. No famous family name. Good at getting people to talk.”

Taren laughed once, bitterly.

“Turns out being likable is a security skill if the wrong people write the job description.”

Kade said nothing.

“They said Asterion had a classified admission they needed observed. No sabotage. No coercion. No contact outside normal student life. Just report changes in behavior, social ties, unusual training.”

“You reported us,” Eli said.

Taren looked at him.

“I reported all of you.”

“My models?”

“Not the contents.”

“You expect me to feel better?”

“I don’t.”

Kade stepped close enough that only the partition separated them.

“You became my friend because someone told you to.”

“At first.”

Kade laughed once.

There was no humor in it.

“At first.”

Taren pressed one hand against the transparent barrier.

“Kade, I thought this was intelligence work. Risk assessment. I thought the Directorate was paranoid and controlling and mostly harmless because everyone around me described it as necessary.”

“When did you figure out it wasn’t?”

Taren looked toward the locked terminal.

“House Trial.”

Kade stared.

“Why?”

“Because my handler asked which of you would follow you if you left Asterion.”

The room contracted.

Mara went completely still.

Sera’s hands curled.

Taren continued.

“That wasn’t a training question. It wasn’t about risk. It was about leverage.”

“And you kept reporting.”

“I kept reporting.”

“Why?”

“Because stopping suddenly would tell them I was compromised.”

Sera said, “Convenient.”

“It was convenient.”

Taren did not defend himself.

“I started feeding them less. Things they could learn elsewhere. Rank changes. Public interactions. Training schedules they already had. I thought I could keep them satisfied while figuring out what they wanted.”

“You thought you could manage the system,” Kade said.

Taren gave a broken smile.

“Apparently you have been a terrible influence.”

Kade did not smile back.

“What did you tell them after my father?”

Taren’s eyes closed for a second.

“That you were angry with Sen.”

“After Run?”

“That went in.”

“That I almost left?”

“I kept that out.”

Kade stared.

Taren met his gaze.

“I did not tell them that.”

“Why?”

“Because that was yours.”

The answer hurt more than if Taren had said everything was fake.

A fake friend would have been easy.

This was not.

The terminal-room speaker activated.

A neutral voice said:

**SECURITY RESPONSE INBOUND. REMAIN IN PLACE.**

Eli looked at the sealed exits.

“How inbound?”

Taren checked a wall indicator on his side.

“Under ninety seconds.”

Sera said, “Open the partition.”

“I can’t until the security cycle resets.”

“You keep saying can’t after repeatedly demonstrating you can.”

“Eight-second override window. Once. Then my access burns.”

Mara stared at him.

“Your Directorate access.”

“That is what the window costs.”

“If you use it, they know you chose us.”

Taren looked at her.

“The choice will be explicit.”

Kade’s anger shifted.

Not away.

Never away.

Just enough to make room for another fact.

Taren could leave them there.

The Directorate would preserve his access.

His family connections.

Whatever future had been built around being useful to people who valued stability above autonomy.

Instead he was preparing to burn it.

Sera asked, “Why?”

Taren looked through the barrier at all of them.

“Because this stopped being an assignment a long time ago.”

The red lights pulsed.

A timer appeared above the door.

**SECURITY CYCLE — 00:12**

Taren said, “When it hits zero, run east. Not west. West lift will lock.”

Eli stared at him.

“How do you know?”

Taren’s face twisted.

“Because I helped write the response map.”

That hurt everyone differently.

Kade’s breath shortened.

Every useful warning.

Every precise piece of information.

All contaminated.

Not necessarily false.

That was worse.

The timer reached five.

Four.

Three.

Taren put his palm to the panel.

Two.

One.

The partition released.

Sera moved first.

Mara followed.

Eli grabbed the data slate.

Kade stepped through last.

Taren stood beside the open control panel.

His access badge flashed red.

**CREDENTIAL REVOKED**

Kade stopped for half a second.

Taren looked at him.

“I am sorry.”

Kade heard footsteps in the distant corridor.

He thought of Meridian Night.

House Trial.

Coffee.

Opponent notes.

Taren carrying pastries after disappearing during prep.

The way he had not reported Kade’s attempt to leave.

The way he had still reported other things.

“I don’t know what that means yet,” Kade said.

Taren nodded.

“That’s fair.”

Then they ran.

---

The eastern service corridor had not been designed for six students sprinting through it.

Pipes crowded the ceiling. Equipment carts narrowed the turns. Red emergency lighting erased depth and made every doorway look occupied.

Taren led.

Sera stayed directly behind him, close enough to stop him if leading became escaping.

At the first junction Taren turned left.

Mara said, “You said east.”

“This is east.”

“It is physically north.”

“Archive grid is rotated relative to campus.”

Eli, somehow running while checking a maintenance map, said, “He’s right.”

Sera looked annoyed by this.

They reached a locked service hatch.

Taren put his revoked credential against it.

Nothing.

His face changed.

“Problem?” Kade asked.

“My access is dead faster than expected.”

Footsteps sounded behind them.

Sera moved to the rear.

“Alternative.”

Taren looked at Eli.

“Manual bus under the panel.”

Eli dropped to his knees and opened the housing.

“You know this model?”

“I know the Directorate retrofit.”

“That phrase is deeply concerning.”

“Later.”

Eli found two contact points.

The hatch released.

They crossed into an old utility tunnel and pulled it shut.

The footsteps passed on the other side.

For several seconds, nobody moved.

Kade could hear everyone breathing.

Taren leaned against the wall.

Sera stepped toward him.

Taren lifted his chin.

“You reserved the right.”

Sera hit him.

One open-handed strike across the face.

Not enough to injure.

Enough to turn his head.

Eli flinched.

Mara did not.

Taren touched his cheek.

“Reasonable.”

Sera’s eyes were wet.

That was worse than anger.

“You sat at our table.”

“I let you.”

“You sat with him while he decided to stay.”

“I reported the decision around it.”

“You knew people wanted leverage over that.”

“I knew by then.”

Sera stepped back before she hit him again.

Kade looked at Taren.

“Who is your handler?”

Taren hesitated.

Then said, “Marisol Crane.”

The name meant nothing to Kade.

Mara recognized it.

“Directorate strategic analysis.”

Taren nodded.

“Formerly public. Now internal.”

He reached beneath his academy overshirt.

Sera’s weight shifted forward.

Taren stopped with his hand visible.

“There is a Directorate wafer behind my student credential.”

“Take the whole badge out by the edge,” Sera said. “Slowly.”

He obeyed.

The credential looked ordinary until he pressed one corner. A second layer slid free, black and thin enough to hide behind the academy seal.

Taren placed both pieces on the tunnel floor and stepped back.

Eli crouched without touching them.

“Active transmitter?”

“Not after revocation. It stores contact tokens and cached instructions.”

“Does it store reports?” Kade asked.

Taren looked at the black wafer.

“Submission headers, and any drafts not yet acknowledged.”

“How many records?”

“Three.”

The number landed harder than a general answer.

Kade kept his voice level.

“About what?”

“My midyear update on you. Your return after Run. And today’s archive search.”

Sera looked ready to strike him again.

“Submitted?” Mara asked.

“The first two were requested. I sent summaries without the underlying files. Today’s was never sent.”

“That is a distinction you made,” Kade said. “Not one we agreed to.”

“I decided for all of you.”

Eli removed an antistatic sleeve from his tablet case.

“If I shield it, can they erase the cache remotely?”

“They should not be able to.”

“Should.”

“I did not design this one.”

Eli used two plastic map tabs to lift the wafer into the sleeve. The instant the shield closed around it, the revoked credential flashed again.

**NETWORK UNAVAILABLE**

Then the surface went dark.

Mara held out her hand.

Eli gave her the sealed wafer.

Not Kade.

Good.

The evidence did not belong to him simply because the surveillance had centered on him.

Mara examined the seal.

“Sen receives this with us present. We preserve the drafts and the headers. Nobody opens it alone.”

Taren nodded.

“There are phrases in the headers that map to Directorate priority codes. I can translate them.”

Sera’s voice stayed flat.

“You can offer. You do not touch the evidence again.”

Taren looked at the sleeve in Mara’s hand.

“Understood.”

Kade studied him.

The surrender did not prove Taren honest. It proved only that he had placed one hidden thing where other people could inspect it.

For tonight, that counted as a change.

“What does she want?”

“Consensus.”

Kade’s stomach tightened.

“She knows the term?”

“She used it before I did.”

“How much?”

“I do not know.”

“Do not say you don’t know when you mean you won’t tell me.”

Taren looked directly at him.

“I genuinely do not know.”

Kade believed him.

That was becoming a problem too.

Taren continued.

“She thinks the old program failed because the command node was weak.”

Eli stared.

“The command node was a person.”

“A person they treated as hardware.”

“And her conclusion was to make the person stronger?”

“Stronger. More controllable.”

Kade thought of the application study.

Participant autonomy degradation acceptable.

One person directing many.

The exact opposite of everything he had learned accidentally through Meridian.

“What does she think I am?” Kade asked.

Taren swallowed.

“A possible command node.”

Nausea climbed into Kade’s throat.

Mara said, “He cannot even intentionally reproduce Boundary architecture.”

“They do not care what he can do today.”

Julian’s father planned long-term.

Apparently institutions did too.

Taren looked at Kade.

“They care what Zero meant last time.”

Aaron.

The Twelve.

Eleven point four seconds.

Kade looked at the data slate Eli still held.

“We need Sen.”

“Yes,” Taren said.

Sera looked at him.

“You do not get to say ‘we’ automatically.”

Taren flinched.

“I haven’t earned it.”

Kade hated the sentence because it was true and because Taren deserved it.

He also hated the part of himself that wanted to make one clean decision about Taren and be finished.

Friend.

Enemy.

Trustworthy.

Traitor.

People refused to stay inside useful categories.

That was inconveniently consistent with everything Kade understood about boundaries.

They exited the utility tunnel near the eastern engineering wing.

Normal campus lights returned.

Students moved through the distant corridor unaware that anything had happened below them.

Taren stopped before stepping into public view.

Sera checked both directions, then turned back to him.

“Until Sen takes custody of this: you stay in the middle of the group. No terminals. No messages. If you recognize a route, a camera, or a person, you say so before it matters.”

Taren removed his personal tablet and held it out by one edge.

Sera did not take it.

“Eli.”

Eli accepted the tablet and powered it down.

“I also need to remove him from Meridian’s shared schedule, training archive, and room controls.”

Taren’s face tightened.

“Do it.”

“You do not get credit for permitting what I can revoke.”

“Then I won’t ask for it.”

Sera looked at Kade.

“You do not meet with him alone.”

Taren’s gaze stayed on the dark tablet.

Kade could have rejected the rule because the surveillance had been about him. He could have claimed the right to decide what danger counted when he was the target.

That would turn injury into authority.

“Agreed,” he said.

Mara shifted the sealed wafer into an inside pocket.

“These are precautions, not a verdict. Sen gets the evidence. Taren gets to answer. Nobody gets to make uncertainty convenient.”

“That includes the Directorate’s version of what happened,” Taren said.

Sera’s expression did not soften.

“It includes yours.”

Taren inclined his head.

Students crossed the far end of the corridor. One raised a hand to Taren, beginning an ordinary greeting.

Taren started to lift his own hand.

Then the student registered the formation around him and lowered theirs.

The cost of his choice had reached the surface.

“My access is gone. They know.”

Kade looked at him.

“What happens now?”

“I become useless to them.”

“Good.”

Taren smiled faintly.

“Probably not how they see it.”

Mara said, “Do you have another way to contact Crane?”

“Through my family.”

“Will you?”

“Not before Sen sees the evidence.”

Sera folded her arms.

“Why should we believe that?”

Taren’s face lost the smile.

“You shouldn’t.”

There it was again.

No demand for trust.

No shortcut.

Kade understood the strange relief in it.

Taren had spent months gathering trust under false terms.

If anything real remained, it would have to start from zero.

Kade looked toward the engineering corridor.

“Sen first.”

Eli checked the time.

“We are six minutes from her nearest secure meeting point if the internal transit is still up.”

Six minutes.

A small amount of time.

Enough for everything to change.

Kade adjusted the strap on his tablet case.

Taren fell in behind the group rather than beside him.

Not forgiven.

Not trusted.

Not abandoned either.

For now, that was the only category Kade could live with.

They headed toward engineering transit together.

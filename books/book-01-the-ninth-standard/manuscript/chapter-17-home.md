# Chapter 17 — Home

Northline looked smaller than Kade remembered.

That annoyed him.

The building had not changed.

Same faded blue wall.

Same tire warehouse next door.

Same vending machine visible through the front window.

But after Asterion—after glass towers, arenas, ocean walls, and rankings that updated in real time—the old county records building looked as if someone had reduced it while he was gone.

Elena parked at the curb.

“You going in?”

Kade looked at the front doors.

“That was the plan.”

“You’ve been staring at it for three minutes.”

“I’m appreciating architecture.”

“Go.”

He opened the door.

Then paused.

“Mom?”

Elena looked over.

“Thanks for coming.”

She smiled faintly.

“I wanted to see Ms. Alvarez.”

“Still counts.”

They walked in together.

The security officer, Mr. Boyd, looked up from a bicycle wheel he was repairing behind the desk.

“Mercer.”

“Boyd.”

Mr. Boyd examined him.

“You taller?”

“No.”

“Shame.”

“Good to be home.”

That word came out before Kade could stop it.

Home.

He pretended not to notice.

Room Four was louder than he remembered.

Darius saw him first.

He stood so fast his chair rolled backward into the wall.

“Zero Boy!”

Every head turned.

Kade closed his eyes.

“I came all this way for that.”

Darius crossed the room and hugged him hard enough to make breathing optional.

Then immediately pushed him away.

“That did not happen.”

“Agreed.”

Ms. Alvarez stood by her desk with both arms folded.

Her coffee was half full.

Some systems were reliable.

“Mercer,” she said.

“Alvarez.”

“You look less irritating.”

“Distance creates nostalgia.”

Her mouth twitched.

Then she hugged him.

Briefly.

No speech.

Kade preferred it that way.

The students wanted stories.

Asterion fights.

Dorm rooms.

Food.

Famous students.

Whether Mara Vey was “actually that scary.”

Kade refused to answer the last one.

Darius noticed.

Unfortunately.

During lunch, Kade sat at his old table and realized he had forgotten how bad Northline’s chairs were.

Darius leaned across from him.

“So you’re rank nineteen hundred now.”

“One nine-oh-four.”

“That is still terrible.”

“There are ten thousand people.”

“Sounds like excuses.”

Kade smiled.

Tessa dropped into the chair beside them.

“My aunt says you’re government property.”

“Which aunt?”

“Moon landing.”

“Reliable source.”

They laughed.

For a while, it felt exactly the same.

Then Vice Principal Russell entered.

Kade’s body tightened before his mind caught up.

Russell smiled broadly.

“Kade.”

“Mr. Russell.”

“I was hoping I’d catch you.”

Kade’s instincts did not trust the warmth.

Russell shook Elena’s hand.

“We are incredibly proud of what Kade has accomplished.”

Ms. Alvarez’s face became very still.

Kade noticed.

Russell continued.

“We always knew he had extraordinary potential.”

There it was.

Kade felt something hot rise in his chest.

Not because the statement was completely false.

Because it was almost true in the most dishonest possible way.

Russell had told him he was wasting opportunities.

Teachers had called him difficult.

Meetings had become negotiations over how little trouble he could cause.

And now success had rewritten everyone’s memory.

Kade opened his mouth.

Ms. Alvarez said quietly, “Kade.”

One word.

He looked at her.

She was not telling him to stay silent.

She was telling him to choose.

Kade turned back to Russell.

“You thought I was smart,” he said.

Russell smiled.

“Exactly.”

“You also thought being smart meant I should work inside a system that wasn’t working for me.”

Russell’s smile weakened.

“Kade—”

“I made things worse. A lot. That part is mine.”

Elena watched him carefully.

“But Northline helped because people here stopped treating compliance like the same thing as learning.”

Kade looked at Ms. Alvarez.

“She helped.”

Ms. Alvarez looked suddenly interested in her coffee.

Kade turned back to Russell.

“You don’t have to pretend you predicted Asterion.”

The room had gone quiet.

Russell cleared his throat.

“I’m simply saying we’re proud of you.”

“That part I’ll take.”

Russell nodded and left a few minutes later.

Darius stared at Kade.

“What?” Kade asked.

“That was very mature.”

“Take it back.”

“No.”

“I have a reputation.”

“Gone.”

Later, Ms. Alvarez found him alone near the vending machine.

It dispensed three bags of pretzels when he bought one.

Kade stared at them.

“Darius was right.”

“About?”

“It respects people.”

Ms. Alvarez leaned against the wall.

“You handled Russell well.”

“I had a good teacher.”

“Careful.”

“That sounded dangerous?”

“Very.”

They stood quietly.

Then Ms. Alvarez said, “I owe you an apology.”

Kade looked at her.

She continued before he could joke.

“I saw that you were bored. Angry. Defensive. I understood more than some people did.”

“You did.”

“But sometimes I still thought if you would just make yourself fit better, things would get easier.”

Kade said nothing.

“I was wrong about part of that,” she said.

The words hit harder than Russell’s speech.

Adults did not apologize to Kade often.

Not because he was always right.

Because institutions were skilled at making every conflict look like a child’s failure.

“You weren’t wrong about all of it,” Kade said.

“No.”

“I was difficult.”

“You remain difficult.”

“Thank you.”

“You’re welcome.”

He smiled.

Ms. Alvarez nodded toward the classroom.

“They watch you now.”

Kade looked through the door.

Darius was pretending not to look at him.

Tessa was definitely looking.

Two younger students whispered near the back.

“That seems unhealthy.”

“It is responsibility.”

Kade grimaced.

“Worse.”

Before leaving, Kade stood at the front of Room Four.

He had not planned to speak.

That was probably why it worked.

“Asterion is strange,” he said.

Darius raised a hand.

“We know.”

“Thank you.”

Kade looked around the room.

“I thought everyone there would be geniuses who had never failed anything.”

“Are they?” Tessa asked.

“Some.”

“Awful.”

“Deeply.”

A few people laughed.

Kade continued.

“But there are people there who messed up. People who came from alternative schools. People who are good at one thing and terrible at another.”

He thought of Eli.

Sera.

Even himself.

“And they’re not all better than you because they got there first.”

The room was quiet.

Kade felt self-conscious.

So he did the dangerous thing.

He kept going.

“Meridian has a House Trial exhibition next month. I asked if Northline could come.”

Darius sat up.

“Come where?”

“Asterion.”

The room erupted.

Kade laughed.

He had expected excitement.

He had not expected how much it would mean to him.

As he and Elena walked back to the car, Darius followed them outside.

“Kade.”

Kade turned.

Darius shoved his hands into his pockets.

“You coming back again?”

The old version of Kade would have made a joke.

Something about surviving Asterion.

Something noncommittal.

Instead he said, “Yeah.”

Darius nodded.

“Good.”

Kade got into the car.

Elena started the engine.

For several blocks, neither spoke.

Then she said, “You called Northline home.”

Kade looked out the window.

“I did not.”

“You did.”

“Audio evidence?”

“I have ears.”

“Unreliable.”

Elena smiled.

Kade watched the old neighborhood move past.

Asterion was becoming home too.

The realization should have frightened him.

It did.

But fear no longer felt like a reason to leave.

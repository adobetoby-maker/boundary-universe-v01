# Chapter 17 — Home

Northline looked smaller than Kade remembered.

That annoyed him.

The building had not changed.

Same faded blue wall.

Same tire warehouse next door.

Same self-storage sign missing the lower corner of its S.

Same vending machine visible through the front window.

But after Asterion—after glass towers, arenas, seawalls, fields that bent trajectories, and ranking boards large enough to influence emotional weather—the old county records building looked as though somebody had quietly reduced it while he was gone.

Elena parked at the curb.

“You going in?”

Kade looked at the front doors.

“That was the plan.”

“You have been staring at it for three minutes.”

“I’m appreciating architecture.”

“Go.”

He opened the passenger door.

Then paused.

“Mom?”

Elena looked over.

“Thanks for coming.”

She smiled faintly.

“I wanted to see Ms. Alvarez.”

“Still counts.”

They walked in together.

The metal detector beeped at Elena’s keys.

Mr. Boyd, Northline’s security officer, looked up from a bicycle wheel he was repairing behind the desk.

“Mercer.”

“Boyd.”

Mr. Boyd examined him.

“You taller?”

“No.”

“Shame.”

“Good to be home.”

The word came out before Kade could stop it.

Home.

He pretended not to notice.

Elena’s expression flickered.

Of course she did.

She had the decency not to say anything yet.

The hallway smelled like floor cleaner, old carpet, and the chili somebody was reheating in the staff room. The ceiling seemed lower. The walls had more dents than Kade remembered. A motivational poster about *CHOOSING YOUR FUTURE* hung slightly crooked beside a fire extinguisher.

Kade had hated that poster for months.

Today it looked almost funny.

Room Four was louder than he remembered.

Darius saw him first.

He stood so fast his chair rolled backward into the wall.

“Zero Boy!”

Every head turned.

Kade closed his eyes.

“I came all this way for that.”

Darius crossed the room and hugged him hard enough to make breathing optional.

Then immediately pushed him away.

“That did not happen.”

“Agreed.”

Tessa shouted, “Show us your Conduit!”

“I do not have one.”

“Then what do they put in your neck?”

“Currently disappointment.”

Someone in the back laughed.

Ms. Alvarez stood beside her desk with both arms folded.

Her coffee was half full.

Some systems were reliable.

“Mercer,” she said.

“Alvarez.”

“You look less irritating.”

“Distance creates nostalgia.”

Her mouth twitched.

Then she hugged him.

Briefly.

No speech.

Kade preferred it that way.

Elena and Ms. Alvarez greeted each other with the tired familiarity of two people who had sat through too many meetings about the same teenager.

“Still alive?” Ms. Alvarez asked her.

“Barely.”

“Kade or emergency medicine?”

“Yes.”

Kade decided to pretend he could not hear them.

The students wanted stories.

Asterion fights.

Dorm rooms.

Food.

Famous students.

Whether the island really floated.

Whether Julian Cross was as good as the clips.

Whether Mara Vey was “actually that scary.”

Kade refused to answer the last question.

Unfortunately.

“Oh,” Darius said slowly.

“No.”

“I did not say anything.”

“You were about to.”

Darius grinned.

Kade considered returning to Asterion immediately.

Tessa raised both hands.

“Is the island really floating?”

“No.”

Half the room groaned.

“It is an island. It is in water. Those are usually the rules.”

“The videos make it look like it moves.”

“The outer training platforms move. Some of the bridges retract. The island mostly stays where geology left it.”

Darius leaned back in his chair.

“Disappointing.”

“You attend school beside a tire warehouse.”

“And yet I maintain standards.”

The questions became more specific once the fantasy lost altitude.

Did everyone have their own room?

No.

Could instructors read minds?

Probably not, although Instructor Vale made a persuasive case.

Was the food free?

Technically. Emotionally, Kade was still paying for breakfast oats.

Did people really fight between classes?

Only if they wanted penalties, medical restrictions, and Julian Cross explaining why they had embarrassed the House.

“So yes,” Darius translated.

“Occasionally.”

Marcus, a younger student Kade did not know, asked, “What does rank nineteen hundred mean?”

The room quieted by a degree.

Kade had answered versions of that question for Student Media. Those answers had involved weighted performance, challenge rules, and enough careful language to survive editing.

None of it would help here.

“It means there are nineteen hundred and three students the system puts above me.”

“At fighting?”

“At a collection of things the school turns into one number.”

“Are they all better than you?”

Kade thought of Mara dismantling him across an arena.

Of Julian taking control of a team before Kade had understood there was a decision to make.

Of Eli building a haptic bridge from discarded components while eating noodles.

“At something,” he said. “Probably.”

“But not everything.”

“Nobody is better at everything.”

Darius pointed at himself.

“I am better at several things.”

“Naming them would weaken the claim.”

Marcus kept looking at Kade.

“What does Zero mean?”

The room went still for real this time.

That name had traveled farther than any explanation attached to it.

Kade rested one hand on the back of his old chair.

“It means their first test could not measure what I did.”

“So you broke it?” Tessa asked.

“That is one interpretation.”

“What is the other one?”

“The test asked the wrong question.”

Ms. Alvarez lifted her coffee to hide the beginning of a smile.

Kade continued before the sentence could become a slogan.

“Then I spent months discovering that being right about one question did not make me good at anything else.”

Darius made a thoughtful noise.

“That part should be in the clips.”

“They cut for time.”

“Cowards.”

Someone asked Kade to show them a Boundary.

He said no.

Not as a joke.

The answer settled the room.

“I do not use it without faculty oversight,” he said. “And I am not turning Room Four into a demonstration site.”

Darius glanced toward the stained ceiling tile above the heater.

“Because you respect the facility.”

“Because Alvarez would kill me before the Boundary did.”

“Correct,” Ms. Alvarez said.

That got the laughter back.

But Kade caught Marcus tracing the edge of his tablet with one finger, considering the difference between a broken test and a broken student.

Good.

Instead he spent the morning moving through the old rhythm.

Room Four’s tablets still froze if too many modules updated at once.

The white clock still stuttered when the heater switched on.

The chair at his old desk still leaned slightly left.

Kade sat there for ten minutes while Tessa worked on a geometry module and Darius pretended to work on one.

The strangest part was how quickly he remembered everything.

Not academically.

Physically.

Where to put his feet so the desk did not wobble.

Which fluorescent light buzzed.

The exact second Ms. Alvarez would look up if Darius’s silence lasted too long.

Asterion had not erased Northline.

It had made Kade aware of how much Northline lived inside him.

At ten seventeen, Darius stood.

“Bathroom.”

Kade looked at the clock.

Ms. Alvarez looked at Darius.

Darius looked at both of them.

Then slowly sat back down.

“Personal growth,” he said.

Kade laughed so hard Tessa threw an eraser at him.

Later, Ms. Alvarez asked Kade to sit in on one of the afternoon project blocks.

He almost refused out of reflex.

Then remembered he no longer attended Northline and therefore could not use school as an excuse to avoid school.

The project was simple: design an evacuation plan for the building during a power failure.

Darius immediately wanted to use the loading dock.

Tessa pointed out the dock gate failed closed without power.

A younger student named Marcus kept insisting the front entrance was best because it was the nearest.

Kade listened.

Ms. Alvarez had drawn the building on three sheets of butcher paper taped across the tables. Bottle caps represented students. Paper clips represented staff. A red marker line blocked the east hall where, according to the scenario, an electrical fire had started above the ceiling.

“How many people?” Kade asked.

Tessa checked the scenario card.

“Forty-three students. Seven staff.”

“Forty-nine,” Darius said.

“That is fifty.”

“I was testing you.”

“You were losing to addition.”

Ms. Alvarez tapped the clock.

“Eleven minutes.”

The groups moved their bottle caps.

Everyone chose the front hall first.

The paper filled with arrows until the entrance looked less like an exit than a drain.

“Done,” Marcus said.

Kade looked at the thirty-seven bottle caps stacked behind a doorway wide enough for two people.

His hands wanted the marker.

At Asterion, taking it would have been efficient. Draw the alternate routes. Assign a lead to every room. Put a staff member at the accessible exit. Solve the system before the clock reached ten minutes.

He kept his hands on the table.

“Test it,” he said.

Marcus frowned.

“It’s a map.”

“Then the map should survive people.”

They borrowed students from the other project group and lined everyone in the hallway. Marcus stood at the front entrance with a stopwatch. Darius stayed inside Room Four to count.

Ms. Alvarez killed the lights.

The emergency fixtures came on three seconds later.

One flickered.

Nobody found that surprising.

“Scenario begins now,” she said.

The line moved.

Then it stopped.

Two students reached the front doors at once and both tried to hold one open. Someone behind them asked which side they were supposed to use. Tessa came out of the staff-room corridor and collided gently with the back of the group.

“Fire in the east hall,” Ms. Alvarez called. “You cannot cross the red line.”

Three people reversed direction.

The hallway locked.

Darius called from Room Four, “I have fourteen people who have not even left.”

Marcus stared at the stopwatch as if it had betrayed him.

“Time,” Ms. Alvarez said after two minutes.

They returned to the butcher paper.

Marcus crossed out half his arrows.

“Nearest is bad.”

“Nearest is useful,” Kade said. “Until everybody knows it is nearest.”

Tessa moved nine bottle caps toward the rear classroom corridor.

“Loading dock.”

“Gate fails closed,” Darius reminded her.

“The gate does. The personnel door beside it doesn’t.”

Darius looked offended by the building.

“Since when?”

“Since always.”

“I reject hidden information.”

“You have been through that door.”

“I reject remembered information.”

Marcus had begun dividing the rooms into zones.

“Front two rooms go front. Back rooms use the personnel door.”

He moved the paper clips.

“Teachers go with their rooms.”

A student named Lacey, who had said almost nothing all morning, pointed to the accessible exit on the north wall.

“Mr. Omondi can’t use either route if the lift loses power.”

The group stopped.

Mr. Omondi taught numeracy in Room Six and used a powered chair.

Darius moved a paper clip toward the north door.

“This one has level access.”

“And a manual push bar,” Lacey said.

Marcus drew a third route.

Kade looked at Ms. Alvarez.

She gave him nothing.

The problem belonged to the students.

“Who checks Room Six?” Marcus asked.

“Mr. Omondi checks Room Six,” Lacey said. “We ask what he needs before making him our assignment.”

Kade glanced at her.

Lacey shrugged.

“My brother uses a chair.”

Different competence.

Different piece of the system.

Kade had come within one second of drawing a plan that treated Mr. Omondi as cargo.

He picked up a bottle cap instead and set it back inside Room Four.

“You still have one problem.”

Darius counted.

“Forty-three students.”

Tessa counted again.

“Forty-two.”

They searched the map.

Marcus pointed to the restroom at the end of the west hall.

Nobody had checked it.

The second test used three routes, assigned sweep zones, and a meeting point beside the tire warehouse fence. Lacey stationed herself where she could see the north exit. Darius carried a paper attendance sheet because the class tablets lost the network when Ms. Alvarez shut off the room router.

The final student crossed the marked threshold in seventy-eight seconds.

“Seventy-six,” Darius said.

Marcus held up the stopwatch.

“Seventy-eight.”

“My count includes morale.”

“Your count includes lying.”

Kade leaned against the wall and let them argue.

The first plan had been his kind of problem: visible, bounded, waiting for the fastest person in the room.

The second plan survived because Lacey knew what the map left out, Tessa knew the building, Marcus was willing to replace his own idea, and Darius—against precedent—had remembered to count.

Asterion had spent months teaching Kade the same lesson with more expensive walls.

The old version of him would have taken over.

He could see the problem quickly.

Two narrow hallways.

One accessible exit.

The staff room creating a bottleneck.

Instead he asked Marcus, “What happens when everybody chooses nearest?”

Marcus looked at the map.

“They all go there.”

“And then?”

The kid frowned.

Darius leaned over.

“Traffic jam.”

Marcus changed his route.

Ms. Alvarez watched Kade from across the room.

Kade pretended not to notice.

When the exercise ended, Darius said, “You got weird.”

“I have always been weird.”

“No. You let him figure it out.”

“That sounds like teaching.”

“Exactly.”

Kade stared at him.

“Take it back.”

Darius refused.

---

At lunch, Kade sat at his old table and realized he had forgotten how bad the chairs were.

Darius leaned across from him.

“So you’re rank nineteen hundred now.”

“One nine-oh-four.”

“That is still terrible.”

“There are ten thousand people.”

“Sounds like excuses.”

Kade smiled.

Tessa dropped into the chair beside them.

“My aunt says you’re government property.”

“Which aunt?”

“Moon landing.”

“Reliable source.”

They laughed.

For a while it felt exactly the same.

Then Tessa left to trade half a sandwich for somebody’s pudding, and the noise of the room shifted around them.

Darius turned Kade’s unopened milk carton until the label faced away.

“You know you are terrible at messages.”

Kade looked at him.

“That transition had no warning.”

“I sent you eleven.”

“I answered.”

“You reacted to a picture of Mr. Boyd asleep at the desk.”

“That communicated delight.”

“It communicated one laughing face.”

Kade picked up the carton.

Darius did not let him escape.

“Then you were on every screen fighting people, and Alvarez knew your rank before I did.”

There was no accusation in the volume.

That made it harder.

“She follows the school reports,” Kade said.

“I follow you.”

The cafeteria kept moving.

Plastic forks against trays.

The refrigerator motor under the staff counter.

Somebody arguing that applesauce counted as a beverage.

Kade set the milk down.

“I should have called.”

Darius blinked once.

He had come prepared for defense.

Kade knew the feeling.

“Yeah,” Darius said.

“I kept thinking I would call when I had something that made sense.”

“You go to combat school on a moving island.”

“The island does not move.”

“Nothing was going to make sense.”

Kade looked toward Room Four.

“At first I hated it. Then I didn’t. That was difficult to explain.”

Darius peeled one corner from the milk label.

“Because you thought we would be mad?”

“Because I was.”

“At us?”

“At myself.”

The answer had been waiting longer than Kade expected.

“I spent years telling everyone I didn’t need a school to want me. Then Asterion did.”

Darius considered that.

“Northline wanted you.”

“Alvarez wanted me. You wanted me. The district wanted me to stop becoming paperwork.”

“Fair.”

“Asterion put it on a screen.”

“Then put zero on the screen.”

Kade opened the milk.

“It has complicated communication skills.”

Darius nodded toward the Conduit port hidden under Kade’s collar.

“Does that hurt?”

“Sometimes.”

“You could have said that too.”

Kade looked at him.

Darius’s expression had changed.

Not curiosity.

Worry, held carefully enough not to embarrass either of them.

“I’m okay,” Kade said.

“That was not my question.”

Kade rubbed one thumb over the milk-carton seam.

“The port aches after hard training. Boundary work gives me headaches. The medical team knows. They stop me before I want to stop.”

“Usually?”

“Usually.”

“And the fights?”

“Protective fields. Supervision. Too many rules.”

Darius waited.

“I got knocked unconscious once,” Kade admitted.

“Kade.”

“Briefly.”

“That is not a unit that helps.”

“I recovered.”

Darius sat back.

“I hate this school.”

“You have never been there.”

“I am building a position from available evidence.”

Kade smiled despite himself.

“You’ll like it.”

“I can hate it and like it.”

“That does appear to be allowed.”

Darius pushed the milk carton back toward him.

“Call next time before you become nationally confusing.”

“I can probably manage after.”

“Before.”

Kade nodded.

“Before.”

It was not a promise to become good at staying in contact.

It was a promise to stop making confusion an excuse for disappearing.

Kade could make that one.

Then two teachers from Kade’s old district high school appeared with Vice Principal Russell.

Kade recognized one immediately.

Mr. Hensley had taught sophomore physics.

He had once told Kade that “being technically correct while refusing the assignment is still failing.”

Kade had hated him for months.

The second teacher, Ms. Rook, had taught English and been one of the few people at Canyon Ridge who stopped arguing when Kade said he needed to leave a room before he said something worse.

Russell smiled broadly.

“Kade. I wanted some of your former teachers to say hello.”

Kade’s body tightened before his mind caught up.

Old reactions had better memory than people did.

Hensley shook his hand enthusiastically.

“We always knew you had extraordinary potential.”

Ms. Alvarez’s face became very still.

Russell continued.

“Everyone did.”

Heat rose in Kade’s chest.

Not because the statement was completely false.

Because it was almost true in the most dishonest possible way.

Russell had told him he was smart.

Hensley had told him intelligence made every failure less excusable.

Meetings had become negotiations over how little trouble he could cause.

And now success had begun rewriting everyone’s memory.

Kade opened his mouth.

Ms. Alvarez said quietly, “Kade.”

One word.

He looked at her.

She was not telling him to be quiet.

She was telling him to choose.

That was different.

Kade turned back to Russell and Hensley.

“You thought I was smart,” he said.

Hensley smiled, relieved.

“Exactly.”

“You also thought being smart meant I should work inside a system that wasn’t working for me.”

The smile weakened.

“Kade—”

“I made things worse. A lot.”

Elena watched him carefully.

Kade continued before anger chose the rest of the sentence for him.

“I did not turn things in because sometimes I wanted the zero more than I wanted somebody else deciding whether I was good enough. I walked out. I argued when I knew I was just arguing. I made teachers’ jobs harder.”

Darius looked at him like Kade had begun speaking another language.

“That part is mine,” Kade said.

Russell nodded carefully.

“But Northline helped because people here stopped treating compliance like the same thing as learning.”

Kade looked toward Ms. Alvarez.

“She helped.”

Ms. Alvarez became deeply interested in her coffee.

Kade turned back to Russell.

“You do not have to pretend you predicted Asterion.”

The room had gone quiet.

Russell cleared his throat.

“I am simply saying we are proud of you.”

Kade took a breath.

“That part I’ll take.”

Something eased in Russell’s face.

“Fair enough.”

Hensley looked less comfortable.

Then Ms. Rook spoke for the first time.

“I owe you an apology too.”

Kade turned.

She folded her arms.

“I knew some of your work was better than the grades reflected. I also got tired of fighting the system about it.”

Kade said nothing.

“I told myself you did not care enough for it to matter.”

That sentence landed differently because it came from someone he had liked.

“I made that easy to believe,” Kade said.

“Yes.”

Rook nodded once.

“Still sorry.”

Kade looked at her.

“Okay.”

No dramatic forgiveness.

No rewrite.

Just a true sentence placed where a false one had been.

That was enough.

Russell and the teachers stayed a few minutes, asked about Asterion, then left.

Darius stared at Kade.

“What?” Kade asked.

“That was very mature.”

“Take it back.”

“No.”

“I have a reputation.”

“Gone.”

“Ruined by personal growth.”

“Tragic.”

---

Later, Ms. Alvarez found Kade alone near the vending machine.

He bought one bag of pretzels.

The machine dispensed three.

Kade stared at them.

“Darius was right.”

“About?”

“It respects people.”

Ms. Alvarez leaned against the wall.

“You handled Russell well.”

“I had a good teacher.”

“Careful.”

“That sounded dangerous?”

“Very.”

Kade handed her one bag of pretzels.

She accepted it.

They stood quietly.

Then Ms. Alvarez said, “I owe you an apology.”

Kade looked at her.

She continued before he could joke.

“I saw that you were bored. Angry. Defensive. I understood more than some people did.”

“You did.”

“But sometimes I still thought if you would just make yourself fit better, things would become easier.”

Kade said nothing.

“I was wrong about part of that.”

The words hit harder than Russell’s revisionism.

Adults did not apologize to Kade often.

Not because he was always right.

He had been wrong frequently and creatively.

But institutions were excellent at arranging every conflict so the young person carried the entire moral weight of it.

“You weren’t wrong about all of it,” Kade said.

“No.”

“I was difficult.”

“You remain difficult.”

“Thank you.”

“You’re welcome.”

He smiled.

Ms. Alvarez opened the pretzels.

“You were also right sometimes for reasons you used badly.”

Kade looked at her.

“That sounds uncomfortably specific.”

“You noticed when assignments measured obedience more than understanding. Then you used that observation as permission not to do anything.”

Kade winced.

“Still teaching.”

“Habit.”

They ate pretzels.

Ms. Alvarez nodded toward Room Four.

“They watch you now.”

Kade looked through the door.

Darius was pretending not to look at him.

Tessa definitely was.

Two younger students whispered near the back.

“That seems unhealthy.”

“It is responsibility.”

Kade grimaced.

“Worse.”

“You do not have to become an inspirational poster.”

“Thank you.”

“But if you let them think Asterion happened because you were secretly perfect all along, that becomes another lie.”

Kade thought about the Student Media interview.

Measurements could be accurate and incomplete.

Stories too.

“Okay.”

Ms. Alvarez looked at him.

“Okay?”

“I am experimenting with teachability.”

“That sounds temporary.”

“Do not ruin it.”

Elena appeared at the end of the hallway carrying two paper cups and a folder thick enough to contain a criminal case.

“I found the visitor forms,” she said.

Kade stared at the folder.

“How many people are visiting?”

“Apparently each student requires medical clearance, media consent, emergency contact verification, transit authorization, and a statement acknowledging that an ocean exists.”

Ms. Alvarez took one of the cups.

“The ocean form is important.”

“It has three pages.”

“Dangerous ocean.”

Elena handed Kade the folder.

The weight pulled his arm down.

“You invited them,” she said.

“I did not invent administration.”

“A weak defense.”

Ms. Alvarez opened the folder against the vending machine. The exhibition request sat on top with a Meridian House seal in one corner and Julian’s precise digital signature at the bottom.

Kade had asked Julian because Julian understood which office could approve thirty visitors without producing a committee.

Julian had returned the signed form eleven minutes later, with a route plan, two dietary spreadsheets, and a note that read: *This is a good use of House access. Next time provide a head count first.*

Mara had added one line beneath it.

*Darius is not permitted to challenge anyone.*

She had never met Darius.

Kade had described him accurately.

Ms. Alvarez scanned the first page.

“Twelve places.”

Kade stopped smiling.

“I asked for thirty.”

“The ferry allocation approved twelve.”

“There are more than twelve students here.”

“Strong arithmetic today.”

He took the form back.

Twelve visitor badges.

One staff escort.

One group selected by Northline faculty according to academic readiness, attendance, and behavioral standing.

Kade read the criteria twice.

“No.”

Elena and Ms. Alvarez exchanged a look.

“No what?” Elena asked.

“If you select by attendance and behavior, the students who already know how to fit get the trip.”

Ms. Alvarez held his gaze.

“What do you suggest?”

The challenge in her voice was familiar.

Not opposition.

Work.

Kade looked through the Room Four window.

Marcus was redrawing the evacuation map neatly. Lacey had taken the marker from Darius before he could add an unauthorized route through the tire warehouse.

“Some places for people who are interested in Resonance work,” Kade said. “Some for people staff think would use it. Some lottery.”

“Lottery among whom?”

“Anyone who completes the safety preparation.”

“That is still a compliance gate.”

“A relevant one. They need to know ferry and field rules.”

Ms. Alvarez nodded slightly.

“What about students who cannot travel?” Elena asked.

Kade looked at the twelve badges again.

His first impulse had been to force the door wider.

Get Julian to call someone. Ask Sen. Spend the small amount of public leverage attached to Zero on a larger ferry allocation.

Maybe that would work.

It would still make the whole visit depend on him.

“Can the exhibition stream here?” he asked.

Ms. Alvarez pointed to the Room Four tablets.

“On those?”

“Eli can make anything talk to anything. The question will offend him.”

“Good.”

“And the twelve who go can run something for everyone when they get back. Not a speech. Stations. Arena maps, equipment, whatever they learn.”

Elena studied him over the rim of her cup.

“You have thought about this.”

“For forty seconds.”

“A personal record.”

Ms. Alvarez took the folder.

“I will draft selection rules. You will read them before making objections in public.”

“That removes my best setting.”

“You will endure.”

She walked toward Room Four with the paperwork.

Elena stayed beside Kade.

For a moment, neither spoke.

Then she said, “You know I used to sit in that room and ask people to give you one more chance.”

Kade looked through the glass.

“I know.”

“Sometimes I was asking them. Sometimes I was asking you.”

He ran a finger along the edge of the approved form.

“I know that too.”

“Do you?”

Elena’s voice was gentle, which meant the question was not.

Kade leaned back against the vending machine.

“Not then.”

She nodded.

The hallway heater engaged. The clock above Room Four paused, considered the new minute, and moved its hand late.

“I thought if I admitted I wanted it to work,” Kade said, “then everyone got to decide the failure was mine.”

“Some of it was yours.”

“I know.”

“Some was not.”

He looked at her.

Elena did not say *I always knew*.

She had not always known. Neither had he.

That honesty made room for both of them.

“Asterion is not proof they were wrong about everything,” he said.

“No.”

“It is not proof you were right about everything either.”

“Deeply unfortunate.”

“But you kept asking.”

Elena looked through the glass at Ms. Alvarez distributing forms.

“So did you. Your method was louder.”

Kade smiled.

She touched his shoulder once.

Not comfort exactly.

Recognition.

Then she nodded toward the room.

“Go tell them about the twelve places before they hear the number and start a government.”

“Darius would be an ineffective revolutionary.”

“Darius has snacks and free time. Never underestimate logistics.”

Kade went back inside.

---

Before leaving, Kade stood at the front of Room Four.

He had not planned to speak.

That was probably why it worked.

“Asterion is strange,” he said.

Darius raised a hand.

“We know.”

“Thank you.”

Kade looked around the room.

“I thought everybody there would be geniuses who had never failed anything.”

“Are they?” Tessa asked.

“Some.”

“Awful.”

“Deeply.”

People laughed.

Kade continued.

“There are also people there who messed up. People who came from alternative schools. People who are brilliant at one thing and terrible at another. People who are rank seven and still have family problems. People who can build a tactical simulator and cannot carry a rescue capsule without complaining for eight straight seconds.”

“Who is that?” Darius asked.

“My roommate.”

“I like him.”

“You would.”

Kade looked toward Ms. Alvarez.

She waited.

“And they’re not all better than you because they got there first,” Kade said.

The room quieted.

He felt self-conscious.

So he did the dangerous thing.

He kept going.

“Meridian has an exhibition next month. House event. I asked if Northline could come.”

Darius sat upright.

“Come where?”

“Asterion.”

The room erupted.

Questions hit from every direction.

How many could go?

Would they fly?

Could they see a real arena?

Would there be security checks?

Could Darius challenge somebody?

“No,” Kade said.

“I did not finish.”

“Still no.”

The excitement filled Room Four until even Ms. Alvarez raised her voice to get everyone seated.

Kade laughed.

He had expected them to be excited.

He had not expected how much it would mean to him to be the person who opened the door.

“There are twelve visitor places,” Ms. Alvarez said.

The room changed immediately.

Excitement became arithmetic.

Students counted each other. Friends leaned closer. Darius’s grin thinned as he looked from Tessa to the younger students at the back.

Kade recognized the moment the door became a ladder.

“Twelve on the island,” he said. “Everyone gets the exhibition stream here.”

Tessa raised her hand.

“That is not the same.”

“It isn’t.”

Kade held up the approval sheet.

“The ferry has limits. So do the visitor fields. I can ask for more, but I’m not going to promise more before someone signs it.”

“How do you pick?” a student near the window asked.

Ms. Alvarez nodded to Kade.

His problem now.

“Some places for people who want to study Resonance or emergency systems. Some chosen by staff. Some lottery among everyone who completes the safety preparation.”

Darius frowned.

“Staff can choose favorites.”

Ms. Alvarez took a long drink of coffee.

“An excellent opening argument for your application.”

“I believe in transparency.”

“Since when?” Tessa asked.

“Since places became limited.”

That was honest enough to earn a few laughs.

Kade tapped the paper.

“The selection rules will be posted before anyone applies. No secret categories. No ranking by who looks most impressive in a school brochure.”

Russell, mercifully, was no longer in the room.

“And the people who go come back with a job,” Kade continued. “You will run stations for everyone else. Equipment, arena rules, House systems, whatever you learn.”

“Can I run combat demonstrations?” Darius asked.

“You can demonstrate sitting through a safety briefing.”

“My weakest discipline.”

“Then train.”

The word came out in Julian’s tone.

Kade disliked that intensely.

Tessa caught it and laughed.

“You really did get weird.”

Maybe.

But the room no longer looked like twelve winners and everyone else.

It looked like a group with a constrained system to solve.

After the room settled, Marcus—the younger student from the evacuation project—raised one hand.

Kade pointed at him.

“What?”

“Do they take people from schools like this?”

The question was quiet enough that the room changed around it.

Kade looked at Marcus.

He thought about Sera.

Three alternative placements.

About himself.

About provisional lanyards and strange routes.

“Yes,” Kade said.

Marcus looked skeptical.

“How?”

Kade almost said he did not know.

That was true.

Instead he said, “Different ways.”

“Like Zero?”

“Hopefully not exactly.”

A few people laughed.

Kade continued.

“But you do not have to become somebody who went to the right middle school at eleven.”

Marcus nodded slowly.

The answer felt inadequate.

It was also honest.

Ms. Alvarez stepped beside Marcus’s desk.

“We have the regional aptitude calendar,” she said. “And application waivers. Anyone who wants them can ask me without announcing it to the room.”

Marcus’s shoulders lowered.

Kade understood the second offer hidden inside the first.

Ambition did not have to become a public performance before it was ready.

“I can answer questions too,” Kade said. “About the parts the website makes sound simple.”

Darius raised a hand.

“Can you answer questions about why the website has six different login screens?”

“No one can.”

“Then what good are you?”

“I have ferry access.”

“Temporarily useful.”

Marcus smiled and folded the aptitude date into his notebook.

Not a destiny.

A date.

Sometimes a door began that small.

As he and Elena walked back toward the car, Darius followed them outside.

“Kade.”

Kade turned.

Darius shoved his hands into his pockets.

“You coming back again?”

The old version of Kade would have made a joke.

Something about surviving Asterion.

Something technically true and emotionally useless.

Instead he said, “Yeah.”

Darius nodded.

“Good.”

Kade waited.

“That it?”

“That’s it.”

“Very moving.”

“Leave before I hug you again.”

Kade got into the car immediately.

Elena started the engine.

For several blocks, neither spoke.

Then she said, “You called Northline home.”

Kade looked out the window.

“I did not.”

“You did.”

“Audio evidence?”

“I have ears.”

“Unreliable.”

Elena smiled.

Kade watched the old neighborhood move past.

The grocery store where he and Darius had once spent an hour arguing over whether a security robot was legally allowed to follow them.

The bus stop where Elena had picked him up after his second suspension.

The street leading toward the high school he no longer attended.

Northline had not become less real because Asterion was larger.

Asterion was becoming home too.

The realization should have frightened him.

It did.

But fear no longer felt like a reason to leave.

That was the difference.

Kade looked at his reflection in the passenger window.

Two homes meant twice as many places that could hurt when something went wrong.

For the first time, he understood that belonging was not the opposite of vulnerability.

It was choosing vulnerability on purpose.

He was not sure he liked that.

He was fairly sure it was too late.

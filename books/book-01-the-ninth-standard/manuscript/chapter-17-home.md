# Chapter 17 — Home

Northline looked smaller than Kade remembered.

That annoyed him.

The building had not changed.

Same faded blue wall.

Same tire warehouse next door.

Same self-storage sign missing the lower corner of its S.

Same vending machine visible through the front window.

But after Asterion—after glass towers, arenas, seawalls, fields that bent trajectories, and ranking boards large enough to influence emotional weather—the old county records building looked as though somebody had quietly reduced it while he was gone.

Elena parked at the curb.

“You going in?”

Kade looked at the front doors.

“That was the plan.”

“You have been staring at it for three minutes.”

“I’m appreciating architecture.”

“Go.”

He opened the passenger door.

Then paused.

“Mom?”

Elena looked over.

“Thanks for coming.”

She smiled faintly.

“I wanted to see Ms. Alvarez.”

“Still counts.”

They walked in together.

The metal detector beeped at Elena’s keys.

Mr. Boyd, Northline’s security officer, looked up from a bicycle wheel he was repairing behind the desk.

“Mercer.”

“Boyd.”

Mr. Boyd examined him.

“You taller?”

“No.”

“Shame.”

“Good to be home.”

The word came out before Kade could stop it.

Home.

He pretended not to notice.

Elena noticed.

Of course she did.

She had the decency not to say anything yet.

The hallway smelled like floor cleaner, old carpet, and the chili somebody was reheating in the staff room. The ceiling seemed lower. The walls had more dents than Kade remembered. A motivational poster about *CHOOSING YOUR FUTURE* hung slightly crooked beside a fire extinguisher.

Kade had hated that poster for months.

Today it looked almost funny.

Room Four was louder than he remembered.

Darius saw him first.

He stood so fast his chair rolled backward into the wall.

“Zero Boy!”

Every head turned.

Kade closed his eyes.

“I came all this way for that.”

Darius crossed the room and hugged him hard enough to make breathing optional.

Then immediately pushed him away.

“That did not happen.”

“Agreed.”

Tessa shouted, “Show us your Conduit!”

“I do not have one.”

“Then what do they put in your neck?”

“Currently disappointment.”

Someone in the back laughed.

Ms. Alvarez stood beside her desk with both arms folded.

Her coffee was half full.

Some systems were reliable.

“Mercer,” she said.

“Alvarez.”

“You look less irritating.”

“Distance creates nostalgia.”

Her mouth twitched.

Then she hugged him.

Briefly.

No speech.

Kade preferred it that way.

Elena and Ms. Alvarez greeted each other with the tired familiarity of two people who had sat through too many meetings about the same teenager.

“Still alive?” Ms. Alvarez asked her.

“Barely.”

“Kade or emergency medicine?”

“Yes.”

Kade decided to pretend he could not hear them.

The students wanted stories.

Asterion fights.

Dorm rooms.

Food.

Famous students.

Whether the island really floated.

Whether Julian Cross was as good as the clips.

Whether Mara Vey was “actually that scary.”

Kade refused to answer the last question.

Darius noticed.

Unfortunately.

“Oh,” Darius said slowly.

“No.”

“I did not say anything.”

“You were about to.”

Darius grinned.

Kade considered returning to Asterion immediately.

Instead he spent the morning moving through the old rhythm.

Room Four’s tablets still froze if too many modules updated at once.

The white clock still stuttered when the heater switched on.

The chair at his old desk still leaned slightly left.

Kade sat there for ten minutes while Tessa worked on a geometry module and Darius pretended to work on one.

The strangest part was how quickly he remembered everything.

Not academically.

Physically.

Where to put his feet so the desk did not wobble.

Which fluorescent light buzzed.

The exact second Ms. Alvarez would look up if Darius’s silence lasted too long.

Asterion had not erased Northline.

It had made Kade aware of how much Northline lived inside him.

At ten seventeen, Darius stood.

“Bathroom.”

Kade looked at the clock.

Ms. Alvarez looked at Darius.

Darius looked at both of them.

Then slowly sat back down.

“Personal growth,” he said.

Kade laughed so hard Tessa threw an eraser at him.

Later, Ms. Alvarez asked Kade to sit in on one of the afternoon project blocks.

He almost refused out of reflex.

Then remembered he no longer attended Northline and therefore could not use school as an excuse to avoid school.

The project was simple: design an evacuation plan for the building during a power failure.

Darius immediately wanted to use the loading dock.

Tessa pointed out the dock gate failed closed without power.

A younger student named Marcus kept insisting the front entrance was best because it was the nearest.

Kade listened.

The old version of him would have taken over.

He could see the problem quickly.

Two narrow hallways.

One accessible exit.

The staff room creating a bottleneck.

Instead he asked Marcus, “What happens when everybody chooses nearest?”

Marcus looked at the map.

“They all go there.”

“And then?”

The kid frowned.

Darius leaned over.

“Traffic jam.”

Marcus changed his route.

Ms. Alvarez watched Kade from across the room.

Kade pretended not to notice.

When the exercise ended, Darius said, “You got weird.”

“I have always been weird.”

“No. You let him figure it out.”

“That sounds like teaching.”

“Exactly.”

Kade stared at him.

“Take it back.”

Darius refused.

---

At lunch, Kade sat at his old table and realized he had forgotten how bad the chairs were.

Darius leaned across from him.

“So you’re rank nineteen hundred now.”

“One nine-oh-four.”

“That is still terrible.”

“There are ten thousand people.”

“Sounds like excuses.”

Kade smiled.

Tessa dropped into the chair beside them.

“My aunt says you’re government property.”

“Which aunt?”

“Moon landing.”

“Reliable source.”

They laughed.

For a while it felt exactly the same.

Then two teachers from Kade’s old district high school appeared with Vice Principal Russell.

Kade recognized one immediately.

Mr. Hensley had taught sophomore physics.

He had once told Kade that “being technically correct while refusing the assignment is still failing.”

Kade had hated him for months.

The second teacher, Ms. Rook, had taught English and been one of the few people at Canyon Ridge who stopped arguing when Kade said he needed to leave a room before he said something worse.

Russell smiled broadly.

“Kade. I wanted some of your former teachers to say hello.”

Kade’s body tightened before his mind caught up.

Old reactions had better memory than people did.

Hensley shook his hand enthusiastically.

“We always knew you had extraordinary potential.”

Ms. Alvarez’s face became very still.

Kade noticed.

Russell continued.

“Everyone did.”

There it was.

Heat rose in Kade’s chest.

Not because the statement was completely false.

Because it was almost true in the most dishonest possible way.

Russell had told him he was smart.

Hensley had told him intelligence made every failure less excusable.

Meetings had become negotiations over how little trouble he could cause.

And now success had begun rewriting everyone’s memory.

Kade opened his mouth.

Ms. Alvarez said quietly, “Kade.”

One word.

He looked at her.

She was not telling him to be quiet.

She was telling him to choose.

That was different.

Kade turned back to Russell and Hensley.

“You thought I was smart,” he said.

Hensley smiled, relieved.

“Exactly.”

“You also thought being smart meant I should work inside a system that wasn’t working for me.”

The smile weakened.

“Kade—”

“I made things worse. A lot.”

Elena watched him carefully.

Kade continued before anger chose the rest of the sentence for him.

“I did not turn things in because sometimes I wanted the zero more than I wanted somebody else deciding whether I was good enough. I walked out. I argued when I knew I was just arguing. I made teachers’ jobs harder.”

Darius looked at him like Kade had begun speaking another language.

“That part is mine,” Kade said.

Russell nodded carefully.

“But Northline helped because people here stopped treating compliance like the same thing as learning.”

Kade looked toward Ms. Alvarez.

“She helped.”

Ms. Alvarez became deeply interested in her coffee.

Kade turned back to Russell.

“You do not have to pretend you predicted Asterion.”

The room had gone quiet.

Russell cleared his throat.

“I am simply saying we are proud of you.”

Kade took a breath.

“That part I’ll take.”

Something eased in Russell’s face.

“Fair enough.”

Hensley looked less comfortable.

Then Ms. Rook spoke for the first time.

“I owe you an apology too.”

Kade turned.

She folded her arms.

“I knew some of your work was better than the grades reflected. I also got tired of fighting the system about it.”

Kade said nothing.

“I told myself you did not care enough for it to matter.”

That sentence landed differently because it came from someone he had liked.

“I made that easy to believe,” Kade said.

“Yes.”

Rook nodded once.

“Still sorry.”

Kade looked at her.

“Okay.”

No dramatic forgiveness.

No rewrite.

Just a true sentence placed where a false one had been.

That was enough.

Russell and the teachers stayed a few minutes, asked about Asterion, then left.

Darius stared at Kade.

“What?” Kade asked.

“That was very mature.”

“Take it back.”

“No.”

“I have a reputation.”

“Gone.”

“Ruined by personal growth.”

“Tragic.”

---

Later, Ms. Alvarez found Kade alone near the vending machine.

He bought one bag of pretzels.

The machine dispensed three.

Kade stared at them.

“Darius was right.”

“About?”

“It respects people.”

Ms. Alvarez leaned against the wall.

“You handled Russell well.”

“I had a good teacher.”

“Careful.”

“That sounded dangerous?”

“Very.”

Kade handed her one bag of pretzels.

She accepted it.

They stood quietly.

Then Ms. Alvarez said, “I owe you an apology.”

Kade looked at her.

She continued before he could joke.

“I saw that you were bored. Angry. Defensive. I understood more than some people did.”

“You did.”

“But sometimes I still thought if you would just make yourself fit better, things would become easier.”

Kade said nothing.

“I was wrong about part of that.”

The words hit harder than Russell’s revisionism.

Adults did not apologize to Kade often.

Not because he was always right.

He had been wrong frequently and creatively.

But institutions were excellent at arranging every conflict so the young person carried the entire moral weight of it.

“You weren’t wrong about all of it,” Kade said.

“No.”

“I was difficult.”

“You remain difficult.”

“Thank you.”

“You’re welcome.”

He smiled.

Ms. Alvarez opened the pretzels.

“You were also right sometimes for reasons you used badly.”

Kade looked at her.

“That sounds uncomfortably specific.”

“You noticed when assignments measured obedience more than understanding. Then you used that observation as permission not to do anything.”

Kade winced.

“Still teaching.”

“Habit.”

They ate pretzels.

Ms. Alvarez nodded toward Room Four.

“They watch you now.”

Kade looked through the door.

Darius was pretending not to look at him.

Tessa definitely was.

Two younger students whispered near the back.

“That seems unhealthy.”

“It is responsibility.”

Kade grimaced.

“Worse.”

“You do not have to become an inspirational poster.”

“Thank you.”

“But if you let them think Asterion happened because you were secretly perfect all along, that becomes another lie.”

Kade thought about the Student Media interview.

Measurements could be accurate and incomplete.

Stories too.

“Okay.”

Ms. Alvarez looked at him.

“Okay?”

“I am experimenting with teachability.”

“That sounds temporary.”

“Do not ruin it.”

---

Before leaving, Kade stood at the front of Room Four.

He had not planned to speak.

That was probably why it worked.

“Asterion is strange,” he said.

Darius raised a hand.

“We know.”

“Thank you.”

Kade looked around the room.

“I thought everybody there would be geniuses who had never failed anything.”

“Are they?” Tessa asked.

“Some.”

“Awful.”

“Deeply.”

People laughed.

Kade continued.

“There are also people there who messed up. People who came from alternative schools. People who are brilliant at one thing and terrible at another. People who are rank seven and still have family problems. People who can build a tactical simulator and cannot carry a rescue capsule without complaining for eight straight seconds.”

“Who is that?” Darius asked.

“My roommate.”

“I like him.”

“You would.”

Kade looked toward Ms. Alvarez.

She waited.

“And they’re not all better than you because they got there first,” Kade said.

The room quieted.

He felt self-conscious.

So he did the dangerous thing.

He kept going.

“Meridian has an exhibition next month. House event. I asked if Northline could come.”

Darius sat upright.

“Come where?”

“Asterion.”

The room erupted.

Questions hit from every direction.

How many could go?

Would they fly?

Could they see a real arena?

Would there be security checks?

Could Darius challenge somebody?

“No,” Kade said.

“I did not finish.”

“Still no.”

The excitement filled Room Four until even Ms. Alvarez raised her voice to get everyone seated.

Kade laughed.

He had expected them to be excited.

He had not expected how much it would mean to him to be the person who opened the door.

After the room settled, Marcus—the younger student from the evacuation project—raised one hand.

Kade pointed at him.

“What?”

“Do they take people from schools like this?”

The question was quiet enough that the room changed around it.

Kade looked at Marcus.

He thought about Sera.

Three alternative placements.

About himself.

About provisional lanyards and strange routes.

“Yes,” Kade said.

Marcus looked skeptical.

“How?”

Kade almost said he did not know.

That was true.

Instead he said, “Different ways.”

“Like Zero?”

“Hopefully not exactly.”

A few people laughed.

Kade continued.

“But you do not have to become somebody who went to the right middle school at eleven.”

Marcus nodded slowly.

The answer felt inadequate.

It was also honest.

As he and Elena walked back toward the car, Darius followed them outside.

“Kade.”

Kade turned.

Darius shoved his hands into his pockets.

“You coming back again?”

The old version of Kade would have made a joke.

Something about surviving Asterion.

Something technically true and emotionally useless.

Instead he said, “Yeah.”

Darius nodded.

“Good.”

Kade waited.

“That it?”

“That’s it.”

“Very moving.”

“Leave before I hug you again.”

Kade got into the car immediately.

Elena started the engine.

For several blocks, neither spoke.

Then she said, “You called Northline home.”

Kade looked out the window.

“I did not.”

“You did.”

“Audio evidence?”

“I have ears.”

“Unreliable.”

Elena smiled.

Kade watched the old neighborhood move past.

The grocery store where he and Darius had once spent an hour arguing over whether a security robot was legally allowed to follow them.

The bus stop where Elena had picked him up after his second suspension.

The street leading toward the high school he no longer attended.

Northline had not become less real because Asterion was larger.

Asterion was becoming home too.

The realization should have frightened him.

It did.

But fear no longer felt like a reason to leave.

That was the difference.

Kade looked at his reflection in the passenger window.

Two homes meant twice as many places that could hurt when something went wrong.

For the first time, he understood that belonging was not the opposite of vulnerability.

It was choosing vulnerability on purpose.

He was not sure he liked that.

He was fairly sure it was too late.

# Chapter 21 — Midyear: Open Rounds

By midyear, Asterion had learned what to do with Kade Mercer.

Mostly.

Put him in an arena.

Measure everything.

Assign two medical technicians where one had previously seemed sufficient.

Try not to let him break the floor.

The Midyear Open began with 2,048 first-years and a bracket large enough to make Eli emotional. Qualifying pools had spent two days cutting the field to one hundred twenty-eight before the bracket rounds began.

“It’s beautiful,” he said.

“It’s a spreadsheet.”

“It’s destiny organized into columns.”

Kade studied the tournament rules instead.

The Open was not the ordinary challenge ladder. Rankings determined seeding, but advancement generated performance weighting based on opponent strength, point differential, clean technique, penalties, and later-round survival. It was possible to lose deep in the bracket and rise substantially.

Asterion had apparently found a way to make losing mathematically stressful.

Mara looked over his shoulder.

“You’ve read that paragraph four times.”

“I’m looking for ways the system can insult me after I lose.”

“It has many.”

“Comforting.”

Midyear Week had taken over the island.

Classes were reduced to morning seminars. House colors covered every bridge. Temporary arena boards stood in plazas that normally displayed research announcements. Students carried bracket projections instead of textbooks, though some faculty had responded by putting exam reminders directly onto the bracket feeds.

The change had begun before sunrise, when second-years crossed the residence bridges tying narrow strips of House cloth to the railings. Blue for Kepler. White for Sol. Green for Meridian. Gold for Lagrange. By breakfast the ocean wind had turned the whole campus into an argument made of flags.

Meridian's contribution included a strip above the west entrance that read **FIELD CONTROL IS TEMPORARY. MERIDIAN IS A CONDITION.**

Nobody admitted making it.

The lettering matched Eli's drafting templates exactly.

At seven, Kade attended a compulsory Tactical Systems seminar with thirty-one people pretending not to check their brackets under the table.

Instructor Hale stood before a freeze-frame of Julian's qualifier footage and waited.

One by one, wrist displays went dark.

“The Open evaluates adaptation under repeated observation,” Hale said. “By this afternoon, any opponent with a functioning network connection will have current footage of you. Your favorite solution expires every time you use it.”

He advanced the image. Julian was caught halfway through an entry, weight committed, rear heel raised.

“Cross. What is wrong here?”

Julian studied his own failure from the second row.

“I entered on an assumed retreat.”

“And?”

“My opponent had stopped retreating two exchanges earlier. I was solving the version of him I expected.”

Hale's gaze moved across the room.

“Mercer. What happens next?”

Three months ago, Kade would have guessed. He would have dressed the guess in confidence and hoped nobody knew the difference.

Now he traced the available force paths: Julian moving forward, opponent planted at an angle, barrier close on Julian's right.

“The obvious answer is that Cross gets redirected into the barrier.”

“Obvious answers are frequently answers.”

“Not this time. His opponent's lead foot is outside the useful angle. Cross gives up the first contact, takes the forearm instead, and rotates through before the field closes.”

Hale let the footage run.

Julian took the forearm, rotated, and turned a bad entry into a draw.

Several people looked back at Kade.

He kept his attention on the screen.

Hale said, “What did Mercer do correctly?”

A student from Sol answered, “Ignored the likely result.”

“More specific.”

Mara said, “Separated the physical position from the reputation of the people in it.”

Hale nodded.

Kade wrote that down.

Mara glanced at his tablet.

“You know he was complimenting you.”

“I was recording the date for legal purposes.”

Hale turned from the screen.

“Your assignment is to submit one prediction about your first opponent and one condition that would prove the prediction wrong. Before your match. Editing timestamps will be checked.”

The room objected as one organism.

“You are not being graded on accuracy,” Hale continued. “You are being graded on whether you can abandon a model when the evidence changes.”

Kade opened Dev Armitage's profile.

**Prediction: under pressure, returns to center through the shortest available route.**

He paused over the second field.

It was easy to write what he expected.

Harder to name the thing that would make him wrong.

**Disproof: chooses a longer reset after an outside strike despite an open center lane.**

He submitted it.

Across the aisle, Julian's tablet chimed at nearly the same moment.

“Race you to four correct revisions,” Julian said.

“That is a very specific competition.”

“I intend to win it.”

“Of course you do.”

The seminar ended at 07:32. Nobody left casually.

The halls split into currents: competitors toward equipment check, spectators toward the dining screens, faculty and medics toward the arena ring. Even the students pretending not to care moved fast enough to expose them.

At the bridge junction, an upper-year Meridian student held out a wooden bowl filled with green thread.

Kade stopped.

“What is this?”

“First-year Open marker.”

“What does it do?”

“Identifies you as a first-year in the Open.”

Kade looked at the tournament uniform he was already wearing.

The student shook the bowl once.

“Tradition does not have to survive cross-examination, Mercer.”

Kade took a thread.

Around him, Meridian students tied theirs at wrist, ankle, braid, zipper pull. Mara looped hers twice around the seal of her left glove. Sera tied one to the shoulder of Priya's warmup jacket when Priya's fingers would not hold still.

Eli tried to attach his to a tablet case.

“Body or uniform,” the upper-year said.

“My tablet is functionally both.”

“Navarro.”

Eli tied it around his wrist.

Kade fixed his to the strap over his right hand. The thread weighed nothing. Still, when the wind pulled at it on the way to breakfast, he kept checking that it remained there.

House Meridian had converted its common room into a command center nobody had officially requested.

Three screens tracked active matches.

One tracked House performance.

One showed a live bracket so large Eli had created filters, subfilters, and an alarming feature called *likely emotional devastation*.

Sera made him rename it.

He chose *upset probability*.

“Less honest,” he said.

“More normal,” Sera replied.

Kade’s first match was scheduled at 09:20.

Mara’s at 08:40.

Julian’s at 08:00 because rank one received the earliest seeded slot and, according to Julian, “somebody believes excellence is a breakfast activity.”

Meridian watched Julian’s first round from the dining hall.

It lasted fifty-one seconds.

His opponent actually scored first.

The whole room reacted.

Julian did not.

He reset, changed the engagement distance, and won the next three points without giving up another.

Kade leaned toward the screen.

Eli said, “You’re studying him at breakfast.”

“I am eating.”

“You stopped chewing.”

Kade started chewing again.

Sera looked at him.

“Convincing.”

Mara’s first match lasted two minutes and fourteen seconds.

She won 4–0.

She returned to the table carrying her own breakfast tray as if nothing had happened.

Kade looked at her.

“That seemed rude.”

“To whom?”

“Your opponent.”

“She scored no points.”

“That is what I meant.”

Mara sat.

“Your first opponent likes outside entries and overuses his rear hand when pressured.”

Kade stared.

“You finished your own match twelve minutes ago.”

“Yes.”

“And watched mine?”

“I watched his.”

“Different kind of concerning.”

Taren slid into the chair beside her.

“I have notes too.”

Kade looked at him.

“Of course you do.”

Taren pushed over a short profile.

Most of it matched public footage.

One line did not.

**Left ankle taped yesterday after conditioning. Not injured; probably stiffness.**

Kade looked up.

“How do you know that?”

“People train in public.”

“That is not the same as me seeing it.”

“I see more people.”

Plausible.

Kade marked the note mentally and ignored the ankle anyway.

If the opponent was injured, he did not want the win that way.

If he was not, targeting it would be pointless.

At 09:02, Kade’s phone buzzed.

Darius.

**ROOM FOUR HAS YOU ON THE BIG SCREEN**

A photograph followed.

Northline’s classroom display showed the Asterion bracket while Ms. Alvarez stood beneath it with both arms folded.

Someone had written **DO HOMEWORK DURING ADS** on the whiteboard.

Kade smiled.

Another message arrived from Elena.

**I am working. I am NOT watching.**

Then, thirty seconds later:

**Why is your warmup shirt inside out?**

Kade looked down.

It was.

He turned it around before anyone else noticed.

Eli noticed.

He said nothing.

That was friendship.

Equipment check occupied the twelve minutes before Kade's first call.

A technician weighed his forearm guards, tested both field seals, and ran a calibration wand along the edge of his shoes. The wand chirped at the right heel.

“Again,” the technician said.

Kade lifted his foot.

The second scan passed.

“What was wrong?”

“Salt residue at the contact strip.”

“From what?”

“You live on an island.”

Fair.

At the next station, Dev Armitage held both arms out while another technician checked the thin redirection nodes sewn into his sleeves. He caught Kade looking.

“Checking for illegal equipment?” Armitage asked.

“Trying to determine whether the dramatic pose is required.”

“It helps.”

Kade held out his arms when his technician pointed.

Armitage nodded solemnly.

“See?”

The prep corridor beyond inspection had six marked squares, one for each competitor due in the next rotation. Kade took the last empty one and began the footwork sequence Julian had forced on him for weeks: forward angle, recover, lateral step, recover, reverse without crossing.

His body wanted speed.

Speed made the sequence sloppy.

He slowed until each foot landed under him.

Through the glass, the crowd appeared in pieces between structural supports. Green cloth in one section. A blue Kepler banner in another. Cameras tucked beneath the scoreboard. Somewhere beyond them, Room Four and Elena were watching the same feed with a delay of less than two seconds.

That knowledge tightened his shoulders.

He reset the sequence.

Forward angle.

Recover.

Sera's voice came through the open prep door.

“Your bag.”

Kade turned.

She held up the duffel he had packed to leave Asterion. He had brought it to the arena because all competitors needed spare clothes and because carrying a different bag would have required owning one.

The zipper was open.

One sock hung out.

“That is intentional ventilation.”

Sera pushed the sock inside and closed the zipper.

“Sure.”

For one sharp second, Kade remembered the same bag packed to leave Asterion.

It sat now beneath a bench beside five other Meridian bags, inconvenient and ordinary.

Sera put it back.

“Your feet stopped moving.”

He returned to the marked square.

Forward angle.

Recover.

This time the rhythm held.

The first opponent was Rank #1,611.

Three months earlier, that number would have felt impossible.

Now it felt dangerous in a useful way.

His name was Dev Armitage, House Kepler, confirmed Redirection with a compact defensive style and enough tournament experience that his pre-match expression contained no interest in Kade’s story.

Kade appreciated that.

They met at center before the barrier sealed.

Armitage said, “No floor tricks.”

Kade sighed.

“Apparently I need a shirt.”

“What would it say?”

“IT WAS TWICE.”

Armitage laughed.

Then the horn sounded.

Armitage opened with exactly the outside entry Mara had predicted.

Kade's rear foot moved to answer before he made a decision. He caught himself, shifted the recovery angle, and let Armitage's first strike pass close enough to stir the fabric at his shoulder.

No point.

Armitage recovered through center.

The prediction on Kade's tablet became one event instead of an answer.

The match lasted four minutes.

Kade won without touching Boundary architecture.

That mattered more than the projected rank gain.

Armitage took the first point by forcing Kade to retreat straight instead of angling out.

The impact was legal and precise. Kade crossed the boundary with both feet and heard the Kepler section rise around him.

At reset, Armitage glanced at Kade's left shoulder.

Not the taped ankle Taren had marked in the profile.

Not the easiest weakness available.

Kade let the thought go before it became gratitude. Armitage wanted a clean match too.

Kade took the second by refusing the same pressure.

He angled off, caught Armitage's sleeve at the permitted contact band, and turned both of them until Armitage stepped out first.

It was not elegant.

It counted.

Third exchange: no score.

Fourth: Armitage nearly caught him with a field-assisted turn Kade had only seen twice on film.

The redirection pulled at Kade's forearm guard and tried to make his own momentum finish the throw. He dropped his center, lost the attack, and stayed upright by half a step.

Armitage smiled as they separated.

There was no story in the expression. Only a fighter pleased that another fighter had understood the question.

Fifth: Kade recognized that after every failed outside strike, Armitage returned to center through the same narrow route.

He did not exploit it immediately.

Julian’s training had finally become expensive enough to use.

Kade waited.

The pattern repeated.

Then again.

On the final exchange, Kade gave Armitage the outside lane, let him miss, and occupied the return route before Armitage arrived.

Armitage recognized the trap after his weight committed. His taped ankle never entered the calculation. He tried the longer recovery Hale's assignment had made Kade name that morning.

For half a second, the prediction was disproved.

Kade abandoned it.

Instead of closing center, he turned with Armitage, cut across the longer route, and touched the scoring band at his shoulder.

Boundary point.

Buzzer.

Kade caught himself smiling.

Not because he had tricked someone.

Because he had planned far enough ahead to know exactly why it worked.

Armitage shook his hand.

“You waited three exchanges for that.”

“Four.”

“Worse.”

“People keep telling me.”

Instructor Hale met Kade at the exit gate.

“No symptoms?”

“No.”

“No pressure behind the eyes?”

“No.”

“Good.”

Kade waited.

Hale frowned. “What?”

“I expected criticism.”

“You won clean.”

“That sounds almost approving.”

“Do not make this unpleasant.”

Kade grinned.

The medical technician scanned his pupils anyway.

Kade held still.

Three months ago he would have complained about being singled out.

Now he understood that unusual consequences justified unusual monitoring.

He still disliked it.

Understanding did not require enthusiasm.

Tournament days contained far more waiting than broadcasts admitted.

Kade had ninety minutes before Round Two.

He showered, ate half a meal, and reached Arena Four in time to watch the last seven minutes of Priya's first round.

Priya fought like every exchange had insulted her family.

Her opponent, a Lagrange kinetic, had nearly a head of height and enough raw output to make the barrier brighten whenever he committed. Priya answered by staying close, where his reach became crowded and his larger motions had no room to develop.

The score stood 2–2.

The Lagrange fighter drove forward.

Priya planted.

For an instant Kade expected her to meet force with force. Two months ago, she would have. Her field flared around her shoulders.

Then she released the brace, stepped across the line of pressure, and let him spend his output on empty space.

He caught the mistake fast. Not fast enough.

Priya hooked an arm inside his guard and touched the scoring band at his ribs.

The barrier chimed.

Point.

Match.

The Meridian section erupted. Priya did not look at them until she had shaken her opponent's hand and cleared the field.

Then she spotted Kade at the rail.

“You missed the good part.”

“I saw you avoid a bad decision.”

“Exactly. Historic.”

Her left hand shook when she reached for the water Sera offered.

Sera caught the bottle before it slipped.

“Medical.”

“I am fine.”

“Your hand disagrees.”

Priya took the bottle with her right hand.

“My hand is dramatic.”

Sera was already guiding her toward the recovery corridor.

Kade followed because his own next arena lay in the same direction and because the lie *I am fine* had become too familiar to ignore.

The recovery hall was quieter than the arenas without being quiet. Barrier impacts traveled through the walls as soft concussions. Shoes squeaked on sealed flooring. Medics spoke in low voices beneath a display listing rest times, neurologic holds, and mandatory withdrawals.

Nessa sat two bays down from Priya. Her own bracket had ended early after a Perception overload.

She wore dark glasses in the recovery hall.

“Too much?” Kade asked.

“Too many fields at once.”

“You okay?”

“Will be.”

Nessa tilted her head toward the arena noise beyond the wall.

“People think Perception means seeing more.”

“It doesn’t?”

“It means being unable to stop seeing more when you need less.”

She pressed two fingers against the bridge of the glasses.

“Every active field leaves a shape. Usually I can choose the relevant one. In there, six practice arenas were running under us, two live barriers shared the wall, and somebody in the stands kept pulsing a projection band.”

“That is allowed?”

“The band? Unfortunately. Having terrible taste remains legal.”

Priya leaned around the divider from her bay.

“Was it orange?”

Nessa winced.

“Do not describe colors right now.”

Priya withdrew.

“Sorry.”

Nessa lowered her hand.

“I knew the overload was building. I stayed because the score was tied.”

Kade looked at the withdrawal notice glowing beside her name.

“Worth it?”

“I lost the next exchange because I could see the barrier anchors more clearly than the person hitting me.”

“So no.”

“Ask me tomorrow.”

Kade thought about Boundary architecture.

The arena becoming one system.

The cold line.

“Sounds familiar.”

Nessa looked at him.

The dark lenses hid her eyes, but not the direction of her attention.

“You know before it happens?” she asked.

Kade flexed the fingers of his right hand. The green thread shifted against the strap.

“Sometimes.”

“Sometimes.”

Nessa nodded toward the mandatory withdrawal display.

He did not explain.

She did not ask.

A medic cleared Priya with a twenty-minute minimum rest and cleared Nessa to sit in darkness until the world contained fewer edges.

Sera adjusted the divider to block more of the corridor light.

It was a small motion, competent and automatic.

On Sera's wrist, the preliminary Disaster Response application band showed a pending amber mark.

Kade pointed at it.

“Still nothing?”

“Still pending.”

“You have checked recently?”

“Six minutes ago.”

From behind the divider, Nessa said, “Four.”

Sera glared at a person who could not see her.

Kade smiled.

At eleven, Eli lost his first engineering-control bracket event—not combat, but a paired systems tournament running in parallel with the Open.

Kade watched that one too.

The engineering hall occupied a converted calibration lab below Arena Two. No crowd chants. No announcer. Just eight workstations, a row of clear safety partitions, and judges who looked professionally immune to excitement.

Each pair received the same task: stabilize a simulated emergency power grid through three load spikes, one component failure, and a forced restart. Highest retained control won. Teams had twelve minutes to inspect their hardware before the simulation began.

Eli and his partner spent eleven of those minutes arguing.

“The secondary sink is under capacity,” his partner said.

“It only has to catch the restart transient.”

“Which will exceed capacity.”

“For point three seconds.”

“That is still a measurement of time.”

Eli pointed at the control graph.

“If we move capacity from primary, the first spike takes us out before restart matters.”

His partner stared at the graph, then at the clock.

“I dislike you.”

“Useful. Lock channel three.”

They locked it with eight seconds remaining.

Across the partition, the Kepler pair had built a brutal single-peak controller. It held the initial surge almost perfectly. Their score jumped ahead.

Meridian's rig allowed a wider deviation, corrected without oscillation, and settled.

Second load spike.

Kepler held again.

Meridian bent, recovered.

The component-failure light turned red.

Kepler's primary controller vanished from the model. Their graph plunged, rebounded past safe range, and stabilized two seconds later.

Meridian's system handed load to the undersized secondary sink. Warning amber flooded the station. Eli's partner swore under her breath. Eli's hands moved over the controls, shaving demand from three noncritical branches while the sink absorbed the transient.

The amber cleared.

Their system recovered without crossing red.

Kade looked up at the scores.

Kepler remained ahead.

“How?” he asked.

Sera sat beside him in the narrow spectator row.

“Peak control weighting.”

“They lost the system.”

“For less time than Meridian spent outside the preferred band.”

“That seems stupid.”

“Navarro has mentioned it.”

The forced restart began.

The secondary sink on Meridian's rig exceeded its listed capacity for three-tenths of a second.

It held.

Eli slapped the cutoff exactly as the primary channel returned. The system settled into a clean recovery curve.

Across the partition, Kepler restarted faster. Their controller overshot once, dropped a noncritical branch, and returned to full control with a peak number the rubric loved.

The final board read **KEPLER: 91.4. MERIDIAN: 88.7.**

Eli's partner leaned both hands on the station.

Eli stared at Kepler's graph.

One of the judges approached with the neutral expression of someone entering dangerous weather.

“Your recovery architecture was excellent,” she said.

“Then why is their number larger?” Eli asked.

“The event measures total control performance.”

“They dropped a hospital branch in the simulation.”

“A noncritical branch.”

“It was labeled surgical ventilation.”

“Within the simulation, the label does not change its weighting.”

Eli opened his mouth.

His partner put one hand on his shoulder.

“Thank you, Judge.”

She moved him toward the exit before his respect for procedure suffered permanent damage.

He returned to Meridian furious.

“Their stabilizer is worse.”

Sera handed him food.

“They won.”

“Because the rubric rewards peak control instead of recovery efficiency.”

“They won.”

“The rubric is wrong.”

Eli's partner dropped into the chair opposite him.

“The rubric measures what it says.”

“Then it says the wrong thing.”

“That is not the same as us winning.”

Eli stabbed a fork into the food Sera had handed him.

“We built the system that comes back.”

Sera sat on the edge of the table.

“Then build it again somewhere recovery matters more than a scoreboard.”

Eli stopped moving.

The anger did not disappear. It reorganized.

“Civilian stabilization,” he said.

“You already applied.”

“My fellowship model uses peak retention because the academy benchmark uses peak retention.”

He pulled his tablet closer, food forgotten.

“I need a recovery metric.”

Eli's partner looked at Sera.

“You should not encourage him during meals.”

“I gave him food. What he does with it is his problem.”

Kade looked at him.

“Welcome to my childhood.”

Eli stared.

Then laughed despite himself.

Ordinary disappointment continued alongside Kade’s tournament.

That mattered.

Not everything in Meridian existed to support his bracket.

The second round paired him with Rank #1,204, Luis Ortez, a short, compact fighter who treated Kade’s reach advantage as a personal challenge.

Ortez shook Kade’s hand before the match.

“You lean when you think.”

Kade frowned.

“What?”

The barrier sealed.

That was the trap.

Ortez entered under his guard and drove him halfway to the boundary before Kade understood the problem.

Point Ortez.

The left shoulder had dipped before Kade moved.

Not much. Less than an inch.

Enough for Ortez, who had entered on the signal instead of the opening.

Kade rolled the shoulder once at reset. The motion only made him more aware of it.

Ortez waited across center with his hands loose.

He had offered Kade a fact and turned Kade's attention into the weapon.

Kade reset.

Julian’s voice returned from months of private sparring.

*You solve the problem you’re currently in instead of the next one.*

Kade changed the question.

Instead of trying to stop Ortez’s entry, he gave him a lane that looked useful and narrowed at the final step.

Second exchange: draw.

Ortez reached the expected position. Kade met him there with both feet set, but Ortez had enough forward control to keep either of them from scoring.

They separated.

“Still leaning,” Ortez said.

Kade checked the impulse to correct it.

“Your concern means a lot.”

On the next reset, he let the shoulder dip.

Ortez moved on the cue.

Kade was already stepping outside the entry.

Third: Kade by two points.

Fourth: Ortez, after Kade tried the same false cue again.

The imitation was obvious this time. Ortez stopped short, caught Kade rotating toward an attack that never arrived, and tagged the scoring band at his back.

Kade returned to center with heat climbing his neck.

One successful adjustment had become a favorite solution in less than a minute.

Hale's morning lesson was becoming irritatingly efficient.

The deciding exchange came with both of them breathing hard and the crowd mostly watching another arena.

Kade planned the counter before Ortez committed.

Same offered lane.

Different endpoint.

Ortez entered.

Kade pivoted outside rather than retreating.

Ortez turned with him. For an instant they were shoulder to shoulder, each fighting for a clean line without enough leverage to force it.

Kade's lead foot neared the boundary.

The arena offered itself at the edge of awareness: floor beneath both stances, barrier anchor two steps away, a connected system he could redefine.

He ignored it.

Instead he released Ortez's sleeve, gave up the stronger grip, and used the sudden space to finish the pivot on balance.

Ortez crossed first.

Point.

Match.

Julian, visible two sections away waiting for his own next bout, saw it and nodded once.

Kade pretended not to notice.

He noticed.

Ortez shook his head as they left.

“You fixed the shoulder thing.”

“No. I stopped thinking about you telling me.”

“Also useful.”

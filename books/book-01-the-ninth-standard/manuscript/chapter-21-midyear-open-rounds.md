# Chapter 21 — Midyear: Open Rounds

By midyear, Asterion had learned what to do with Kade Mercer.

Mostly.

Put him in an arena.

Measure everything.

Assign two medical technicians where one had previously seemed sufficient.

Try not to let him break the floor.

The Midyear Open began with 2,048 first-years and a bracket large enough to make Eli emotional.

“It’s beautiful,” he said.

“It’s a spreadsheet.”

“It’s destiny organized into columns.”

Kade studied the tournament rules instead.

The Open was not the ordinary challenge ladder. Rankings determined seeding, but advancement generated performance weighting based on opponent strength, point differential, clean technique, penalties, and later-round survival. It was possible to lose deep in the bracket and rise substantially.

Asterion had apparently found a way to make losing mathematically stressful.

Mara looked over his shoulder.

“You’ve read that paragraph four times.”

“I’m looking for ways the system can insult me after I lose.”

“It has many.”

“Comforting.”

The first opponent was Rank #1,611.

Three months earlier, that number would have felt impossible.

Now it felt dangerous in a useful way.

The match lasted four minutes.

Kade won without touching Boundary architecture.

That mattered more than the projected rank gain.

He won because he kept his stance under pressure, because he did not chase a feint, and because he recognized that his opponent always returned to center after a failed outside strike.

Boring things.

Excellent things.

When the buzzer sounded, Kade caught himself smiling.

Instructor Hale met him at the exit gate.

“No symptoms?”

“No.”

“No pressure behind the eyes?”

“No.”

“Good.”

Kade waited.

Hale frowned. “What?”

“I expected criticism.”

“You won clean.”

“That sounds almost approving.”

“Do not make this unpleasant.”

Kade grinned.

The second round paired him with Rank #1,204, a short, compact fighter named Luis Ortez who treated Kade’s reach advantage as a personal challenge.

Kade lost the first exchange badly.

Ortez entered under his guard and drove him halfway to the boundary before Kade understood the problem.

Julian’s voice returned from months of private sparring.

*You solve the problem you’re currently in instead of the next one.*

Kade changed the question.

Instead of trying to stop Ortez’s entry, he gave him a lane that looked useful and narrowed at the final step.

Second exchange: draw.

Third: Kade by one point.

Fourth: Ortez.

The deciding exchange came with both of them breathing hard and the crowd mostly watching another arena.

Kade won because he planned the counter before Ortez committed.

Not improvisation.

A plan.

Julian, visible two sections away, saw it and nodded once.

Kade pretended not to notice.

He noticed.

Round three was Rank #883.

An upper-ladder first-year named Jae Min who had cleaner field control than anyone Kade had beaten before.

Halfway through the match, Min redirected a shoulder check through Kade’s stance and the arena changed at the edge of Kade’s perception.

Floor.

Anchors.

Wall.

A connected path waiting to be used.

The cold line formed behind his eyes.

Kade could have reached for it.

He knew that now.

He also knew what came after.

Tremor.

Neurologic lag.

The possibility of force resolving somewhere neither fighter intended.

He let the sensation go.

Min scored.

Kade heard a few disappointed reactions in the crowd.

That irritated him.

Not because they wanted him to win.

Because they wanted the strange thing.

So he won the ordinary way.

It took six minutes, two lost points, a painful sweep, and one correction Mara had taught him so many times it now appeared in his dreams.

Rear foot late.

Center stable.

Kade took the final exchange by timing Min’s reset.

When he left the arena, Park checked his pupils anyway.

“Any symptoms?”

“I thought about it.”

Her gaze sharpened.

“And?”

“Didn’t use it.”

Park nodded.

No praise.

That made the approval more meaningful.

By the fourth round, people were coming specifically to watch him.

Not because he was favored.

Because he was unpredictable.

Kade preferred being underestimated to being observed.

Asterion had removed that option.

The next opponent, Rank #503, was named Hana Bisset.

She was better than him.

Kade recognized that in the first minute.

Her footwork was cleaner.

Her field timing was faster.

She did not repeat obvious patterns.

For three exchanges, Kade learned almost nothing except how many ways a forearm could hurt without causing injury.

Then he noticed one tiny habit.

After a successful inside counter, Bisset exited left.

Not always.

Enough.

Kade built a plan around a probability instead of certainty.

It nearly failed.

He took a point he did not expect.

Lost the next.

Survived a boundary push by centimeters.

On the final exchange, Bisset countered inside.

Kade gave her the exit.

Left.

She took it.

He was already there.

The buzzer sounded with six seconds remaining.

Kade was on one knee.

Bisset stood over him, breathing hard.

Then she laughed.

“You waited five minutes for that.”

“Four and a half.”

“That is worse.”

She offered a hand.

Kade took it.

“You got me once,” she said.

“It only needed to count once.”

“Next time it won’t.”

“I was hoping you’d say that.”

His live tournament projection jumped into the low hundreds.

Eli stared at the number as though it had personally proposed marriage.

“Do you understand what this means?”

“I have survived four rounds.”

“It means the recalculation model has stopped treating you like a statistical accident.”

“That sounds healthy.”

“It’s progress.”

Sera handed Kade a towel.

“You look terrible.”

“Also progress.”

Mara arrived carrying water and a replay tablet.

“You’re drifting left when tired.”

“I know.”

“You’re still doing it.”

“That’s different.”

She handed him the bottle.

Their fingers touched.

Neither reacted.

That was technically true.

Eli turned away with exaggerated dignity.

Sera smacked the back of his head.

Kade drank.

Mara scrolled through the replay.

“You almost reached for it against Min.”

He looked at her.

“You saw?”

“Your focus changed.”

“That is unnerving.”

“You didn’t use it.”

“No.”

“Why?”

Kade looked toward the medical bay.

“Because winning one tournament is not worth finding out which wall breaks next.”

Mara’s expression softened by a fraction.

“Good.”

Kade raised an eyebrow.

“That was praise.”

“Do not make it unpleasant.”

He smiled.

The bracket board refreshed.

Eli made a noise.

Not quite a word.

Kade turned.

The next pairing filled the display.

**QUARTERFINAL — VEY, MARA (#7) vs. MERCER, KADE (LIVE PROJECTION: #286)**

Kade stared.

Eli stared harder.

Sera said, “Well.”

Taren appeared behind them with two cups of coffee and no obvious reason to know exactly where they were.

He read the board.

“Oh, this is excellent.”

Kade looked at him.

“For whom?”

“Everyone except you.”

Across the prep level, Mara had already seen the pairing.

She turned.

Their eyes met.

And Mara Vey smiled.

Not politely.

Not accidentally.

Like a person who had finally been given a problem she had wanted for months.

Kade sighed.

“Yes,” he said. “This is probably going to be terrible.”

# Chapter 16 — Famous for Tuesday

Kade became famous at 08:12 on a Tuesday.

By 08:19, he wanted to stop.

The first interview request came from Asterion Student Media.

The second came from a national sports channel.

The third came from a company that sold training gloves and described itself as “performance-adjacent,” which Kade thought sounded suspicious.

At 08:21, his House Trial fall appeared in a looping clip set to dramatic orchestral music.

At 08:26, someone added the caption:

**WHEN YOUR PLAN IS SO GOOD IT WORKS BETTER AFTER YOU LEAVE**

Kade almost liked that one.

By breakfast, **ZERO TO 1904** was trending on Asterion’s internal network.

Eli loved every second.

“This one has six hundred thousand views.”

“Stop telling me.”

“This one says you’re mysterious.”

“I live twelve feet from you.”

“That has not improved clarity.”

“This one thinks you and Vey are secretly—”

Kade snatched the tablet.

“No.”

Eli looked delighted.

“I did not finish.”

“You did not need to.”

Taren was worse.

“You need a media strategy.”

“No.”

“You already have one. It is currently ‘look uncomfortable and say something dry.’”

“That has served me well.”

“Exactly. We refine it.”

Sera walked by carrying coffee.

“Do not refine him.”

“Thank you,” Kade said.

“I’m protecting everyone else.”

Mara refused to discuss the attention at all.

Kade appreciated this until he discovered she had declined three requests to comment on him.

“You were asked?”

“Yes.”

“What did they want?”

“Whether you are as difficult in person as you appear publicly.”

Kade waited.

Mara looked at him.

“I declined to answer.”

“That was kind.”

“It was efficient.”

“Recovered.”

The strangest part was not attention from strangers.

It was attention from people who had ignored him before.

Someone had saved him a seat in Vector Analysis.

Not Mara. She sat where she always sat, second row near the aisle, with three sharpened pencils aligned beside her tablet.

The empty chair was in the center of the room.

A second-year Kade vaguely recognized had one hand resting on its back.

“Mercer. Here.”

Kade stopped beside the chair.

“Why?”

The second-year blinked.

“It’s a seat.”

“There are eleven others.”

“This one has a good board angle.”

Kade looked at the board.

Then at the camera lens clipped discreetly to the student’s collar.

“Does the angle improve if I sit in it?”

The student covered the lens with one hand.

“That is for notes.”

“Of course.”

Kade took his usual seat in the back.

Mara did not turn around.

“You are developing survival instincts.”

“Fame has changed me.”

Professor Chao entered carrying a stack of physical paper, which meant somebody was about to suffer deliberately.

She began returning the previous week’s transfer-network proofs face down.

Kade received a sixty-one.

Across the aisle, the student with the collar camera glanced over.

Kade put one hand over the score.

“Private academic record.”

“You designed the House Trial relay.”

“And apparently skipped two conservation steps here.”

Professor Chao heard him.

“Three.”

“Thank you for the correction.”

Several people laughed.

Before the Final, the laughter would have made the back of Kade’s neck hot. Now it sounded friendly, which was somehow more dangerous. Friendly laughter invited him to become the version of himself people expected.

Professor Chao drew a municipal flywheel network on the board: six storage nodes, two variable loads, one maintenance shutdown.

“Mercer,” she said.

Kade looked up.

“This feels targeted.”

“The academy currently believes you can solve systems after falling off them. Come demonstrate the less theatrical method.”

Twenty-four faces turned toward him.

Mara kept writing.

Kade went to the board.

The network was ordinary. No hidden operator. No disputed boundary. No elegant trick waiting beneath the wording. Just six coupled equations and the tedious discipline of carrying every term.

He searched for the strange angle anyway.

People expected one.

He redrew the maintenance shutdown as an active condition rather than an absence.

Professor Chao tilted her head.

“Why?”

“The unavailable node changes the distribution constraints.”

“Correct. Which the conventional model already represents with this zero.” She tapped the board. “Continue.”

Kade continued.

He dropped a negative sign in the third equation.

Mara’s pencil stopped.

Somebody near the front whispered, “Is that part of it?”

Kade stared at the sign.

For one irrational second, he wanted to announce that it was. A planned error. A setup. The beginning of something clever enough to justify everyone looking at him.

Then he erased it.

“No,” he said. “That was wrong.”

The room changed.

Only slightly.

Expectation losing air.

Professor Chao nodded toward the remaining equations.

“Again.”

Kade started again.

This time he carried every term.

It took nine minutes. Nothing surprising happened. The flywheels balanced, the lights in the hypothetical district stayed on, and Kade returned to his seat with chalk on his sleeve.

Professor Chao faced the room.

“Competence is what remains when the clip is boring.”

She looked directly at the collar camera.

“And recording in my classroom without unanimous consent remains a conduct violation.”

The camera disappeared.

After class, a professor who had marked Kade absent twice without learning his name stopped him to say the House Final strategy had shown “interesting systems intuition.”

Kade thanked him because Elena had raised him correctly despite limited evidence.

Two students from House Atlas asked if he wanted to review the match with them.

Then the second-year with the hidden camera invited him to a training group that had rejected Eli a month earlier.

Kade declined.

The pleasure arrived sharp and immediate.

He disliked it enough to add, “If you need someone who understands distributed control, ask Eli directly.”

The second-year’s expression tightened.

“We asked you.”

“I noticed.”

Kade walked away before the answer could become a speech.

A first-year who had called him an administrative error three weeks earlier sent a message that began, **Always knew you were going to climb**.

That one made Kade laugh hard enough that Eli came over to see why.

Kade showed him.

Eli frowned.

“I do not remember them always knowing.”

“Memory is unreliable.”

“Do not use my own wisdom against me.”

The attention reached Northline by lunchtime.

Darius sent a screenshot of the school’s front-office television showing Kade’s House Trial clip on a regional sports segment.

**RUSSELL IS PRETENDING THIS IS NORMAL**

A second message followed.

**ALVAREZ SAID TURN IT DOWN BECAUSE SOME OF US ARE TRYING TO FAIL MATH IN PEACE**

Kade smiled.

Then Tessa sent a photo of Room Four’s whiteboard.

Someone had written:

**MERCER — #1,904**

Underneath:

**STILL CANNOT FIX VENDING MACHINE**

That one made the fame feel different.

Less like being watched.

More like being remembered by the right people.

At 13:17, Darius called.

Kade answered expecting another clip.

Instead he got a tilted view of Northline’s front office and half of Darius’s forehead.

“There are reporters outside,” Darius whispered.

Kade sat up.

“What?”

“Two. One has local-news hair. The other keeps asking whether Room Four students are dangerous.”

Behind him, Ms. Alvarez crossed the office carrying a cardboard box.

“Darius, if you are hiding behind the filing cabinet to livestream this, I will assign you fractions.”

“This is not a livestream.”

“Then Mercer can hear me directly. Stop filming the windows.”

Darius rotated the tablet.

Through the blinds, Kade could make out a camera operator beside the curb. A reporter stood under Northline’s faded sign rehearsing a serious expression.

The image made Kade’s stomach tighten.

Asterion had gates, media offices, armed security, and an entire department whose job appeared to be saying *no comment* in several languages.

Northline had Mr. Boyd and a bicycle wheel.

“Did they talk to anybody?” Kade asked.

“Tessa told them you once stole a county bus.”

“I did not.”

“They did not believe her, which has damaged her confidence.”

Ms. Alvarez took the tablet.

Her face filled the screen.

“They are leaving.”

“How?”

“I explained that every student here is a minor and our consent policy is written in words short enough for television.”

“You have a consent policy?”

“As of seven minutes ago.”

Kade looked again toward the blinds.

“I’m sorry.”

Ms. Alvarez’s expression hardened.

“For what?”

“They are here because of me.”

“They are here because somebody sent adults with cameras to bother children at school.”

“Still.”

“Mercer. You are not responsible for every fool who learns your name.”

Kade went quiet.

Ms. Alvarez glanced toward the front doors.

“But if a national network calls you, tell me before Darius answers the office phone and offers a package rate.”

From somewhere behind the filing cabinet, Darius said, “That was private enterprise.”

“That was fraud.”

“The economy is complicated.”

Ms. Alvarez ended the call.

Kade stared at the blank screen.

Being remembered by the right people meant strangers could find them too.

At fourteen hundred, Cal Reid sent:

**Nice climb. Enjoy the week.**

Kade showed Taren.

Taren read it once.

“He is congratulating you while predicting your irrelevance.”

“I respect efficiency.”

“He is also reminding you he has been famous here longer.”

“Less efficient.”

Taren handed the tablet back.

“Do not answer.”

Kade looked at him.

“That is surprisingly mature advice.”

“I contain hidden depths.”

“Eli owns that phrase.”

“I stole it.”

“More believable.”

---

The Student Media interview happened because Kade made the mistake of believing an internal academy outlet would be less strange.

The interviewer was a third-year student named Lena Cho who had prepared well enough to make him nervous.

They sat in a small studio overlooking the western training fields.

A camera drone hovered silently three meters away.

Lena said, “You went from last in the first-year cohort to 1,904 in less than a month.”

“Apparently.”

“Do you think your original ranking was wrong?”

Kade considered the question.

A month earlier, he would have said yes because the alternative hurt.

Now he thought about Park’s baseline.

“I think it measured what it measured.”

Lena waited.

“That is a very Asterion answer.”

“I’m adapting.”

“So it was accurate?”

“At the things they tested, probably.”

“And incomplete?”

Kade looked at her.

“Also probably.”

Lena smiled slightly.

“Do you think that applies to people?”

That was annoyingly good.

Kade looked toward the training fields.

“Most numbers probably do.”

“Even 1,904?”

“Especially 1,904.”

Lena changed subjects.

“Your tactical role in the House Trial became more valuable after your elimination. Does that bother you?”

Kade laughed.

“It did for about three seconds.”

“And after?”

“I realized that was the point.”

“What point?”

“If the plan only works while I’m there, it is not a very good plan.”

Lena sat back.

Kade immediately regretted saying something usable.

“That is going to be the clip, isn’t it?”

“Yes.”

“Tragic.”

The interview spread because Kade said fewer ridiculous things than expected and two accidentally thoughtful things.

Eli told him that was terrible branding.

The national sports request was different.

Their producer wanted a remote segment called **FROM ZERO TO HERO**, which Kade rejected on title alone.

Taren argued the segment would reach twelve million viewers.

Sera asked whether twelve million viewers would help Kade pass Vector Analysis.

Taren admitted they would not.

Mara said nothing until Kade declined.

Then she said, “Good.”

Kade looked at her.

“You had an opinion?”

“Obviously.”

“Why did you not say it?”

“Because it was your decision.”

The answer should not have mattered.

It did.

The first open practice after the interview drew spectators.

Meridian’s west training room had one narrow gallery behind armored glass. On normal afternoons it held injured students, bored friends, and people waiting for a court to open.

That afternoon every seat was occupied.

More students stood two deep along the rear wall.

Instructor Grae looked through the glass, then back at Kade.

“Popular.”

“Temporary.”

“So is oxygen. Still affects performance.”

Grae paired Kade with Mara for a boundary-control drill. The court projected four silver zones between them. Score by crossing two in sequence while preventing the other operator from doing the same. No structural manipulation. No boundary redefinition. Conventional movement, field pressure, and timing.

The first exchange began.

Kade shifted left.

Mara took the center.

The gallery leaned toward the glass as one body.

He could almost hear what they wanted.

A trap.

A reversal.

Something that would look inevitable after it worked.

Kade abandoned the open right zone and cut behind Mara, trying to turn her control of the center into a route around the perimeter.

She pivoted once and drove a compact field pulse through his lane.

His foot crossed the outer line.

The court sounded a penalty tone.

Mara claimed two zones before he recovered.

“One-zero,” Grae called.

The second exchange lasted eleven seconds.

Kade feinted toward the same perimeter route, doubled back, and attempted to pull Mara’s field pressure across both center zones at once.

She did not follow.

She simply stepped into the space he had surrendered.

“Two-zero.”

Whispers moved through the gallery.

Kade’s pulse climbed.

Mara returned to her start mark.

“You are fighting the version of you they came to watch.”

“He seems demanding.”

“He is also losing.”

The third exchange began.

Kade looked at the four zones.

Only the four zones.

Mara took the center again.

This time Kade did not punish her for it. He let her own the stronger position and took the two adjacent routes she could not cover simultaneously. She closed one. He crossed the other.

Then he waited.

Mara adjusted.

He moved on the adjustment and claimed his second zone.

The court chimed.

“Two-one,” Grae said.

The gallery made a disappointed sound.

The point had been clean, patient, and almost impossible to clip into anything dramatic.

Kade smiled.

Mara’s eyes narrowed.

“Why are you pleased?”

“I was boring.”

“Do not become attached to it.”

She won the drill five-three.

Kade made her work for the last point.

When the gallery emptied, most of the spectators left without approaching him. They had come for the boy who fell through a platform and defeated a House after being eliminated.

Conventional losses did not support the story.

Grae handed Kade a towel.

“Useful afternoon?”

Kade wiped sweat from his face.

“I learned oxygen is temporary.”

“Asterion education remains unmatched.”

---

The sponsor meeting was worse.

Kade agreed only because Elena told him money was real even if attention was foolish.

The representative met him in a glass conference room near the arena complex. She wore a perfect smile and knew his public history better than some teachers who had actually taught him.

Northline.

Suspensions.

Zero.

Last place.

House Trial.

Every bad moment had become a bullet point with market value.

“We think your story connects,” she said.

Kade looked at the presentation behind her.

His face appeared beside large silver words.

**BREAK THE BOUNDARY**

His stomach tightened.

“Who gave you that phrase?”

The representative blinked.

“What phrase?”

“Boundary.”

“It’s campaign language. We have used versions of it for years.”

Kade stared at the screen.

Coincidence.

Probably.

Still.

“What would you want me to do?”

“Product appearances. Training content. A short personal feature. We would build around the outsider angle.”

“The outsider angle.”

“Alternative school to Asterion. Last place to—”

“I know the story.”

The representative paused.

Kade realized she was not being cruel.

That somehow made it worse.

She was doing her job. His failures had simply become usable material.

“How much?” he asked.

She gave him a number.

Kade stopped being offended for several seconds.

That was enough money to cover months of Elena’s rent.

Enough to replace the car.

Enough that his mother would tell him to think before being proud.

Kade looked again at his own face beneath BREAK THE BOUNDARY.

“Who owns the story?” he asked.

The representative frowned slightly.

“The campaign?”

“My story.”

“You do.”

“Then I decide what parts you use.”

“We would negotiate approvals.”

“That means no.”

“It means shared creative control.”

Kade almost laughed.

There was that word again.

Control.

He looked at the slide showing Northline’s exterior behind his face.

“Did you ask Northline before using that photo?”

The representative checked her tablet.

“It is licensed news imagery.”

“That was not what I asked.”

She went quiet.

Kade closed the presentation.

“I need time.”

“Of course.”

He ended the meeting early anyway.

Outside, Taren waited against the corridor wall.

Kade stopped.

“How did you know when the meeting ended?”

Taren lifted his wrist display.

“Your calendar.”

“My calendar is private.”

“House team schedule permissions.”

Kade frowned.

“That sounds almost legitimate.”

“Best kind.”

Taren fell into step beside him.

“How much?”

Kade told him.

Taren whistled.

“You considering it?”

“I don’t know.”

“Good.”

Kade looked over.

Taren shrugged.

“Money makes people answer too fast in both directions.”

Another surprisingly mature answer.

Kade was beginning to find those suspicious.

They crossed the arena plaza.

A group of younger visitors recognized Kade and asked for a picture.

He nearly refused automatically.

Then one girl said, “I’m from a continuation school.”

Kade stopped.

“Where?”

“Phoenix.”

She looked about fifteen and deeply embarrassed to have admitted it.

The other students quieted around her.

Kade knew that quiet.

It was the silence after someone gave strangers information they could use to sort them.

“Mine was a converted county office next to a tire warehouse,” he said.

The girl looked up.

“Ours used to be a grocery store.”

“Better parking.”

“The freezer room is counseling now.”

“That seems efficient.”

Her mouth twitched.

One of the academy guides hovered several meters away, uncertain whether the exchange belonged on the official tour.

The girl said, “My counselor showed us your fight.”

Kade braced for the speech people kept building out of him.

“She said you started last and got to nineteen hundred.”

“That part happened.”

“Did you know you were going to?”

“No.”

The answer came too fast to make impressive.

Good.

Kade continued.

“I thought Asterion made a mistake letting me in. Then I thought they were trying to prove something by keeping me last. Then I nearly left.”

The girl studied him.

“Why didn’t you?”

Kade glanced at Taren.

At the training complex behind them.

At the distant Meridian tower.

“People made leaving harder than staying.”

“That sounds bad.”

“It was inconvenient.”

She smiled fully this time.

“Did they fix everything?”

“Absolutely not.”

The tour guide shifted.

Kade lowered his voice.

“Do not let a school use being imperfect as an excuse to be careless with you. But do not decide you hate every open door just because most of them closed.”

The girl nodded slowly.

Kade hoped the sentence was true.

He knew it was at least more true than the clips.

Kade took the picture.

Then another with the rest of the group.

The girl checked the first image and grimaced.

“I look terrified.”

“I am told cameras make people perform themselves.”

“What does that mean?”

“No idea. My father used to say it.”

The sentence escaped before Kade could examine it.

The girl accepted the answer without knowing it was complicated.

They took a third picture.

After they left, Taren said nothing.

That was the correct thing.

Kade appreciated it.

The sponsorship contract arrived during dinner.

Twenty-six pages.

Kade opened it in Meridian’s common room, read the first paragraph four times, and understood less on each attempt.

Taren sat across from him working on a strategy review.

He waited almost a full minute before saying, “You are making the face.”

“I have one face.”

“The contract face has more hostility.”

Kade turned the tablet around.

“Tell me what this means.”

Taren looked at the page.

“It means you need the student advocate’s office.”

“You wanted a media strategy.”

“Media is people. Contracts are traps with page numbers.”

Eli leaned over the back of the couch.

“What contract?”

Kade pulled the tablet closer.

Eli read upside down anyway.

“Why do they need biometric performance data?”

Sera looked up from a disaster-response manual.

“They need what?”

Within thirty seconds, the contract had become a House problem.

Kade objected.

Nobody moved.

Mara took the tablet and scrolled to the campaign description.

“They call you an authentic outsider seven times.”

“I was hoping repetition would make it meaningful.”

“It makes it a product category.”

Eli had opened a second copy and was searching the permissions section.

“They can retain training telemetry for product research.”

Sera held out one hand.

“Give me that.”

Eli passed it over.

She read silently.

“No injury exclusion. If he collapses during sponsored training, they can use the footage.”

Kade looked at Taren.

“How much hostility is on my face now?”

“Appropriate amount.”

Taren moved beside him and opened a blank document.

“What do you actually want?”

“Not this.”

“That is a useful limit. It is not an answer.”

Kade looked at the compensation figure again.

The number had not become less real because the language around it was ugly.

“I want Mom to take fewer night shifts for a while.”

Nobody joked.

He continued.

“I want Northline to get something if Northline is in the campaign. I do not want them using Darius or Tessa. I do not want to explain every suspension I ever got so somebody can put sad music under it.”

Mara set the tablet on the table.

“Then those are conditions.”

“They can say no.”

“So can you.”

The words landed with the same weight they had after the national interview.

Not advice about which choice to make.

Permission to have one.

Taren began a list.

No Northline images without the school’s direct approval.

No other students.

No medical or biometric data retention.

No campaign ownership of the word *Boundary*.

Kade added, “No zero-to-hero.”

Eli nodded solemnly.

“A moral necessity.”

Sera pointed to the termination section.

“They can leave if your ranking drops. You cannot leave if the campaign misrepresents you.”

Taren added mutual termination.

Mara found an approval clause that gave Kade three business hours to object to edited material.

“During school days,” she said.

“Generous.”

They changed it to three days.

When they finished, the list of conditions was longer than the campaign summary.

Kade read it once.

“They are never agreeing to this.”

Taren shrugged.

“Then you will know what the money was purchasing.”

Kade sent the document to the student advocate’s office for review.

Relief did not come.

Neither did disappointment.

The money remained real.

So did the price.

That evening he called Elena.

She answered from a hospital break room wearing scrubs, hair pulled back, vending machines glowing behind her.

“You look tired,” Kade said.

“So do you.”

“I’m famous.”

“So I heard.”

“You sound impressed.”

“I changed your diapers.”

“Cruel.”

Her expression softened.

“You okay?”

Kade looked around the empty study room he had chosen for privacy.

“I liked it for about six minutes.”

“Being famous?”

“Being seen.”

Elena waited.

Kade continued.

“Then people started acting like they knew me.”

“That is the tax.”

“Helpful.”

“You called.”

He smiled.

“I got offered a sponsorship.”

“How much?”

He told her.

Elena’s eyebrows rose.

“Real money,” she said.

“That is what you said this morning.”

“And?”

“They want the Northline story.”

“Of course.”

“You don’t sound offended.”

“I work in emergency medicine. If somebody offers real money because the worst day of your life photographs well, I can be offended after checking the contract.”

Kade laughed.

Then he told her about the licensed photo.

Elena’s expression changed.

“They used the school without asking?”

“Legally.”

“That was not my question.”

Kade smiled.

“Family resemblance.”

Elena leaned closer to the camera.

“Remember something.”

“What?”

“The people who knew you before the ranking moved are information.”

Kade looked through the glass wall.

Eli was outside making faces because he could see the call was serious.

Sera walked past and pushed Eli’s face away from the glass.

Mara appeared farther down the corridor carrying books.

Taren waved from behind her.

Kade laughed.

“Yeah,” he said. “I think I have some data.”

Elena looked at him for a moment.

“Your father hated being photographed.”

Kade’s smile faded slightly.

The Aaron references had become rare enough that each one felt deliberate.

“Why?”

“He thought cameras made people perform themselves.”

“That sounds annoyingly like something I would say.”

“Yes.”

Elena looked away for half a second.

Then the hospital intercom called her department.

“I have to go.”

“Mom.”

She paused.

“What did Dad do?”

Her expression became careful.

“He worked in industrial systems.”

“I know the official sentence.”

“Kade.”

He heard the warning.

Not now.

He let it go.

“Okay.”

“I love you.”

“Love you too.”

The call ended.

Kade stared at the blank screen for several seconds.

Then Eli pressed his face against the glass again.

Kade laughed and opened the door.

---

Fame lasted longer than Tuesday.

But by Wednesday, another student broke an academy record.

By Thursday, a celebrity upper-year announced a transfer to an orbital training program.

On Friday morning, Kade left Meridian ten minutes early.

He took the central path to Vector Analysis instead of the covered route behind the library. The central path crossed two residence quads, the academy store, and the plaza where visiting media usually set up.

Nobody stopped him.

A pair of first-years ran past arguing about the new record. Three upper-years stood under the transfer display comparing orbital programs. The barista at the plaza kiosk called him *Mercer* rather than *the House Trial guy*, then charged him full price.

Kade reached class six minutes early.

The center seat no longer waited for him. Somebody’s bag occupied it.

He went to the back.

Professor Chao returned his corrected flywheel proof with an eighty-four and a note beneath it.

**THE BORING METHOD APPEARS TO WORK.**

Kade put the paper away before anyone could ask about it.

After class, the second-year with the collar camera was waiting in the corridor with Eli.

The camera was gone.

“I asked him directly,” the student said.

Eli adjusted his bag.

“They are building a distributed sensor model and apparently nobody checked whether their nodes agree on time.”

“Do they?” Kade asked.

“Not even slightly.”

The second-year looked between them.

“Is that a yes?”

Eli considered the question with more seriousness than Kade thought it deserved.

“It is a request for your error logs.”

“I sent them.”

“Then maybe.”

The answer belonged to Eli.

Kade kept walking.

At lunch, he checked the House Trial clip.

Three point two million views.

He refreshed.

The number did not move.

He set the tablet face down.

Then turned it over and refreshed again.

Still nothing.

Sera sat across from him with a bowl of soup and watched the third attempt.

“Are you trying to make the number increase through intimidation?”

“I’m testing the platform.”

“Obviously.”

Kade locked the screen.

“I thought this part would be more relieving.”

“People leaving you alone?”

“People getting bored.”

Sera waited.

Kade looked toward the ranking wall, where footage of the orbital transfer had replaced his interview.

“A small part of me misses it.”

That admission bothered him less once it existed outside his head.

She shrugged.

“Everybody likes applause.”

“I thought you were going to say something wise.”

“No. Applause feels good. Just don’t confuse it with love.”

Kade went quiet.

Sera looked at him.

“That one landed?”

“Unfortunately.”

She stole a fry from his tray.

“Good.”

Kade looked across Meridian Commons.

Eli was arguing with an instructor about drone regulations.

Taren was telling a story to three upper-years and somehow making all of them listen.

Mara sat near the window reading, one foot hooked around the chair leg.

Priya had joined Anton at another table.

Nobody looked at Kade because he had become famous.

They looked at him because there was an empty chair.

Kade picked up his tray and crossed the room.

He passed the message wall on the way.

Someone had changed the old insult.

**ZERO NEEDS TRAINING WHEELS** had been crossed out.

Underneath, somebody had written:

**APPARENTLY TRAINING WORKED**

Kade laughed.

Then sat down with his House.

Famous or not, he knew where he wanted to be.

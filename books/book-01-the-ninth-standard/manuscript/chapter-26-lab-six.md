# Chapter 26 — Lab Six

The explosion happened six minutes after they left the archive.

That was long enough for Kade to begin believing they had escaped.

It was also long enough for Eli to complain about Taren’s routing choice.

“You understand,” Eli said as they crossed the eastern engineering wing, “that there were four shorter ways back to Meridian.”

Taren kept walking.

“Three.”

“Four.”

“Service stair three is locked after nineteen hundred.”

Eli looked at him.

“It is not.”

“It was yesterday.”

“How do you know?”

Taren glanced over one shoulder.

“This feels like a bad time to criticize my knowledge of doors.”

Sera said, “It is always a good time now.”

Nobody laughed.

That was new.

Before the archive, Taren could turn almost any silence into something lighter.

Now every useful thing he knew came with a second question attached.

Where did you learn that?

Who told you?

Who else knows?

Kade hated the questions.

He hated more that they were fair.

The engineering wing was mostly empty. Midyear projects had ended two days earlier, leaving fabrication bays quiet and classroom glass dark. A cleaning drone hummed along one wall, paused to avoid them, then resumed its patient war against footprints.

Eli stopped beside Lab Five long enough to peer through the glass.

A half-assembled stabilization frame occupied the center table.

“My fellowship panel meets Friday,” he said.

Kade looked at him.

“You are thinking about that now?”

“I am trying to think about literally anything else.”

“That seems healthy.”

“My mother says compartmentalization is either a useful skill or a future therapy bill.”

“Your mother sounds practical.”

“She raised me.”

“Evidence against.”

Eli almost smiled.

Then he looked back at the stabilization frame.

“If I get the fellowship, they let me use Systems Lab fabrication without a faculty sponsor.”

“You told me.”

“I know.”

“You are telling me again.”

“That is how anxiety works.”

Mara slowed beside them.

“What are you building first?”

Eli’s expression changed immediately.

The fear from the archive did not disappear.

It moved aside for something he loved.

“Portable bridge stabilizer.”

Sera looked interested.

“For rescue?”

“Eventually. Cheap enough for municipal crews. Current field braces are priced like collapsed buildings are a luxury market.”

Sera nodded once.

“When you make one, Response will break it for you.”

Eli brightened.

“That is the nicest thing you have ever offered me.”

“Not a compliment.”

“It is in engineering.”

Kade watched them.

A month ago, he had thought of Eli mostly as his roommate.

Then as his friend.

Now Eli had a fellowship interview, a civilian hardware plan, opinions about municipal procurement, and a future Kade had nothing to do with.

Kade liked that.

It made friendship feel less like ownership.

Taren stopped at the next junction.

“East service bridge or central hall?”

Sera answered first.

“Central. More cameras.”

Taren flinched.

Sera saw it.

“So they can see us,” she said. “Not so you can.”

“I know what you meant.”

“Good.”

They took central hall.

The corridor widened near Lab Six.

Kade knew the room mostly because Eli complained about it.

Lab Six contained thermal-transfer hardware, coolant systems, and an old experimental Conduit test bed that had been grandfathered through three generations of safety standards because replacing it would apparently cost enough money to make administration discover tradition.

Eli called it the museum that could explode.

Normally there were warning lights over the access doors.

Tonight one of them blinked amber.

Then green.

Then amber again.

Eli stopped walking.

Not slowed.

Stopped.

Kade took three more steps before noticing.

“What?”

Eli stared at the maintenance panel beside the door.

The small diagnostic light repeated the sequence.

Amber.

Green.

Amber.

“That is wrong,” Eli said.

Sera looked back.

“How wrong?”

“Not broken wrong.”

Eli stepped closer.

“Watched wrong.”

Kade’s skin tightened.

Taren went pale.

Mara asked, “What does that mean?”

Eli opened his tablet and pulled up a network map.

“I have been seeing phantom maintenance pings in Lab Six for weeks.”

Kade stared at him.

“Weeks?”

“I thought it was an orphaned monitor.”

“English.”

“Old service software asking whether Lab Six still exists.”

“Does that happen?”

“At Asterion? Constantly. Half the island is new systems pretending the old systems are dead.”

His fingers moved faster.

“But these pings are not requests.”

Mara leaned closer.

“What are they?”

“Status acknowledgments.”

Sera said, “To whom?”

“That is the interesting question.”

Kade looked at him.

“You said weeks.”

Eli’s mouth tightened.

“I noticed them while I was tracing the hidden surveillance layer before the archive.”

“You did not mention Lab Six.”

“I had eighteen unexplained nodes.”

“That sounds like something you mention.”

“I was going to.”

“When?”

Eli looked at him.

“After we stopped committing archive crimes.”

Fair.

Taren said, “Directorate overlay.”

Eli turned.

“You know?”

“Not specifically.”

“Excellent distinction.”

Taren stepped toward the panel but stopped before touching it.

“They use passive status mirrors. If they compromise a facility, they do not need full control. They need to know what state it is in.”

“Why?” Mara asked.

“To act when the system is vulnerable.”

Nobody spoke.

The diagnostic light flashed red.

Eli’s face changed.

His joking expression disappeared so completely that Kade felt fear before he knew why.

“What?”

Eli enlarged the thermal network.

One branch had turned orange.

Then red.

“That valve is not supposed to be open.”

Sera moved beside him.

“What happens if it stays open?”

“Coolant pressure climbs in the lower chamber.”

“How long?”

Eli looked at the numbers.

“Not long.”

Taren said, “Move.”

Eli did not.

He opened a service channel.

Kade recognized the ugly interface.

It was the same kind of maintenance network Eli used for unauthorized drone tests.

A list of channels appeared.

Most had names.

One said only:

**NULL-7**

Kade pointed.

“What is that?”

“Dead channel.”

“Meaning?”

“Supposed to be unmonitored. Legacy maintenance route. I use it when I need test packets that do not clutter live logs.”

Sera stared.

“You have an unmonitored campus network.”

“No.”

“You just said—”

“I said supposed to be.”

Eli sent one diagnostic packet.

It vanished.

No acknowledgment.

He sent another through the standard network.

A reply returned instantly.

Then a second reply.

From somewhere else.

Eli went still.

“Someone is mirroring status.”

Taren’s face lost color.

“Directorate.”

“Could be.”

“Eli.”

“Fine. Very probably Directorate.”

The lower pressure reading jumped.

Eli closed the tablet.

“Move.”

Nobody argued.

They ran.

The first blast was not loud.

It was pressure.

The corridor floor rose under Kade’s feet as if the building had inhaled.

He hit the wall shoulder-first.

Air disappeared from his lungs.

A second later the sound arrived.

Deep.

Metallic.

Wrong.

Alarms screamed through the eastern wing.

Emergency shutters dropped from the ceiling.

Sera rolled beneath the first.

Mara followed.

Kade slid through as the gap narrowed.

Taren came after him.

Eli did not.

Kade turned.

Eli stood on the other side of the descending pressure door, staring at his tablet.

“Eli!”

“The coolant chamber is venting!” Eli shouted.

“Then get through!”

“If it vents into the upper lab, it takes the whole level!”

The gap beneath the door was less than half a meter.

Eli looked at the service map.

Then at them.

Kade saw the decision happen.

“No.”

Eli’s eyes met his.

“Kade—”

“No. Get through the door.”

“I can isolate it from inside the service control.”

“Then let emergency crews do it.”

“The controller is compromised. Remote commands are being rejected.”

Sera dropped to one knee beside the gap.

“How long before upper vent?”

Eli checked.

“Maybe ninety seconds.”

“Can a response team reach the local control in ninety?”

He did not answer.

That was answer enough.

Kade flattened himself to the floor.

The opening had narrowed to thirty centimeters.

“Eli.”

His friend looked down at him.

Kade wanted to say something useful.

Do not be stupid.

Wait.

I need you.

All of them sounded like commands.

Eli’s whole life had just spent a chapter becoming larger than Kade.

The worst possible moment to remember that.

Eli gave him a frightened smile.

“If I get the fellowship, tell them this counts as practical experience.”

“Eli.”

“I know.”

“Do not—”

The pressure door sealed the rest of the sentence.

Eli turned and ran toward Lab Six.

Kade hit the door with both palms.

“ELI!”

No answer.

Mara was already at the emergency panel.

“Manual override locked.”

Taren slammed his revoked access badge against the reader.

Nothing.

“I burned the credential,” he said.

Kade spun on him.

“Then unburn it.”

“I cannot.”

“Try harder.”

The wall shook again.

This time fire flashed behind the reinforced window.

Kade saw white.

Then smoke.

Then nothing.

He reached for the whole system before deciding to.

Door.

Wall.

Pressure.

Structure.

The cold line formed behind his eyes.

A route appeared in his mind.

Force from the chamber.

Seal frame.

Wall anchors.

He could change where the pressure resolved.

Maybe.

He had never done anything like it deliberately.

Sera grabbed his wrist.

“Kade.”

“I can open it.”

“You do not know that.”

“He is in there.”

“I know.”

“I can—”

“You are standing beside a damaged thermal lab full of unstable field hardware.”

Her voice cut through him.

“If you guess wrong, you make it worse.”

The cold line sharpened.

Kade looked at the window.

Smoke.

Pressure.

Eli somewhere beyond it.

Every part of him wanted power to mean not having to wait for someone else.

That was exactly why he could not use it.

He let the perception go.

Pain remained behind his eyes anyway.

Sera released his wrist.

“Good.”

Kade hated her.

For three seconds.

Because she was right.

Emergency teams arrived in fifty-four seconds.

Kade counted every one.

Responders in sealed armor flooded the corridor. Sera immediately began giving them the information she had: number of people, last known position, lab hazards, direction Eli had run, suspected compromised remote control.

She became someone else under emergency lights.

Not calmer.

More precise.

This was what she wanted to do with her life.

Kade understood that now.

A response officer shoved a portable map toward her.

“Which route?”

Sera pointed without hesitation.

“Service spine C. Do not enter from west stair. Pressure differential will trap your team.”

The officer looked at her identification.

“You Response Corps?”

“Candidate.”

He nodded once and followed her recommendation.

Another responder asked, “Local controller?”

Kade answered before Sera could.

“Navarro went for it.”

The responder looked at the sealed door.

“Student?”

“Yes.”

“Training?”

“Systems engineering.”

The responder’s face did not change.

“Then if he reached it, he may have bought us time.”

Kade held onto the sentence.

May have.

Bought time.

Not dead.

Not yet.

He stood uselessly against the wall.

That was almost unbearable.

Mara stayed beside him.

Not touching.

Close.

Taren remained across the corridor, hands empty.

For one second Kade thought he had disappeared.

Then realized Taren had stepped away to speak to an emergency terminal.

Kade noticed himself noticing.

He hated Taren for making noticing necessary.

Taren returned.

“What?” Kade demanded.

“I sent Directorate credentials to Sen.”

“Your burned ones?”

“The backend token still has audit history. Maybe they can trace who touched Lab Six.”

“Maybe?”

“That is all I have.”

Kade wanted more.

Everyone around him kept having only what they had.

It was becoming a theme.

After seventeen minutes, the first rescue team emerged with two injured engineering students.

Neither was Eli.

Thirty-three minutes.

A faculty technician.

Not Eli.

Forty-one.

A responder came out alone.

His helmet was scorched.

Kade knew before the man spoke.

“Lower lab is compromised. Field-stabilized coolant flooded the chamber. Thermal sensors are unreliable.”

“Navarro?” Mara asked.

The responder looked at the list.

“Not located.”

Not located.

Kade held onto those words.

Not dead.

Not located.

“Local controller?” Sera asked.

“Closed.”

The responder looked at her.

“Someone manually isolated the upper vent before failure.”

Kade stopped breathing.

“Eli.”

“We do not know.”

“It was him.”

The responder did not argue.

“He may have prevented a much larger release.”

May have.

Again.

Kade had never hated uncertainty more.

---

The official casualty notice did not post for two hours.

That delay was its own kind of torture.

Meridian students filled the common room without anyone calling a meeting. People who barely knew Eli sat anyway. Nessa brought food nobody touched. Priya paced from window to door and back. Anton repaired the same training strap three times.

Kade stood near the wall display.

The casualty page remained blank.

Eli’s bed was eight floors above them.

Kade could feel it like a location on a map.

At one point his phone rang.

Elena.

He declined it.

Then immediately texted:

**Alive. Eli missing. Cannot talk yet.**

She replied:

**I love you. I’m here.**

No demand.

No advice.

Kade put the phone away.

Mara sat beside him on the floor.

“You should drink something.”

“No.”

“That was not a question.”

“You have been spending too much time with Sera.”

“Probably.”

She handed him water.

Kade took it.

On the far side of the room, Taren stood alone.

Nobody had told him to leave.

Nobody invited him closer.

He looked more isolated than Kade had ever seen him.

For once, Taren did not try to fix the room with conversation.

The casualty page updated.

Every person in Meridian went still.

Five injured.

Two critical.

One missing, presumed dead.

**ELIAS NAVARRO**

Kade read the name until it stopped looking like language.

No body recovered.

The lab collapse had damaged the thermal chamber and flooded the lower level with coolant designed to remain stable under field stress. The same properties that prevented a larger explosion made normal recovery impossible until the system cooled.

Technical language.

Clean language.

Merciful language.

Presumed dead.

Someone behind Kade started crying.

He did not turn to see who.

The page contained one additional line.

**INCIDENT STATUS: CONTAINED. EXTERNAL ACCESS UNDER INVESTIGATION.**

External access.

The clue should have made Kade angry.

Instead there was no room.

Eli’s name occupied all of it.

---

Kade sat on the stone outside Meridian House because going inside meant seeing Room 814.

Mara sat beside him.

Sera paced ten meters away, still answering questions from response supervisors because she had become useful enough that nobody told her to stop.

One of the supervisors handed Sera a contact card.

“Response assessment office,” he said. “When this settles, call.”

Sera stared at it.

“This is not the time.”

“No,” the supervisor said. “It isn’t. Keep it anyway.”

She put it in her pocket without looking at it again.

Julian arrived after evening training had been canceled across half the island.

He stood in front of Kade for a while.

Then sat.

Rank One on the pavement.

No one cared.

Kade stared at the ground.

“If I hadn’t gone to the archive—”

Julian cut him off.

“No.”

Kade looked up.

Julian’s face was tired and angry.

“You do not get to make yourself the center of every bad thing that happens.”

“They targeted us.”

“Probably.”

“Eli was there because of me.”

“Eli was there because Eli made decisions.”

Kade almost snapped at him.

Julian continued before he could.

“You can be responsible for your choices without claiming ownership of everyone else’s.”

“You sound like my mother.”

“My father would say the opposite.”

Kade looked at him.

Julian’s jaw tightened.

“He thinks leadership means every failure in your orbit belongs to you.”

“And you do not?”

“I used to.”

Julian looked toward the dark engineering wing.

“It makes you feel important while destroying your ability to trust anyone else with a decision.”

Kade thought of Eli turning back toward Lab Six.

A decision Kade hated.

A decision Kade had not been allowed to own.

The sentence sounded like something Eli would have turned into a spreadsheet column.

That made it worse.

Kade looked away.

Across the courtyard, Sera finally stopped moving.

She put both hands on the back of a chair and bowed her head.

Mara reached over and took Kade’s hand.

Not romantic.

Not careful enough to pretend it was accidental.

Kade let her.

He had spent months learning that needing people made life larger.

Nobody had explained the other side.

Larger things left larger holes.

His tablet buzzed.

A message from Elena.

**I heard. Call when you can. I love you.**

Kade stared at it.

For once, he did not answer with a joke.

He typed:

**I love you too. I can’t talk yet.**

Then he put the tablet down.

Inside Meridian House, someone rang the old bronze bell once.

Not a victory ring.

One low strike.

The sound moved through the courtyard and out toward the ocean.

Kade closed his eyes.

Somewhere in the engineering network, a maintenance channel called NULL-7 still existed because Eli had once needed somewhere to send packets nobody cared about.

Somewhere beneath the eastern wing, a local controller had been closed by hand.

Somewhere inside Asterion’s systems, someone had been watching status traffic before the blast.

All of those were facts.

None of them were large enough to fight the line on the casualty page.

Eli was missing.

Presumed dead.

And Kade had no system large enough to make that fact go somewhere else.

# Chapter 26 — Lab Six

The explosion happened six minutes after they left the archive.

That was long enough for Kade to begin believing they had escaped.

It was also long enough for Eli to complain about Taren’s routing choice.

“You understand,” Eli said as they crossed the eastern engineering wing, “that there were four shorter ways back to Meridian.”

Taren kept walking.

“Three.”

“Four.”

“Service stair three is locked after nineteen hundred.”

Eli looked at him.

“It is not.”

“It was yesterday.”

“How do you know?”

Taren glanced over one shoulder.

“This feels like a bad time to criticize my knowledge of doors.”

Sera said, “It is always a good time now.”

Nobody laughed.

That was new.

Before the archive, Taren could turn almost any silence into something lighter.

Now every useful thing he knew came with a second question attached.

Where did you learn that?

Who told you?

Who else knows?

Kade hated the questions.

He hated more that they were fair.

The engineering wing was mostly empty. Midyear projects had ended two days earlier, leaving fabrication bays quiet and classroom glass dark. A cleaning drone hummed along one wall, paused to avoid them, then resumed its patient war against footprints.

Eli stopped beside Lab Five long enough to peer through the glass.

A half-assembled stabilization frame occupied the center table.

Four jointed braces lay around it in a shape that suggested a bridge had been taken apart and taught to crawl.

Colored load tape covered the braces. Blue at the feet. Yellow along the telescoping arms. Red around one hinge that had apparently earned Eli’s distrust.

Kade pointed through the glass.

“That yours?”

“Most of it.”

“Which part is not?”

“The table.”

“Comforting.”

Eli ignored him and woke the project display mounted outside the lab. A three-dimensional model unfolded above his tablet: two broken roadway sections, a gap between them, the portable frame opening underneath like a metal hand.

“Current emergency braces push against whatever is still standing,” Eli said. “Which means the damaged structure has to carry the rescue system too. Mine takes the load somewhere else.”

He rotated the model. Four anchor feet moved beyond the fracture line and drove into intact ground.

Mara leaned closer.

“You are moving the boundary of the supported structure.”

Eli looked offended.

“I am using normal engineering words.”

“You are moving it physically.”

“Exactly. Like a civilized person.”

Kade studied the simulated bridge.

The frame did not repair anything. It made a temporary path through damage by deciding which parts of the damage no longer had to carry themselves.

That seemed important.

He kept the thought to himself.

Sera touched one of the load points on the display.

“How heavy?”

“Thirty-eight kilograms.”

“Too heavy.”

“For you?”

“For a responder already carrying armor, medical gear, line equipment, and somebody who cannot walk.”

Eli zoomed in on the folding assembly.

“Two-person carry.”

“Then it costs two people before it saves one.”

“Municipal crews work in teams.”

“Municipal crews arrive short.”

The answer came without heat. Sera was not arguing against the machine. She was putting it in smoke and rain and a stairwell with the lights out.

Eli’s fingers stopped over the display.

Then he opened his notes.

“What weight makes it useful?”

“Twenty-five. Twenty if you want anyone to love you.”

“Twenty is insulting to physics.”

“Physics does not have to carry it up six flights.”

Eli added a line under the red hinge.

**RESPONSE REVIEW: MASS / ONE-PERSON DRAG CONFIGURATION**

He looked at Sera.

“Anything else?”

She considered the model more seriously.

“The feet assume level ground.”

“They articulate.”

“They articulate on a clean lab floor. Put half of one in mud and the other half on loose concrete.”

Another note.

“Glove controls?”

“Physical toggles.”

“Good. Reflective markings?”

“The material emits its own status field.”

“Does a firefighter know that color means load limit or does a firefighter die learning?”

Eli added a third note.

Mara selected the red hinge.

“What happens when this fails?”

“If.”

“When.”

Eli gave her a wounded look, then ran the fault model.

One anchor folded.

The simulated roadway dropped twelve centimeters and stopped on the remaining braces. Warning bands turned red across the frame. Two escape arrows appeared on the deck.

“It loses capacity but not all at once,” Eli said. “People get warning and a route off.”

Kade pointed at the sagging model.

“Your success demonstration includes the bridge breaking.”

“Everything breaks. Good engineering decides how much warning it gives you and where the damage goes.”

Sera studied the escape arrows.

“Make those visible through smoke.”

Eli added a fourth note.

The broken model remained above his hand: damaged, limited, still holding long enough for someone else to move.

Then he reset it.

The project had become a conversation between a future engineer and a future responder. Neither needed Kade to translate.

He stood beside them and liked being unnecessary.

The display chimed.

**FELLOWSHIP DEMONSTRATION SLOT: FRIDAY, 09:20**

Eli dismissed it too quickly.

Kade looked at him.

“You are thinking about that now?”

“I am trying to think about literally anything else.”

“That seems healthy.”

“My mother says compartmentalization is either a useful skill or a future therapy bill.”

“Your mother sounds practical.”

“She raised me.”

“Evidence against.”

Eli almost smiled.

Mara caught the motion.

“You have a demonstration as well as a panel.”

“A small one.”

“With a structure capable of falling on the committee?” Kade asked.

“Only if they stand where I mark the floor.”

“That answer will reassure them.”

Eli touched the locked Lab Five door.

“If I get the fellowship, they let me use Systems Lab fabrication without a faculty sponsor.”

Sera nodded toward the frame.

“Build the next version. Response will break it properly.”

Eli brightened.

“That is the nicest thing you have ever offered me.”

“Not a compliment.”

“It is in engineering.”

Eli looked through the glass at the unfinished frame.

For a few seconds, the archive disappeared from his face.

There was only the problem he had chosen.

Kade stood beside them.

A month ago, he had thought of Eli mostly as his roommate.

Then as his friend.

Now Eli had a fellowship interview, a civilian hardware plan, opinions about municipal procurement, and a future Kade had nothing to do with.

Kade liked that.

It made friendship feel less like ownership.

Behind them, Taren remained near the junction instead of joining the discussion.

Before the archive, he would have found a way into it. He would have claimed diplomatic immunity for physics or offered to steal Eli a lighter alloy from House Helix.

Now he kept his hands visible and waited for someone else to decide when the group moved.

Eli shut down the project display.

“You knew the fellowship schedule changed,” he said without looking at Taren.

Taren’s expression tightened.

“It was on the engineering calendar.”

“It was changed this morning.”

“Then I read it this morning.”

“Through which system?”

Taren did not answer immediately.

Sera said, “Eli.”

“I am asking.”

“I know.”

Eli turned then.

Fear and anger made him look younger, not older.

“Was the Directorate reading my project traffic too?”

Taren shook his head.

“I never gave them your models.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“I don’t know what they could access.”

Eli laughed once.

“You always know.”

Taren accepted that without defending himself.

“Not this.”

The cleaning drone reached the edge of their group and stopped, unable to route around six stationary people.

Its warning light blinked politely.

Nobody moved.

Finally Taren stepped aside.

The drone passed through the space he had occupied.

It was a ridiculous thing to make room for.

Kade understood the choice anyway.

“Central hall,” Sera said.

They started walking.

Taren stopped at the next junction.

“East service bridge or central hall?”

Sera answered first.

“Central. More cameras.”

Taren flinched.

Sera saw it.

“So they can see us,” she said. “Not so you can.”

“I know what you meant.”

“Good.”

They took central hall.

The corridor widened near Lab Six.

Kade knew the room mostly because Eli complained about it.

Lab Six contained thermal-transfer hardware, coolant systems, and an old experimental Conduit test bed that had been grandfathered through three generations of safety standards because replacing it would apparently cost enough money to make administration discover tradition.

Eli called it the museum that could explode.

The museum occupied two levels behind one continuous wall of reinforced glass.

The upper teaching floor held worktables, tool cages, and a control booth overlooking the central rig. Yellow stairs descended around both sides of the rig to a lower chamber full of coolant lines and field-shielded transfer pipes. A narrow service spine ran along the eastern wall. The west side had a pressure door wide enough for equipment carts.

Three people were still inside.

Two engineering students in gray lab coats were securing a suspended transfer ring over the upper floor. Beneath them, a faculty technician crouched beside an open panel in the lower chamber.

One of the students raised a hand when Eli passed.

Eli raised his tablet in reply.

“They helped me machine the red hinge,” he said.

Kade looked at the articulated frame still visible behind them.

“The one you don’t trust?”

“That is why I trust them.”

The teaching floor lights dimmed to evening mode.

Inside, the two students began lowering the transfer ring.

Normally there were warning lights over the access doors.

Tonight one of them blinked amber.

Then green.

Then amber again.

Eli stopped walking.

Not slowed.

Stopped.

Kade took three more steps before noticing.

“What?”

Eli stared at the maintenance panel beside the door.

The small diagnostic light repeated the sequence.

Amber.

Green.

Amber.

“That is wrong,” Eli said.

Sera looked back.

“How wrong?”

“Not broken wrong.”

Eli stepped closer.

“Watched wrong.”

Kade’s skin tightened.

Taren went pale.

Mara asked, “What does that mean?”

Eli opened his tablet and pulled up a network map.

“I have been seeing phantom maintenance pings in Lab Six for weeks.”

Kade stared at him.

“Weeks?”

“I thought it was an orphaned monitor.”

“English.”

“Old service software asking whether Lab Six still exists.”

“Does that happen?”

“At Asterion? Constantly. Half the island is new systems pretending the old systems are dead.”

His fingers moved faster.

“But these pings are not requests.”

Mara leaned closer.

“What are they?”

“Status acknowledgments.”

Sera said, “To whom?”

“That is the interesting question.”

Kade looked at him.

“You said weeks.”

Eli’s mouth tightened.

“I noticed them while I was tracing the hidden surveillance layer before the archive.”

“You did not mention Lab Six.”

“I had eighteen unexplained nodes.”

“That sounds like something you mention.”

“I was going to.”

“When?”

Eli looked at him.

“After we stopped committing archive crimes.”

Fair.

Taren said, “Directorate overlay.”

Eli turned.

“You know?”

“Not specifically.”

“Excellent distinction.”

Taren stepped toward the panel but stopped before touching it.

“They use passive status mirrors. If they compromise a facility, they do not need full control. They need to know what state it is in.”

“Why?” Mara asked.

“To act when the system is vulnerable.”

Nobody spoke.

The diagnostic light flashed red.

Eli’s face changed.

His joking expression disappeared so completely that Kade felt fear before he knew why.

“What?”

Eli enlarged the thermal network.

One branch had turned orange.

Then red.

“That valve is not supposed to be open.”

Sera moved beside him.

“What happens if it stays open?”

“Coolant pressure climbs in the lower chamber.”

“How long?”

Eli looked at the numbers.

“Not long.”

Taren said, “Move.”

Eli did not.

He opened a service channel.

Kade recognized the ugly interface.

It was the same kind of maintenance network Eli used for unauthorized drone tests.

A list of channels appeared.

Most had names.

One said only:

**NULL-7**

Kade pointed.

“What is that?”

“Dead channel.”

“Meaning?”

“Supposed to be unmonitored. Legacy maintenance route. I use it when I need test packets that do not clutter live logs.”

Sera stared.

“You have an unmonitored campus network.”

“No.”

“You just said—”

“I said supposed to be.”

Eli sent one diagnostic packet.

It vanished.

No acknowledgment.

He sent another through the standard network.

A reply returned instantly.

Then a second reply.

From somewhere else.

Eli went still.

“Someone is mirroring status.”

Taren’s face lost color.

“Directorate.”

“Could be.”

“Eli.”

“Fine. Very probably Directorate.”

One of the upper-floor students looked toward the access door.

The warning light above it had turned solid amber.

Eli hit the wall intercom.

“Lab Six, clear the floor.”

Static answered.

He pressed again.

“This is Navarro outside east access. Your lower coolant valve is open.”

The student nearest the window crossed to the internal panel.

Her voice came through broken and thin.

“Panel says valve closed.”

Eli turned his tablet so she could see the red branch on his map.

“The panel is lying. Get to the west pressure door.”

The two students moved immediately.

Below them, the faculty technician stood and looked up.

He could not hear the intercom from inside the shielded chamber.

The student at the window pointed down and then toward the stairs.

The technician checked his own panel instead.

The lower chamber lights changed from white to blue.

Eli swore.

Kade had never heard him use that word.

“What?”

“Automatic stabilization field. The system thinks pressure is already outside tolerance.”

Mara looked through the glass.

“Does the field keep them safe?”

“It keeps coolant properties stable.”

“That was not the question.”

“No.”

Sera hit the emergency alarm beside the door.

Nothing happened.

She hit it again.

“Local alarm is isolated.”

Taren moved toward the next corridor station.

Sera blocked him with one arm.

“Tell me before you touch a system.”

He stopped.

“The main building alarm is at the junction. Separate network.”

Sera held his gaze for half a second.

“Go.”

Taren ran.

Mara pulled the emergency plan from the public wall display. The lab diagram opened above it.

“West pressure door returns to central hall,” she said. “Lower service stair returns east.”

Eli shook his head.

“Not once the field engages. The stair becomes part of the chamber seal.”

Inside, the two students reached the west door.

It did not open.

One struck the release.

The other looked back toward Eli.

He pressed the intercom.

“Manual wheel. Right side of the frame.”

She found it and pulled.

The wheel did not move.

The lower pressure reading jumped.

Eli’s tablet flashed a ninety-second projection, revised it to seventy-six, then removed the estimate entirely.

“Leave the door,” he told them. “Go up to the control booth. It has an independent seal.”

The student at the intercom pointed toward the technician below.

“He won’t leave the controller.”

Eli looked through the glass at the man in the lower chamber.

“Then you cannot help him from there. Move.”

The answer cost him.

He gave it anyway.

The students ran for the booth.

The faculty technician finally looked up.

The students were running.

Then Eli moved into view outside the glass.

Eli pointed toward the lower service control.

The technician looked at the same place.

He shook his head.

Not accessible from his side.

Eli understood.

So did Kade.

Eli closed the tablet.

“Move.”

Nobody argued.

They ran.

The first blast was not loud.

It was pressure.

The corridor floor rose under Kade’s feet as if the building had inhaled.

He hit the wall shoulder-first.

Air disappeared from his lungs.

A second later the sound arrived.

Deep.

Metallic.

Wrong.

Alarms screamed through the eastern wing.

Emergency shutters dropped from the ceiling.

Sera rolled beneath the first.

Mara followed.

Kade slid through as the gap narrowed.

Taren came after him.

Eli did not.

Kade turned.

Eli stood on the other side of the descending pressure door, staring at his tablet.

“Eli!”

“The coolant chamber is venting!” Eli shouted.

“Then get through!”

“If it vents into the upper lab, it takes the whole level!”

The gap beneath the door was less than half a meter.

Eli looked at the service map.

Then at them.

Decision settled across Eli’s face.

“No.”

Eli’s eyes met his.

“Kade—”

“No. Get through the door.”

“I can isolate it from inside the service control.”

“Then let emergency crews do it.”

“The controller is compromised. Remote commands are being rejected.”

Sera dropped to one knee beside the gap.

“How long before upper vent?”

Eli checked.

“Maybe ninety seconds.”

“Can a response team reach the local control in ninety?”

He did not answer.

That was answer enough.

Kade flattened himself to the floor.

The opening had narrowed to thirty centimeters.

“Eli.”

His friend looked down at him.

Kade wanted to say something useful.

Do not be stupid.

Wait.

I need you.

All of them sounded like commands.

Eli’s life had just become larger than the part Kade occupied, with plans and people that did not ask Kade’s permission at all.

The worst possible moment to remember that.

Eli gave him a frightened smile.

“If I get the fellowship, tell them this counts as practical experience.”

“Eli.”

“I know.”

“Do not—”

The pressure door sealed the rest of the sentence.

Eli turned and ran toward Lab Six.

Kade hit the door with both palms.

“ELI!”

No answer.

Mara was already at the emergency panel.

“Manual override locked.”

Taren slammed his revoked access badge against the reader.

Nothing.

“I burned the credential,” he said.

Kade spun on him.

“Then unburn it.”

“I cannot.”

“Try harder.”

The wall shook again.

This time fire flashed behind the reinforced window.

Kade saw white.

Then smoke.

Then nothing.

He reached for the whole system before deciding to.

Door.

Wall.

Pressure.

Structure.

The cold line formed behind his eyes.

A route appeared in his mind.

Force from the chamber.

Seal frame.

Wall anchors.

He could change where the pressure resolved.

Maybe.

He had never done anything like it deliberately.

Sera grabbed his wrist.

“Kade.”

“I can open it.”

“You do not know that.”

“He is in there.”

“I know.”

“I can—”

“You are standing beside a damaged thermal lab full of unstable field hardware.”

Her voice cut through him.

“If you guess wrong, you make it worse.”

The cold line sharpened.

Kade looked at the window.

Smoke.

Pressure.

Eli somewhere beyond it.

Every part of him wanted power to mean not having to wait for someone else.

That was exactly why he could not use it.

He let the perception go.

Pain remained behind his eyes anyway.

Sera released his wrist.

“Good.”

Kade hated her.

For three seconds.

Because she was right.

Emergency teams arrived in fifty-four seconds.

Kade counted every one.

The corridor tried to become chaos before they got there.

Students spilled from two evening study rooms when the main alarm began. A teaching assistant opened a stairwell door directly into smoke and staggered back coughing. One emergency shutter had stopped halfway down, forcing everyone from the north corridor toward the sealed lab door.

Sera climbed onto a bench.

“Turn around. Central stairs only. Walk until the corner, then run if you need to.”

Nobody listened for the first two seconds.

She pulled the wall fire hood over the teaching assistant’s head, tightened the seal, and pointed at the nearest pair of students.

“Take him between you. Central stairs. Tell the next floor east wing is closed.”

The students obeyed.

After that, everyone did.

Mara stood at the wall plan with her tablet and crossed off routes as shutters reported closed.

“North junction clear,” she said. “Two rooms on this level still show occupancy.”

“Kade,” Sera said.

He looked at her.

“Room sweep. Stay this side of the blue floor line. Do not open anything warm.”

It was an order.

He was grateful for it.

Kade ran to the first study room.

Empty.

Chairs pushed back. Tablets abandoned. A cup rolling in a slow circle from the blast.

He checked beneath the tables anyway.

The second room held a first-year curled beside the instructor station with both hands over his ears.

Kade crouched outside the blue line painted across the doorway.

“Can you walk?”

The student nodded without moving.

“Useful in theory,” Kade said. “Try it physically.”

The student’s eyes found him.

He got one foot under himself.

Kade offered a hand, then stopped before crossing the line.

The metal frame around the doorway had turned faintly red.

Warm.

Do not open anything warm.

He took the emergency pole from the wall instead and extended it across the threshold.

The student grabbed it.

Kade pulled him forward until he could reach cloth instead of skin.

They made the corridor as a ceiling panel fell behind them.

The student stared at it.

Kade turned him toward central hall.

“Keep moving.”

When Kade returned, Mara had found one more occupancy signal in a storage alcove.

Taren stood at the main alarm station with both hands away from the controls.

“It is a heat sensor,” Mara said after checking the reading. “No person.”

Sera looked at Taren.

“What did you send?”

“Building alarm and hazard code. Nothing else.”

“Show her.”

He gave Mara the terminal log.

She checked every line before nodding.

Trust had become a thing assembled one verified action at a time.

Kade did not know whether that was enough to save a friendship.

It was enough to clear a corridor.

Responders in sealed armor flooded the corridor. Sera immediately began giving them the information she had: number of people, last known position, lab hazards, direction Eli had run, suspected compromised remote control.

She became someone else under emergency lights.

Not calmer.

More precise.

This was what she wanted to do with her life.

Kade understood that now.

A response officer shoved a portable map toward her.

“Which route?”

Sera pointed without hesitation.

“Service spine C. Do not enter from west stair. Pressure differential will trap your team.”

The officer looked at her identification.

“You Response Corps?”

“Candidate.”

He nodded once and followed her recommendation.

Another responder asked, “Local controller?”

Kade answered before Sera could.

“Navarro went for it.”

The responder looked at the sealed door.

“Student?”

“Yes.”

“Training?”

“Systems engineering.”

The responder’s face did not change.

“Then if he reached it, he may have bought us time.”

Kade held onto the sentence.

May have.

Bought time.

Not dead.

Not yet.

He stood uselessly against the wall.

That was almost unbearable.

A third responder pushed a casualty slate into his hands.

“You know the missing student?”

“Yes.”

“Fill what you can. Medical alerts, suit type, comm ID.”

The slate opened a form.

Kade knew Eli’s preferred breakfast, his worst music, and the exact expression he made when code compiled on the first attempt.

He did not know his blood type.

He stared at the empty field.

Mara reached around him and entered Eli’s student identifier.

“His medical record is linked.”

The form populated.

No medication conflicts.

No field implant.

Standard student lab coat, gray.

Kade selected **LAST KNOWN CONSCIOUS**.

Under distinguishing equipment, he entered:

**Black tablet. Cracked upper-left corner. Orange tool roll.**

He almost wrote *talks when frightened*.

The form did not have a box for that.

Mara stayed beside him.

Not touching.

Close.

Taren remained across the corridor, hands empty.

For one second Kade thought he had disappeared.

Taren had stepped away to speak to an emergency terminal.

Kade noticed himself noticing.

He hated Taren for making noticing necessary.

Taren returned.

“What?” Kade demanded.

“I sent Directorate credentials to Sen.”

“Your burned ones?”

“The backend token still has audit history. Maybe they can trace who touched Lab Six.”

“Maybe?”

“That is all I have.”

Mara held out her hand.

“Show me the token before Sen opens it.”

Taren gave her his tablet.

“It is signed. You can verify the signature without executing anything.”

“And if the signature calls home?”

“Use an isolated reader.”

Mara looked at Sera.

Sera took an evidence sleeve from the responder station and sealed the tablet inside it.

“We give it to incident command,” she said. “They decide how to route it.”

Taren nodded.

No complaint that he knew the system better.

No request that they trust him.

He crossed the corridor again and sat on the floor beneath the dead alarm light.

Kade had never seen him sit alone when other people were available.

He did not invite him closer.

He did not tell him to leave.

Both omissions required effort.

Kade wanted more.

Everyone around him kept having only what they had.

It was becoming a theme.

After seventeen minutes, the first rescue team emerged with two injured engineering students.

They were the students from the upper floor.

One walked between two responders, face hidden behind an oxygen mask. The other came out on a rigid litter with a clear field hood over her head and one hand clenched around nothing.

Kade pushed away from the wall.

The walking student pulled off his mask.

“Navarro?” he asked.

“Still inside,” a responder said.

The student shook his head hard.

“He got the booth seal closed. The intercom died, then the internal screen gave us directions.”

Kade gripped the casualty slate.

“Eli sent them?”

“Must have.” The student coughed into the mask. “He told us not to wait for the lower chamber.”

The response medic pushed the mask back into place.

“You can interview him later.”

Kade stepped aside.

The litter passed close enough for him to recognize the student who had turned the manual wheel.

Alive.

Eli had moved them somewhere survivable before he ran toward the controller.

The knowledge made hope worse.

Neither was Eli.

Thirty-three minutes.

A faculty technician.

His gray lab coat had been cut away and one arm was fixed against his chest. Coolant frost coated his boots in a blue-white skin that did not melt under the corridor lights.

He caught Eli’s name through the oxygen hood.

“Navarro reached east control.”

Kade moved beside the litter.

“Was he there?”

The technician’s eyes opened.

“On chamber status.”

“Was he alive?”

“He closed upper vent.”

“After that.”

The technician tried to speak again.

The medic put a hand on Kade’s shoulder and moved him back.

“After that, the cameras failed.”

Not an answer.

The only answer available.

Not Eli.

The first team did not go back in.

Their armor was scored white at the joints where the stabilization field had tried to hold coolant and metal in the same condition. A new team replaced their air cylinders and checked tether lines while incident command changed the map.

The east-control route turned red.

The service spine turned yellow.

The lower chamber remained black.

Kade listened to words he understood individually and could not make useful together.

Field persistence.

Thermal inversion.

Structural delamination.

Unreliable telemetry.

He wanted one word.

Alive.

Nobody had it.

At thirty-eight minutes, the response commander ordered the second team out.

Sera stepped toward her.

“They have four minutes left on air.”

“Their suits are losing seal integrity.”

“The controller is less than thirty meters past their position.”

“Through a chamber the instruments cannot read.”

Sera stopped.

The commander looked at her.

“Candidate, what is the first rescue priority?”

Sera’s jaw tightened.

“Do not create more casualties.”

“Then let them return.”

Sera looked through the smoked glass.

Every part of her wanted to argue.

She touched her comm.

“Team Two, confirm withdrawal route.”

The answer crackled back.

“Service spine C. Ninety seconds.”

Sera closed her eyes once.

“Route clear.”

Knowing when not to send people farther was also response work.

Kade hated that too.

Forty-one.

A responder came out alone.

His helmet was scorched.

Kade knew before the man spoke.

“Lower lab is compromised. Field-stabilized coolant flooded the chamber. Thermal sensors are unreliable.”

“Navarro?” Mara asked.

The responder looked at the list.

“Not located.”

Not located.

Kade held onto those words.

Not dead.

Not located.

“Local controller?” Sera asked.

“Closed.”

The responder looked at her.

“Someone manually isolated the upper vent before failure.”

Kade stopped breathing.

“Eli.”

“We do not know.”

“It was him.”

The responder did not argue.

“He may have prevented a much larger release.”

May have.

Again.

Kade had never hated uncertainty more.

The responder’s helmet turned toward the incident map.

“We cannot safely search the lower chamber until the field decays.”

“How long?” Kade asked.

“We don’t know.”

“Hours?”

“Possibly.”

“Days?”

The pause answered before the responder did.

“Possibly.”

Kade looked at the black square representing the chamber.

Somewhere inside it, a line had closed on a screen.

Upper vent isolated.

The visible proof of Eli’s last decision.

No visible proof of what came after.

---

The official casualty notice did not post for two hours.

That delay was its own kind of torture.

Meridian students filled the common room without anyone calling a meeting. People who barely knew Eli sat anyway. Nessa brought food nobody touched. Priya paced from window to door and back. Anton repaired the same training strap three times.

The House turned itself into a waiting room by instinct.

The long dining table became an information desk. Someone connected the engineering-wing incident feed to the wall display, then disabled comments after strangers began arguing about sabotage. First-years carried water upstairs to anyone who could not face the crowd. An upper-year wrote the response-center number on a board large enough that nobody had to ask for it twice.

No one claimed leadership.

Work simply found people.

Kade stood near the wall display.

The casualty page remained blank.

Eli’s bed was eight floors above them.

Kade could feel it like a location on a map.

At one point his phone rang.

Elena.

He declined it.

Then immediately texted:

**Alive. Eli missing. Cannot talk yet.**

She replied:

**I love you. I’m here.**

No demand.

No advice.

Kade put the phone away.

An administrator arrived with a communications officer and asked for Kade, Sera, Mara, and Taren.

Nobody asked for Julian.

He came anyway.

They used Meridian’s strategy room because the eastern interview rooms were inside the closure zone. The search map covered one wall. Four areas were marked green, two amber, and the lower chamber black.

The communications officer placed a draft notice on the table.

**LABORATORY INCIDENT — ONE STUDENT UNACCOUNTED FOR**

Kade read it.

“His name is Eli Navarro.”

“We cannot release a name until family notification.”

“Has anyone notified his family?”

The officer looked at the administrator.

“Student Services is confirming contact information.”

Eli’s mother knew how anxiety worked.

Eli had told them that less than three hours ago.

Now an office was confirming she existed.

Kade stood.

“Give me the number.”

“You cannot make an official casualty notification.”

“You have not established a casualty.”

“Exactly.”

The room went quiet.

The administrator’s voice softened.

“Mercer, if his mother hears from you before our liaison reaches her, what will you tell her?”

Kade opened his mouth.

Eli ran into the lab.

Eli saved three people.

Eli might be alive.

Eli might not.

Every sentence either concealed something or made uncertainty sound like hope.

He sat again.

“Tell her his name should not be the last thing your form discovers.”

The administrator nodded.

“I can do that.”

Sera pointed at the map.

“Why is the west teaching floor green if the pressure door never opened?”

The administrator glanced at the response notes.

“Two occupants recovered from the booth.”

“Then mark the route they used, not the room. People reading this need to know what was actually searched.”

The communications officer started to object.

The administrator changed the map.

Green collapsed into a narrow line from the booth to the service spine.

Most of Lab Six became black again.

Less comforting.

More honest.

Mara asked for the timestamp on the mirrored status packet. Taren asked whether the incident team had isolated the Directorate signature. Each question went into a separate evidence queue.

Kade had only one.

“When do you go back in?”

The administrator looked at the black chamber.

“When the response commander believes another person can enter without adding a name to the notice.”

Kade wanted to hate him.

The man looked too tired for it to work.

They returned to the common room.

The draft notice went live without Eli’s name.

Forty minutes later, it disappeared.

That was worse.

Mara sat beside him on the floor.

“You should drink something.”

“No.”

“That was not a question.”

“You have been spending too much time with Sera.”

“Probably.”

She handed him water.

Kade took it.

On the far side of the room, Taren stood alone.

Nobody had told him to leave.

Nobody invited him closer.

He looked more isolated than Kade had ever seen him.

For once, Taren did not try to fix the room with conversation.

The casualty page updated.

Every person in Meridian went still.

Five injured.

Two critical.

One missing, presumed dead.

**ELIAS NAVARRO**

Kade read the name until it stopped looking like language.

No body recovered.

The lab collapse had damaged the thermal chamber and flooded the lower level with coolant designed to remain stable under field stress. The same properties that prevented a larger explosion made normal recovery impossible until the system cooled.

Technical language.

Clean language.

Merciful language.

Presumed dead.

Someone behind Kade started crying.

He did not turn to see who.

The page contained one additional line.

**INCIDENT STATUS: CONTAINED. EXTERNAL ACCESS UNDER INVESTIGATION.**

External access.

The clue should have made Kade angry.

Instead there was no room.

Eli’s name occupied all of it.

The wall display opened an evidence link beneath the notice.

Kade touched it.

Mara caught his hand before the packet list expanded.

“Not here.”

“They were watching the lab.”

“We know.”

“The packet destination might still be live.”

“Which is why you do not open it on a House network.”

Taren crossed half the common room before stopping outside their circle.

“I can identify the routing format.”

Every head near them turned.

The silence was not grief now.

It had edges.

Taren kept his hands at his sides.

“Not the destination. Maybe the Directorate office that issued the mirror.”

Kade looked from Eli’s name to Taren.

Here was something to do.

A route.

A person to blame standing close enough to reach.

His body tried to make that into relief.

Mara still held his wrist.

Not restraining him.

Waiting.

Kade pulled his hand away from the display.

“Evidence team has it.”

Taren nodded.

“They may not know what they are reading.”

“Then tell them.”

“They may be Directorate.”

“Then tell Sen.”

Taren’s mouth tightened.

“Kade—”

“I cannot make Eli less dead by solving you tonight.”

The sentence struck harder than Kade intended.

Taren stepped back.

He did not say *presumed*.

He did not offer an apology large enough to make Kade comfort him.

“No,” he said. “You can’t.”

He returned to the far side of the room.

Mara released Kade’s wrist.

“You could have hit him,” she said.

“He might have preferred that.”

“Probably.”

“I might have preferred that.”

“Probably.”

Kade looked at the evidence link.

The archive had taught him that old records could rearrange a life. Lab Six had taught him that a current system could lie while people stood inside it.

He wanted to tear through every packet until the lie acquired a name.

That was not the same as wanting the truth.

Not tonight.

Tonight it was wanting escape in a shape that looked useful.

Across the room, Priya took the untouched food from the table and divided it into sealed containers. Anton stopped repairing the strap and began writing down the names of students who had gone upstairs alone. Nessa muted the incident feed but left Eli’s notice visible.

Nobody asked Kade what the House should do.

They did what they could without him.

The relief and pain arrived together.

Mara sat beside him again.

“Drink the water.”

He did.

The display remained unchanged.

Five injured.

Two critical.

One missing, presumed dead.

Contained.

Under investigation.

Every category attempted to put an edge around what had happened.

None of them could hold Eli.

---

Kade sat on the stone outside Meridian House because going inside meant seeing Room 814.

Mara sat beside him.

Sera paced ten meters away, still answering questions from response supervisors because she had become useful enough that nobody told her to stop.

One of the supervisors handed Sera a contact card.

“Response assessment office,” he said. “When this settles, call.”

Sera stared at it.

“This is not the time.”

“No,” the supervisor said. “It isn’t. Keep it anyway.”

She put it in her pocket without looking at it again.

Julian arrived after evening training had been canceled across half the island.

He stood in front of Kade for a while.

Then sat.

Rank One on the pavement.

No one cared.

Kade stared at the ground.

“If I hadn’t gone to the archive—”

Julian cut him off.

“No.”

Kade looked up.

Julian’s face was tired and angry.

“You do not get to make yourself the center of every bad thing that happens.”

“They targeted us.”

“Probably.”

“Eli was there because of me.”

“Eli was there because Eli made decisions.”

Kade almost snapped at him.

Julian continued before he could.

“You can be responsible for your choices without claiming ownership of everyone else’s.”

“You sound like my mother.”

“My father would say the opposite.”

Kade looked at him.

Julian’s jaw tightened.

“He thinks leadership means every failure in your orbit belongs to you.”

“And you do not?”

“I used to.”

Julian looked toward the dark engineering wing.

“It makes you feel important while destroying your ability to trust anyone else with a decision.”

Kade thought of Eli turning back toward Lab Six.

A decision Kade hated.

A decision Kade had not been allowed to own.

The sentence sounded like something Eli would have turned into a spreadsheet column.

That made it worse.

Kade looked away.

Across the courtyard, Sera finally stopped moving.

She put both hands on the back of a chair and bowed her head.

Mara reached over and took Kade’s hand.

Not romantic.

Not careful enough to pretend it was accidental.

Kade let her.

He had spent months learning that needing people made life larger.

Nobody had explained the other side.

Larger things left larger holes.

His tablet buzzed.

A message from Elena.

**I heard. Call when you can. I love you.**

Kade stared at it.

For once, he did not answer with a joke.

He typed:

**I love you too. I can’t talk yet.**

Then he put the tablet down.

Inside Meridian House, someone rang the old bronze bell once.

Not a victory ring.

One low strike.

The sound moved through the courtyard and out toward the ocean.

Kade closed his eyes.

Somewhere in the engineering network, a maintenance channel called NULL-7 still existed because Eli had once needed somewhere to send packets nobody cared about.

Somewhere beneath the eastern wing, a local controller had been closed by hand.

Somewhere inside Asterion’s systems, someone had been watching status traffic before the blast.

All of those were facts.

None of them were large enough to fight the line on the casualty page.

Eli was missing.

Presumed dead.

And Kade had no system large enough to make that fact go somewhere else.

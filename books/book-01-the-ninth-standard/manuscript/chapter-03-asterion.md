# Chapter 3 — Asterion

Kade had expected the transport to be impressive.

He had not expected it to refuse to have wings.

He stood beside his mother on a restricted pad at the municipal airfield, staring at a matte-gray aircraft shaped like someone had taken a normal jet, removed every part that made intuitive sense, and then dared gravity to complain.

Four recessed lift rings glowed faintly beneath the fuselage.

No propellers.

No visible engines.

No wings.

Darius, attending by video call from Kade’s phone, said, “That thing looks fake.”

Kade held the phone toward the aircraft.

“Helpful.”

“It looks like a lunchbox rich people would buy.”

Elena adjusted the strap on Kade’s duffel bag.

“Tell Darius goodbye.”

“I can hear you, Mrs. Mercer.”

“Then goodbye, Darius.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

Kade looked at the screen.

Darius sat on the steps outside Northline. Behind him, somebody was arguing with the vending machine.

Normal life had continued with offensive efficiency.

Darius said, “You got everything?”

“One bag.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

Kade looked down at the grocery-store duffel Elena had owned since before he was born. One zipper had been repaired with black thread. The Asterion travel instructions recommended two formal uniforms, athletic clothing, personal electronics, and “appropriate attire for institutional events.”

Kade owned exactly one shirt that might qualify as institutional.

It had a collar.

“Probably,” he said.

Darius nodded.

“Send pictures.”

“Of what?”

“Everything.”

“Your standards are low.”

“Have you seen Northline?”

Behind him, Ms. Alvarez stepped through the school doors carrying her half-empty coffee.

Darius noticed Kade looking and turned the phone.

Ms. Alvarez stopped.

For a second none of them said anything.

Then she raised her cup toward the camera.

“Try.”

Kade stared at her.

“That is becoming threatening.”

“Good.”

The connection distorted her smile into blocks for half a second.

Then she disappeared back inside.

Darius turned the phone toward himself again.

“She’s going to put that on your grave.”

“Optimistic departure talk.”

A uniformed flight officer approached.

“Mercer?”

Kade raised a hand.

“Boarding now.”

He looked back at his phone.

Darius lifted two fingers in salute.

“Go be weird somewhere expensive.”

Kade ended the call before he could answer.

His mother hugged him.

Not the quick shoulder squeeze she used when leaving for work.

A real one.

Kade held still for half a second, then hugged her back.

“You call me,” she said into his shoulder.

“I know.”

“Not text.”

“People still call?”

She pulled away and looked at him.

“Kade.”

“I’ll call.”

“Every day for the first week.”

“That seems excessive.”

“Then twice a day.”

“Once is excellent.”

She smiled.

Then the smile faded.

“If you want to come home after the evaluation, you come home.”

He looked toward the aircraft.

The lift rings brightened.

“I know.”

“No matter what they tell you.”

“I know.”

“And if they try to turn mandatory evaluation into mandatory enrollment—”

“I call you.”

“You call me.”

He picked up the duffel.

Elena looked at it.

“You could have taken the suitcase.”

“It has wheels.”

“That is usually considered useful.”

“It looks like luggage.”

She stared at him.

“Remarkable observation.”

Kade grinned.

Then she reached out and straightened the repaired cuff of his hoodie.

Her hand stopped there.

“Your father would have—”

The sentence died.

Kade looked at her.

Elena’s eyes moved away first.

“Would have what?”

“Made fun of the bag.”

“That feels plausible.”

She smiled, but not quite enough.

Kade stored the unfinished sentence with the other things he did not understand.

Then he boarded.

The cabin held twelve seats and only five passengers.

Three wore Asterion jackets.

One wore a suit and never looked up from a display.

The fifth was Kade.

He took the seat farthest from everyone else.

An academy student across the aisle glanced at his duffel, then at him.

Kade knew that look.

He had seen versions of it in school offices, waiting rooms, scholarship interviews he had not wanted to attend, and the lobby of a dentist whose magazines cost more than his shoes.

Assessment.

Classification.

Placement.

He put the bag beneath his seat.

The flight officer closed the cabin door.

“Null-lift departure. Remain secured until the seat indicator clears.”

Kade fastened the harness.

There was no engine whine.

No accelerating roll.

The aircraft rose.

Just rose.

Kade’s stomach remained on the runway for a moment.

The city dropped beneath them.

His hand closed around the armrest.

The lift rings produced almost no vibration. The ground simply fell away as though the agreement between him and gravity had been renegotiated without his participation.

He forgot to look unimpressed.

The student across the aisle noticed.

“First null-lift?”

Kade turned.

The student was perhaps nineteen, broad-shouldered, with a silver Asterion crest stitched onto the collar of a dark jacket.

“First one that didn’t involve a building elevator.”

The student smiled.

“Wait until descent.”

“Encouraging.”

“Name’s Rian Okafor.”

“Kade.”

Rian’s eyes sharpened.

“Mercer?”

There it was.

Kade sat back.

“Unfortunately.”

Rian glanced toward the other students, then lowered his voice.

“You’re Zero.”

Kade had heard the nickname online twelve times before breakfast.

Apparently somebody had clipped footage of Station Seven before the district took the videos down.

Zero Boy had lasted eleven minutes.

Then somebody improved it to Zero Mercer.

Then simply Zero.

Kade preferred the last one, mostly because it sounded like a breakfast cereal for people who hated joy.

“That’s not actually my score.”

“So they say.”

“What do you say?”

Rian shrugged.

“I say people don’t shut down a national system for a bad test.”

Kade looked back out the window.

“Neither do I.”

Rian nodded once.

That should have been the end of it.

Then he said, “You know people are betting on you.”

Kade turned slowly.

“What?”

“Campus pool.”

“I’m not a horse.”

“Most horses have more public data.”

“How comforting.”

Rian pulled up a student forum on his wrist display.

Lines of anonymous predictions scrolled past.

**ZERO LASTS < 72 HOURS**

**MERCER BASELINES TOP 100**

**NO OUTPUT / ADMIN ERROR**

**CLASSIFIED STANDARD**

**HE IS LITERALLY JUST SOME KID**

Kade pointed at the last one.

“Smart money.”

Rian laughed.

“Odds are terrible.”

“Of course.”

Kade should have found the whole thing ridiculous.

He did.

He also found himself reading the predictions twice.

That bothered him.

The flight leveled.

For the first forty minutes, the view was mountains and desert turning toward coast.

Kade had flown commercially once, at age eight, to visit Elena’s sister. He remembered crying because the wing flexed in turbulence and then denying it for nine years.

This was different.

The aircraft moved with almost no vibration. Every few minutes the floor tilted beneath them while the horizon remained level, compensation fields taking force Kade’s body expected to feel.

He watched the cabin instead of the window.

A paper cup sat on the tray table across from him.

The liquid inside barely moved through a turn.

“How?” he asked.

Rian followed his gaze.

“Local inertial redirection.”

“That’s a phrase, not an explanation.”

“You want the tensor version?”

“No.”

“Then it’s an explanation.”

Kade watched the cup again.

Force happened.

The cup did not experience all of it.

Somewhere, something decided where the transfer resolved.

A pressure touched the base of his skull.

Faint.

Gone before he could decide whether it was real.

He looked away.

Rian said, “You okay?”

“Fine.”

The answer came automatically.

Kade frowned at himself.

“Actually, weird headache.”

Rian nodded toward the call panel. “Tell the crew.”

“It’s gone.”

“Still tell them if it comes back.”

No curiosity.

No Zero questions.

Just practical advice.

Kade liked him more.

A meal tray emerged from the seat console twenty minutes later.

Kade stared at it.

“Did I pay for this?”

Rian looked over.

“You’re on an academy transport.”

“That didn’t answer the question.”

“It’s included.”

“In what?”

“Being abducted educationally, apparently.”

Kade laughed.

The tray contained a sandwich, fruit, a protein bar, and a bottle of water bearing the Asterion crest.

The protein bar probably cost more than lunch at Northline.

Kade ate it anyway.

He had spent enough years resenting expensive things to know resentment did not improve hunger.

Then the ocean appeared.

Kade had seen the Pacific twice.

Both times from public beaches crowded with umbrellas and coolers and children determined to cover every available surface with sand.

This was different.

From two thousand meters up, the ocean looked less like water and more like a fact.

Huge.

Flat only from far away.

Impossible to argue with.

The aircraft turned west.

Rian leaned across the aisle.

“Two minutes.”

Kade pretended not to care.

He lasted nineteen seconds.

Then the clouds opened.

Asterion Island appeared below them.

Kade stopped breathing.

It did not look like a school.

It looked like someone had built a city specifically to win an argument.

The artificial island formed a rough crescent nearly six kilometers across. White seawalls cut clean lines through dark water. Towers rose from the northern arc, glass and pale stone catching afternoon light. Green spaces divided academic buildings into broad terraces. Training arenas sat along the southern half like enormous silver coins embedded in the ground.

At the center stood one structure larger than all the others.

The Spire.

Kade knew it from broadcasts.

Every championship feed showed it.

Every recruitment commercial used it.

A tapered tower of dark glass and structural lattice climbed above the island, its upper levels widening instead of narrowing, as if gravity had been consulted and overruled.

The aircraft descended.

Kade saw bridges between buildings.

Running tracks.

Open courtyards.

Students moving in groups.

Thousands of them.

Above the western shoreline, two figures rose into the air.

No aircraft.

No visible support.

Two students.

One accelerated upward in a clean arc while the other redirected sideways, boots skimming past the face of a tower before both vanished behind it.

Kade’s hand flattened against the window.

Rian laughed softly.

“Yeah.”

Kade pulled his hand away.

“I’ve seen Resonants.”

“On screens.”

“Same physics.”

“Not from ten meters.”

Kade had no answer for that.

The aircraft banked hard.

His stomach shifted.

Then the floor seemed to rotate beneath him without the cabin rotating at all.

He grabbed the seat.

Rian grinned.

“Descent.”

Kade glared at him.

“I dislike you.”

“You’ll survive.”

The aircraft settled onto a pad with almost no impact.

The doors opened.

Warm ocean air rushed in.

Salt.

Jet fuel from a neighboring conventional pad.

Flowers from landscaping Kade could not identify.

He stepped onto Asterion Island.

For three seconds, nobody knew who he was.

It was wonderful.

Then a girl thirty meters away looked at him, looked at her wrist display, and looked back.

Her friend turned too.

Then another student.

Kade picked up his duffel.

Wonderful over.

A staff member met him at the edge of the pad.

“Mr. Mercer. Welcome to Asterion.”

The man wore a gray academy uniform with blue trim. He had close-cropped hair and the expression of someone who considered schedules a moral obligation.

“I’m Enrollment Officer Cole. Your evaluation packet.”

He handed Kade a thin slate.

The top line displayed:

**MERCER, KADE — PROVISIONAL FIRST-YEAR**

Kade stared at it.

“I’m not enrolled.”

“Provisional.”

“That word is carrying a lot of weight.”

Cole did not smile.

“Your baseline evaluation begins at zero seven hundred tomorrow.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Yes.”

“My federal evaluation was supposed to last three days.”

“It may.”

“That sounded suspiciously efficient.”

Cole started walking.

Kade followed.

The landing complex opened into a broad plaza lined with trees unfamiliar to him. Water moved through narrow channels cut into black stone. Students crossed in every direction, most wearing some variation of academy uniform.

Nobody seemed lost.

Kade immediately distrusted them.

A transparent panel rose from the plaza floor.

At first he thought it was an advertisement.

Then the names began moving.

Thousands of them.

A ranking board.

**ASTERION COMBAT LADDER**

Kade slowed.

Names scrolled beside numbers, academic years, Houses, and Standard classifications.

At the top:

**1 — CROSS, JULIAN — YEAR 1**

Kade blinked.

“Freshman?”

Cole glanced at the board.

“Yes.”

“He’s first in the whole academy?”

“First among incoming first-years. Your view is filtered.”

“Oh.”

Kade hated that the answer disappointed him.

Cole touched his slate.

The display shifted.

**INCOMING FIRST-YEAR COHORT — 10,482 STUDENTS**

“Ten thousand?” Kade said.

“Ten thousand four hundred eighty-two.”

“How many applicants?”

“Enough.”

“That’s not a number.”

“It is the number relevant to your day.”

Kade looked at him.

Enrollment Officer Cole might be the first person he had met who could weaponize bureaucracy as a personality.

Kade respected the commitment.

They crossed the plaza.

Everywhere he looked, something competed for attention.

A group of students in training uniforms sprinted uphill while weighted sleds floated behind them on controlled fields.

Two others stood inside a transparent practice enclosure redirecting streams of water between suspended rings.

On a lawn, a professor lectured around a three-dimensional projection of an orbital platform while students sat in the grass.

A maintenance crew replaced a cracked walkway panel while one student hovered the entire slab five centimeters off the ground and another argued with an engineer about load distribution.

Nobody seemed to think any of it was remarkable.

Kade thought all of it was remarkable.

He kept his face neutral.

Cole said, “You may stare.”

Kade looked at him.

“I wasn’t.”

“You nearly walked into a bench.”

Kade looked down.

A bench stood ten centimeters from his shin.

He changed direction.

“Poor placement.”

Cole’s mouth moved.

Almost a smile.

They passed an open courtyard where twenty students stood in a circle around a hovering steel ring.

An instructor called numbers.

Each number sent a different student stepping forward to alter the ring’s movement—slow it, redirect it, divide its rotation, hold stored energy, release it.

Kade stopped.

The ring accelerated toward a wall.

Three students acted at once.

The ring curved.

Stopped.

Dropped neatly onto a stand.

“What class?” Kade asked.

“Introductory coupled control.”

“Introductory.”

“Yes.”

“At my old school introductory physics involved a cart.”

“Carts remain available.”

“I’m relieved.”

They continued.

At the far side of the courtyard, Cole stopped beneath a blue sign marked **ARRIVAL ORIENTATION**.

Six wall displays showed the same island from different angles. Families crowded around five of them while academy staff explained residence access, meal schedules, medical routes, weather shelters, and a list of rules long enough to make Kade suspect Asterion had criminalized being new.

The sixth display was empty.

Cole tapped his slate against it.

A map spread across the wall.

The crescent became less impressive when rendered as colored blocks and evacuation arrows.

“You are here,” Cole said.

A blue point pulsed on the central academic level, east of the Spire.

“I had gathered that generally.”

“Show me your assigned residence.”

Kade looked at him.

“Is this a test?”

“It is orientation.”

“Those are usually tests with worse scoring.”

Cole waited.

Kade studied the map.

The island had three levels of movement. A lower transit ring curved beneath the open courtyards. The academic level crossed between buildings in enclosed bridges. A service level threaded through the foundation in gray lines that disappeared whenever he tried to select them.

The Spire stood at the center of all three.

Training arenas occupied the broad southern terraces. Academic buildings stepped north and west. Residences climbed the eastern shoulder and the northern arc, close enough to the sea that half the rooms probably existed to appear in brochures.

Kade touched the largest residence block near the eastern landing complex.

The map rejected it with a soft tone.

**HOUSE MERIDIAN — ASSIGNED RESIDENTS ONLY**

“Not there,” Cole said.

“The machine had opinions first.”

He found a smaller building beyond Meridian, marked **ARRIVAL RESIDENCE C**. The route from their position crossed Central Bridge, cut through Meridian courtyard, and ended above the landing pads.

“There.”

“Now the nearest hardened shelter.”

Kade glanced at the sky beyond the courtyard.

No clouds.

“Is weather scheduled?”

“The Pacific does not consult enrollment.”

Kade switched the map to emergency view.

The polished academy colors vanished.

Load capacities appeared over every bridge. Flood doors divided the lower ring. The Spire became a cluster of red-controlled access zones. A shelter icon blinked beneath the northern residential towers, on the opposite side of the central complex.

Kade traced the obvious route from Arrival C across Central Bridge.

The map marked it green.

“And if Central is closed?” Cole asked.

Kade selected the lower ring.

The route ran south beneath Meridian before turning north at the Spire.

“Lower transit.”

“If the lower ring is closed?”

“Why would both be closed?”

“Fire. Flooding. Field instability. A crowd moving the wrong direction.”

“Comforting place.”

“Safe places know what can go wrong.”

That sounded like something Elena would say at work.

Kade enlarged the academic level. A narrower bridge curved west around the Spire through the older classroom blocks. It joined a library underpass before reaching the northern towers.

“West academic bridge,” he said.

“Longer and partially exposed to sea weather.”

“But separate from Central and lower transit.”

Cole nodded.

Kade touched the route.

The map asked him to identify two assembly points and one route he could travel without academy access.

He found the landing complex immediately.

Cole said nothing.

“It is a route I can travel,” Kade said.

“Yes.”

No lecture about commitment.

No claim that arrival had erased departure.

Kade marked it.

The second route took longer. His provisional lanyard barred the direct path through Meridian, so he had to use an exterior stair beside the dining hall.

When the display accepted both answers, his lanyard vibrated once.

**EMERGENCY ORIENTATION COMPLETE**

At the next panel, a father was trying to answer for his daughter.

“North shelter,” he said, reaching past her.

The staff member covered the screen with one hand.

“She has to show me.”

The girl stepped in front of him. She could not have been more than fourteen. A rigid brace ran from her left wrist beneath the sleeve of her academy jacket.

Her father lowered his hand.

The girl found a route neither he nor Kade had used, through a lift sized for emergency stretchers.

The display accepted it.

She smiled at the map instead of at anyone around her.

Cole moved on.

Kade followed more slowly.

At Northline, fire-drill maps curled behind scratched plastic and one stairwell door stuck during winter. Here, the evacuation system knew exactly which doors Kade could open and required him to prove he could leave without somebody more powerful choosing for him.

The place had just become slightly harder to dismiss.

A group passed wearing green-and-gold House jackets. One student looked at Kade, whispered something, and all four turned.

Kade raised one hand.

“Hello, subtle people.”

They faced forward immediately.

Cole did not react.

“How long until that stops?” Kade asked.

“Attention?”

“Yes.”

“Depends on what you do tomorrow.”

That answer stayed with him.

They reached a residence complex overlooking the eastern water.

The lobby alone was bigger than Northline.

Kade stopped at the entrance.

Students moved through carrying hard-shell luggage, garment cases, athletic bags, instrument cases, equipment trunks.

Parents stood in clusters.

Some wore business suits.

Some wore military uniforms.

One family had three assistants moving boxes while a girl in academy blue complained about which room faced the ocean.

Kade looked down at his grocery-store duffel.

For the first time all day, it embarrassed him.

He hated that immediately.

Cole noticed.

Of course he did.

“Baggage services can provide additional storage.”

“I don’t need it.”

“I did not say you did.”

Kade tightened his grip on the strap.

They entered.

At the intake desk, another staff member scanned Kade’s wrist band and handed him a meal card, temporary housing key, campus map, emergency contact strip, and a blue cloth lanyard marked PROVISIONAL.

Kade held it up.

“Do I have to wear this?”

“During evaluation.”

“It’s very tasteful.”

“It prevents you entering restricted training zones.”

“Less tasteful.”

A student passing behind him glanced at the lanyard.

Then at Kade’s face.

Recognition flashed.

Kade clipped it on anyway.

His room was on the eighth floor.

Temporary housing, Cole explained. House assignments would follow baseline testing.

The elevator had a transparent wall.

Kade watched the ocean drop beneath him as they rose.

At floor five, two students entered arguing in Mandarin. One had a training bruise across her jaw. The other carried a violin case.

At floor seven, a boy stepped in wearing pajama pants and an academy jacket covered in competition pins.

Nobody looked like a recruitment commercial.

That helped.

The room had two beds, two desks, floor-to-ceiling glass, and an ocean view that would have added several hundred dollars to the rent anywhere Kade had ever lived.

The second bed was empty.

Cole handed him a key card.

“Dinner begins at eighteen hundred. Orientation materials are on your slate. Medical intake at zero six fifteen. Baseline testing at zero seven hundred.”

Kade set his bag on the bed.

“What happens if I fail?”

Cole paused at the door.

“Fail what?”

“The evaluation.”

“There is no passing score.”

“That is exactly what schools say shortly before inventing one.”

Cole studied him.

Then said, “Mr. Mercer, Asterion is not interested in proving you belong here.”

Kade pressed his tongue against the back of his teeth and kept the first answer there.

Cole continued.

“We are interested in determining what you can do.”

The door closed behind him.

Kade stood alone.

Outside the window, the island stretched beneath late-afternoon light.

Students crossed bridges far below.

A training arena opened its roof like a mechanical flower.

Beyond it, the Pacific reached to the horizon.

Kade unpacked.

It took four minutes.

Three shirts.

Two pairs of jeans.

Training clothes Elena bought the night before because he owned nothing designed for “academy physical evaluation.”

One collared shirt.

A photograph of him and Elena he had not packed.

He stared at it.

His mother had slipped it into the side pocket.

Behind the photograph was a folded note.

**Call me. Eat food. Ask questions. You are allowed to leave. You are also allowed to stay. — Mom**

Kade read the last sentence twice.

Then put the note in his desk drawer where nobody would accidentally see it and make the situation emotional.

---

At dinner he went alone.

The temporary-residence dining hall had eight food stations, two hundred seats, and more different languages being spoken than Kade had heard in one place in his life.

He took a tray.

The student behind him said, “Zero?”

Kade did not turn.

“Probably not on the menu.”

A girl laughed.

“No. You.”

He looked back.

She was short, curly-haired, wearing a provisional lanyard the same color as his.

“Unfortunately.”

“Imani Bell. Medical review.”

“Is that why you’re provisional?”

“Heart surgery at fourteen. They want to see whether the implant interferes with field coupling.”

She said it casually.

Kade had no prepared response.

“Does it?”

“Tomorrow I find out.”

She picked up an apple.

“What are you?”

“Apparently a routing code.”

Imani nodded as if that made sense.

“Good luck with that.”

“You too.”

The line ended at a pair of scanners built into the counter.

Imani tapped her card against the left one. A green ring moved around the edge.

**RESIDENT MEAL — INCLUDED**

Kade used the right scanner.

It flashed red.

**FACULTY ACCOUNT NOT FOUND**

“I did warn you,” Imani said.

“You warned me after I had food.”

“That is when the lesson becomes memorable.”

Kade used the other scanner.

The green ring appeared. Beneath it, an itemized list filled the screen: dinner access, residential nutrition allocation, evaluation lodging. Every line ended in zero.

That was somehow worse than a price.

“What account is this charging?” he asked the dining employee.

The woman looked at his lanyard.

“Provisional services.”

“Who pays provisional services?”

“The academy.”

“The academy gets money from somewhere.”

The line behind him stopped.

Imani leaned around his shoulder.

“He is having an ethical crisis about the apple.”

“It’s a very polished apple.”

The employee rested both hands on the counter.

“Evaluation meals, lodging, and medical intake are covered whether you enroll or not. Nothing you put on that tray creates a family charge.”

Kade looked at the list again.

“And after enrollment?”

“Dining is part of residence fees. Need-based aid covers the same meal plan.”

“Same stations?”

“Same food.”

No cheaper line hidden behind the expensive one.

Kade had attended enough scholarship presentations to distrust equality that existed on a slide. He looked past the register. Students in tailored jackets and students wearing repaired sleeves carried identical trays away from identical scanners.

That did not prove the claim.

It made the claim testable.

The employee nodded toward the waiting line.

“You can audit the academy after dinner.”

“Scheduled for nineteen hundred,” Kade said.

He picked up his tray.

Imani fell into step beside him.

“You could have asked after you ate.”

“Then I might owe them an apple.”

“You are going to be exhausting here.”

“Provisional.”

Imani started toward the tables, then stopped.

“Do you want to sit with us?”

Kade looked past her.

Three students with blue provisional lanyards occupied the end of a long table. None of them were looking at him with enough concentration to suggest an ambush.

“Who is us?”

“People the academy has not classified yet.”

“That sounds like a support group.”

“It became one when the meal cards stopped working.”

Kade looked down at his card.

“They stop working?”

“Only if you use the faculty side of the scanner. We learned through sacrifice.”

Kade followed her.

The table held more food than conversation. A tall boy in a faded red sweater was building a wall out of unopened yogurt cups. Across from him, a girl with a close-shaved head compared two orientation schedules on her slate. The third student, who wore an academy training shirt beneath an obviously new jacket, was peeling every piece of white membrane from an orange.

Imani set down her tray.

“Zero, this is Nora, Tomas, and Yuki. Everyone, Zero says he is a routing code.”

“Kade,” Kade said.

“That too.”

The boy in the red sweater—Tomas, apparently—moved his yogurt wall to make room.

“We agreed not to ask what anyone can do until after baseline,” Nora said.

“We did not agree,” Yuki said. “You announced it while I was reading the housing rules.”

“Did you object?”

“I was reading the housing rules.”

“Motion carried.”

Kade sat.

“You are all here for evaluation?”

Tomas put another yogurt cup on the wall.

“Late Conduit expression. First measurable event at eighteen.”

Nora rotated her slate so the overlapping schedules became one.

“Transfer certification. My regional program scores control in a seven-band system. Asterion insists it cannot convert the results without observing me.”

“Can it?” Kade asked.

“Of course. It does not trust the conversion.”

Yuki separated the last white strand from her orange.

“My field works normally until I cross a moving reference frame. Trains, lifts, aircraft. Then it changes direction.”

Kade thought of the paper cup on the transport. Force happening somewhere other than where a body expected it.

“That seems inconvenient.”

“The academy called it operationally significant.”

“That is inconvenient in expensive language.”

Yuki pointed one orange segment at him.

“Exactly.”

Imani unwrapped a fork.

“And my heart has aftermarket parts.”

“You make it sound modified for racing,” Tomas said.

“The surgeon promised at least twelve percent more speed.”

Nobody performed sympathy.

Imani seemed to appreciate that.

Kade opened his water.

“What happens if the implant interferes?”

“They map the interference. If it is stable, I train with limits. If it is unstable, they adjust the shielding and test again.”

“And if that fails?”

Imani looked down at the academy crest on her bottle.

“Then I go home with better data than I arrived with.”

Her voice stayed even.

The fork in her hand bent by a fraction.

She put it down and chose another.

“I would still hate it,” she added.

“Reasonable,” Kade said.

Nora nodded.

“Classification is not the same as permission to care.”

“Did orientation give you that sentence?” Tomas asked.

“No. Orientation gave me three passwords and a weather alarm.”

The wall display above the food stations shifted from dinner hours to next-day schedules.

**PROVISIONAL BASELINE GROUPS**

Names appeared beneath testing locations.

The table went quiet.

Nora found hers first.

“Six forty. Academic control.”

“Seven ten,” Tomas said. “South arena four.”

Yuki pointed to her name.

“Null-lift lab. They have a sense of humor.”

Imani’s name appeared under **MEDICAL COUPLING REVIEW — CLINIC TWO**.

Then Kade’s name filled an entire line by itself.

**MERCER, KADE — CONTROLLED BASELINE — SUBLEVEL ACCESS**

Conversation thinned at the nearest tables.

Tomas looked at the display.

Then at Kade.

“No questions before baseline,” he said.

“That was Nora’s rule,” Yuki replied.

“It has improved.”

A student from the next table leaned across the gap. His white lanyard marked him as a fully enrolled first-year.

“Is it true you made a federal test read zero zero zero?”

Kade picked up his sandwich.

“The sandwich refuses to comment.”

The student smiled as if Kade had performed on request.

“Can you actually suppress other fields?”

“I don’t know.”

“Try it on this.”

He held out a spoon. It hovered above his palm, turning slowly.

The surrounding tables paid attention now.

Kade stared at the spoon.

Part of him wanted to reach for it.

Not because he believed anything would happen.

Because failure would be an answer and an answer would belong to him.

The pressure at the base of his skull had happened during a turn. The sphere at Station Seven had moved after he stopped treating it as the object that mattered.

Neither event made this safe.

“No,” he said.

The student’s smile thinned.

“Why not?”

“Because I’m holding dinner, not a federal testing waiver.”

Tomas lifted one yogurt cup from his wall.

“Also, if you lose control of the spoon, it could damage the structural yogurt.”

Yuki added, “We have not calculated load tolerance.”

Imani took a bite of her apple.

“Operationally significant.”

The enrolled student looked around the table, found nobody willing to make Kade more entertaining, and let the spoon drop into his hand.

“Tomorrow, then.”

“Apparently,” Kade said.

The student returned to his friends.

Conversation resumed around them in pieces.

Kade exhaled.

“Thank you for defending the yogurt.”

“It has no one else,” Tomas said.

Nora returned to her schedule.

“You would have tried if we were not here.”

It was not a question.

Kade looked at her.

“You’ve known me for nine minutes.”

“Eight. And you spent six seconds deciding whether a dining utensil counted as useful evidence.”

“It might have.”

“That is why she is right,” Imani said.

Kade took a bite of his sandwich so he would not have to answer.

The bread was warm.

The cheese was real.

The academy had placed him on a private testing line and announced it to a dining hall. It had also put him at a table where four strangers could refuse to help turn him into a demonstration.

He had no category for that.

Yet.

They stayed through dessert.

Tomas’s yogurt wall survived two impacts from a redirected bread roll before a dining employee asked him to put food back on the tray. Nora found an error in the orientation schedule and submitted a correction before anyone could stop her. Yuki mapped the quickest route to the null-lift lab, rejected it because it used three moving walkways, and chose stairs twice as long.

Imani traded her second apple for Kade’s protein bar.

“They gave you one on the transport,” she said. “You’re already sick of them.”

“How do you know?”

“You looked at it like it had disappointed your family.”

Kade gave her the bar.

For the first time it occurred to him that provisional did not mean unwanted.

It meant unresolved.

And unresolved was not a private defect. It was a table full of people comparing routes into tomorrow.

At nineteen forty-five, the others left to locate clinics, training rooms, and one elevator Yuki intended to avoid. Kade carried his water to the end of the table near the window.

He watched students eat, argue, study, flirt, trade training clips, complain about instructors, and steal food from one another’s trays.

A school.

An impossible school, but still a school.

A student at the next table groaned over a chemistry problem.

Someone else threw a bread roll at him.

The bread roll curved in midair, stopped, and returned to the thrower.

Nobody looked up.

Kade laughed into his water.

At 20:05 he called Elena.

She answered on the second ring.

“Well?” she said.

He looked around the room.

Looked at the ocean.

Looked at the ranking board visible across the courtyard.

He had prepared several answers during the flight.

Ridiculous.

Pretentious.

Too expensive.

Probably a cult.

Instead he said, “Mom?”

“Yes?”

“This place is completely unfair.”

There was a pause.

Then she laughed.

Kade smiled.

“What does that mean?”

“The cafeteria has actual fruit.”

“A scandal.”

“Someone redirected a bread roll.”

“Dangerous technology.”

“And I almost walked into a bench.”

“That one sounds like you.”

Kade leaned back.

He told her about Rian.

The island.

The student flyers.

The provisional girl with a heart implant.

He did not tell her how badly the duffel had embarrassed him for five seconds.

He did not tell her about the pressure behind his eyes during the aircraft turn.

Not yet.

Then Elena said, “You sound happy.”

Kade stopped.

“I sound tired.”

“You sound happy and tired.”

“Medical diagnosis?”

“Maternal observation.”

Kade looked through the window.

A student launched from a lower terrace and crossed the gap between buildings in one impossible leap.

He watched until the student disappeared.

“Maybe,” he said.

The word surprised both of them.

Elena did not ruin it by commenting.

“Call tomorrow,” she said.

“I will.”

“After testing.”

“Yes.”

“And Kade?”

“What?”

“You don’t have to decide tonight whether you belong there.”

His eyes moved to the ranking board across the courtyard.

Ten thousand four hundred eighty-two first-years.

Every one of them had gotten here by some route.

His was simply stranger.

“Good,” he said. “Because I have no data.”

“Exactly.”

They ended the call.

Kade stood at the window another ten minutes.

He did not tell his mother the second thing he was thinking.

He wanted to stay.

Not forever.

Not even definitely.

But long enough to find out what this place would ask him next.

# Chapter 13 — Cross Examination

*Thirty-two. Mom says you called her to ask if you could ask a doctor if you could ask the number one kid on the island to knock you down. I said that's three permissions for one bruise. She said that's exactly right, and she's having it framed.*

Julian Cross watched the upper-years lose from the fourth row of Arena Two, and he sat in the same seat every time.

Kade knew this because he had spent two evenings finding it out. Not asking anyone. Sitting high in the stands with the stragglers who came for the free bouts, watching where rank one went when rank one was not being watched. The upper-year ladder ran its open challenges at seven, after the first-year floors went dark, and the stands held maybe sixty people. Julian came in after the bell for the first bout, sat fourth row center with nothing in his hands, and left before the last one ended. He never looked at the board. He never looked at the crowd. He watched whoever was losing.

On the third evening Kade went down the steps and sat beside him.

Julian did not turn his head. On the floor a fifth-year with a Redirection band and shoulders like a doorframe was being walked backward by somebody half his width, and the fifth-year did not know it yet. The crowd was watching the smaller one. Julian was watching the big one's feet.

"You're restricted," Julian said.

"Not from sitting. The Director said I could watch anything I liked."

"She would." The fifth-year threw a hand that arrived where his opponent had been. "He's going to lose to the wall in about six seconds. Watch his back foot, not the other man."

Kade watched the back foot. It stepped, and stepped, and stepped again, each step a little shorter than the last, and on the fourth there was no more floor behind it and the fifth-year discovered the wall with his shoulder blades. The buzzer went. The crowd made the noise crowds made when the person they came to see had done the thing they came to see.

"He was dead at the second step," Kade said.

"He was dead when he decided the wall was far away and stopped checking." Julian finally looked at him. Up close he had a face that gave the impression of having already finished the conversation and being polite enough to attend it anyway. "You didn't come down here to learn about walls."

"Teach me."

"No."

The bout after was two Amplification students who hit each other very hard for eleven seconds. Julian looked at the ceiling for most of it.

"I have a clinic on Field Three every other week," he said, when it was over. "The school makes me hold it. Forty people come to stand near the number. That's a queue, not teaching, and I don't do the other kind. Here's what I do. I fight people who make me better. There are not many of them. The ones above me won't say what they see when they beat me, because it's worth too much to them, and the ones below me can't get close enough to see anything at all."

"So where does that leave me?"

"Below me. A long way." He said it the way a person reads a distance off a map. "But I've watched every second of you that's been recorded on this island, and I can't build you. Everybody has a curve. Yours looks like somebody keeps changing the question. Whatever that is, you're going to see something in me that a normal fighter doesn't, and you have no reason on earth to keep it to yourself."

"That's the offer? I get thrown and you get told?"

"That's the fee. I'll put you on a mat where nobody's counting, as often as you can get up, and you'll find the thing I'm bad at and say it to my face." The next pair was walking out below them. Julian watched the one with the lower band. "Nobody's hit it in two years. It's stopped being a weakness and started being a style. I'd like it back while it's still small enough to fix."

Kade sat with that. It sounded like a man describing a leak he could hear and could not find.

"I'm not allowed on a floor."

"Then don't use one. You've got a doctor who signs things." Julian stood. Below them, the lower band had just stepped onto the mat, and he was already moving toward the aisle. "Bring me what she'll sign. Six tomorrow, if you can manage it."

"You've thought about this."

"Since the roof." He paused one step down. "I said a number to you up there. I don't say numbers I'm not planning to check."

He was gone before the bout started. Kade watched the rest of it alone and found he was watching the loser's feet.

---

He called Elena first, from the covered way outside the arena, because she had said she heard things first. She listened, said *ask her*, said *then call me with the part you left out*, and went back to a shift. Then he went to the clinic.

Park listened with her arms folded and her face doing nothing.

"You want me to sign you into a fight."

"Into being thrown. He doesn't hit."

"Everyone hits." She let the silence run, then unfolded her arms and went to the schedule board behind her desk, where the clinic's rooms were laid out in colored blocks. Most of the blocks were somebody's knee. "Rehab Two. Fields are locked at the door, because half the people who use that room have Conduits switched off for their own protection and the other half are learning to walk. Position and impact record to my desk. Not to the ladder, not to a House, not to a band. The stop word is wired. You say it, the room says it after you, and my door unlocks." She wrote a block in and turned. "One more condition. He signs the card."

"The card's medical."

"Yes. If he wants to throw a patient of mine, he reads what he's throwing. The card, in his hand, with his name under Hale's." She held out her palm, and he gave it to her, and she added a line under the fourth trigger and left the space beside it empty. "Six tomorrow, he said?"

"He said if I could manage it."

"You will manage to be here at five forty-five so I can see your eyes first." She handed the card back. "For the record, I have never signed anyone into Rehab Two to be knocked down on purpose. Please do not make me interesting."

---

Rehab Two was ten meters by six of ribbed gray mat with tile at both ends, and it had been built for people who had lost something and were learning to move without it.

A handrail ran the length of one long wall at hip height. The wall opposite was mirror, floor to ceiling, so that patients could watch their own gait and argue with it. A harness trolley hung parked at the far end of a ceiling rail, its straps looped neatly, for people whose legs could not yet be trusted to catch them. There was a folded wheelchair by the door and a rack of foam wedges and a wall clock whose second hand made a small dry sound at every step. It smelled of rubber and the clinic's soap. It was the least dangerous room Kade had ever been in and he had never been more certain of being hurt in one.

The door had read his band and shown him the Conduit mark grayed out under a lock. Off. He had been switched off before on this island, and it had felt like an accusation. This time it felt like taking off a coat somebody else had put on him.

Julian was already on the mat, reading the card.

He read it standing, the way a person reads a document they intend to sign rather than skim, and when he got to the fourth trigger he looked up. "This one. What does it look like from the outside?"

"Park says I go quiet and my eyes stop tracking. She's seen it twice. I've never seen it."

"Then I'll be the one who has." He took the stylus off the clip and wrote under the fourth trigger, one clean line, and handed the card back. *Read and accepted. J. Cross.* "Mat is the room. Tile is out. Rail's a wall. Glass is a wall. You say your word and I stop where I am, even if I'm in the air."

"Park says you stop *after* the room says it."

"Park has never seen me stop." He stepped back to the center of the mat and did nothing in particular with his hands. "Don't count."

Kade had a plan, and it was built out of Jonah Pike.

Two weeks of footage had taught him to see the moment before a strike, when a fighter asked the floor for permission. Pike had loaded his lead leg a quarter of a second before the leg became dangerous, every time, and Kade had learned to move on the ask instead of the answer. Julian was taller than Pike and slower to look at. Kade meant to give him nothing to load against and go the instant Julian settled onto a foot.

He waited for the ask.

Julian walked. That was all it appeared to be. He moved across the mat the way a person crosses a kitchen, with no more weight on one foot than the other, and every step took a little of the room with it. Kade found himself nearer the rail than he had chosen to be. He shifted. Julian was already where the shift was going. And when Kade finally went, because standing still had begun to feel like a decision he was making in public, he went at a man who had been half a step to the left of his own eyes for some time. A hand found his sleeve. A heel found the place his recovery step had wanted. The mat took him along the shoulder blades without a sound.

"Up," Julian said.

Three times, the same shape, in under a minute. There was no ask. Julian never put enough on one foot for the foot to have an opinion. He borrowed Kade's weight, tilted it a few degrees, and returned it to a place Kade had not arranged to receive it, and by the time Kade understood that he had been carrying a key for a door this man did not have, he was looking at the ceiling rail for the third time and the harness straps were swaying slightly from the air he had moved.

The other thing he had carried in was under that. He had not said it to Park, or to Elena, or to himself in words: some part of him had walked into this locked room to find out whether the cold line would come anyway. Whether the thing that had cracked a floor could be made to notice a man. It did not come. It had nothing to come for. Nothing loaded. Nothing resolved. There was no transfer in what Julian did that a boundary could be drawn around, only a body being lent and given back, and the rare thing in Kade lay wherever it lived and was not consulted. Two floors and a wall had made him the strangest object on the island. On this mat he was a first-year with slow feet, and the mat did not know his name.

So he used the room.

He gave ground toward the mirror, because a wall of glass was a way of seeing behind himself, and for a moment it worked. He watched Julian's back in the glass while Julian's front approached him, saw the line Julian was taking to the rail side, and moved to close it. Julian was not there. Kade was thrown from the side he had been looking at and landed facing the mirror, in which a first-year lay on a gray mat with his mouth open.

"The glass is half a second late and backwards," Julian said, from above him. "You knew that. You've stood in front of one."

"I was watching you."

"You were watching a picture of me. I was over here." He did not offer a hand. "Rail."

Kade tried the rail. He worked Julian toward it, meaning to use the bar as the fixed point he did not have in his own feet, a thing to pivot around that would not move when he pushed. Julian let himself be worked. Then Julian's hand was on the rail first, and it turned out that a fixed point belonged to whoever reached it, and Kade's pivot was somebody else's.

Seven. He stopped trying to be clever and tried to be nothing. Hands loose, weight center, commit to no line at all, let the man come. Julian came, without hurry, and the stillness turned out to have a clock in it too, only a slower one. Eight. Nine.

On the tenth, something happened.

He could not have said what he did. He went in on the mirror side, low, and Julian's answer was where he expected, and for once he was already through it. He had center. He could feel Julian's weight reach for a line that was no longer there and have to come back for it, and the second hand on the wall made its dry sound twice while rank one was being made to think. It was working. It was the first thing all morning that had worked.

So he changed it.

He did not decide to. His body abandoned the working line for a better one that was not better, and Julian, who had been about to have a problem, had a first-year instead. Ten.

Kade lay there longer than he needed to.

"That one," Julian said. He had not moved from where the finish had put him. "You had me thinking. Two whole seconds. Then you left."

"I saw a better line."

"There wasn't one. I was in the only place you could put me." He crouched, which he had not done all morning, so that his face was level with Kade's. "I've been wrong about you all morning. I thought you improvised because you couldn't plan. You can plan. You planned that one fine. You improvise because a plan that's working is a thing somebody could take from you, and you'd rather leave it than have it taken."

Kade got up. It took a while.

"I'm not here for that."

"You're here for whatever's on the mat." Julian straightened. "Once more. Then we stop, because tired people learn the wrong thing and remember it forever."

The eleventh was the fastest of the morning. Kade went at him with nothing behind it but temper, honest and hard and straight, and Julian took it the way a coat hook takes a coat.

"Four seconds," Julian said, and then, because Kade was looking at him, "I'm allowed to count. You're not."

Kade stayed on his back and looked at the clock. The second hand went on doing the only thing it knew.

"Well?" Julian said. "What am I bad at?"

"I don't know."

"You've had eleven looks."

"I've had eleven looks at the ceiling." He sat up. The last two fingers of his right hand were doing their small separate thing, the way they did when he had been upright a while, and he saw Julian see it and saw him check it against the card in his head and file it as not yet. "I was busy."

"You were busy losing eleven different ways. I lost the same way eleven times: I didn't. Which of us knows more this morning?"

It was not a question with a kind answer, so Kade did not give it one. He found the water Park had left by the door and drank, and Julian took the other bottle, and for a minute the room was two people breathing and a clock.

"Ask it," Julian said.

"Why here?" It was not what Julian had meant him to ask. "You've got Field Three. You've got a name that gets a floor unlocked by asking. Why a room with a wheelchair in it?"

Julian took a long drink before he answered.

"Because every second of me on a public floor is reviewed. Not by the school. By someone who pays to review it, every week, and asks me why I gave up center at forty-one seconds against a fourth-year who was never going to beat me." He capped the bottle. "In here there's no footage. There's Park's desk, and Park doesn't answer to anyone who calls my House."

"That sounds like family."

"It sounds like exactly what it is." Nothing moved in his face. "Don't build on it. I'm telling you so that you know why the door's locked. Now ask the other one."

"Why me. You said the fee. You didn't say why you think I can pay it."

"Because you got center on the tenth and threw it away, and nobody has got center on me in this building since the spring." He picked up the card from where he had set it on the rail and handed it back. "I don't need you to beat me. I need you to make me answer a question I haven't been asked. You nearly did. Tomorrow you'll be tired. Take the days you need. Thursday. Six."

Kade put the card in his jacket. His legs had opinions about the walk to the door.

"You didn't ask about the floor," he said. "Everyone asks about the floor."

"I know what the floor was. It's on every band on the island." Julian was already walking the mat's edge, heel to toe along the line where rubber met tile, as if checking that the room was still the size he had left it. "It's a fact about the island. It isn't a fact about your feet. I'm only interested in the feet."

Eli wanted the data and could not have it, and he took the refusal like a man denied dessert.

"Park's desk. Only Park's desk. I understand." He did not sound as if he understood. He was on his bed with the tablet on his knees and the model open, and Kade was on his own with his boots off and a cold pack on a hip that had found the rail. "Then we do it the old way. Narrative reconstruction. Highly unreliable. Fall one."

"He walked at me. I went. He wasn't there."

"Not him. You. What was the plan?"

"To see what he did."

Eli typed something. "Fall two."

"See what he did."

"Three."

"I'd worked out he doesn't load. So I was going to see what he did about that."

Eli stopped typing and looked at him over the tablet, and it was a look Kade had seen him give a sensor that returned the same value three times. "Fall four."

"The mirror. Use the mirror to watch his back."

"Good. That's a plan. It's a bad plan, because a mirror is an instrument and you know what you think of instruments, but it's a plan. Five."

They went through all eleven. When they got to ten, Kade told it properly, the low entry on the glass side and the two seconds of center and the leaving, and Eli listened without typing and then set the tablet face down on the blanket.

"Here's what I've got," he said. "Eleven entries on Cross. Where he stepped, what he borrowed, what he did with the rail. I could nearly build him from this." He picked the tablet back up and turned it so Kade could see the other column. It had eleven rows. Nine of them said *see.* "And I've got that on you."

"That's not fair. I was in it."

"That's my point." Eli tapped the column. "If I'd been in that room with you, standing behind you, holding something, when would I have known what you were doing?"

Kade opened his mouth and found nothing waiting.

"Right," Eli said. "Never. You'd have been alone in there with me." He said it lightly, the way he said everything, and then he did not follow it with a joke, which was how Kade knew he meant it. "I'm eight thousand nine hundred and eleven and I've got a model. You're the first thing in thirty-one years and you've got *see.* I'm not saying it to be cruel. I'm saying it because the model can't help you until you give it something to hold."

"He said something like that."

"He's number one. I'm not required to enjoy agreeing with him." Eli went back to typing. "Roster meeting is next Sunday. Qualifiers post the week after. Sera wants everybody there whether Park's cleared them or not, because in her words a clipboard has never been medically restricted."

"I know."

"You know because you read the House board, or because you were told?"

"Because Sera told me on the stairs and did not stop walking."

"That's how you know it's real," Eli said, and typed *see* into the tenth row, and then deleted it and typed *center, two seconds, abandoned,* and looked at that for a while as if it were the most interesting thing on the screen.

*Thirty-four. Mom says you lost eleven times in one morning and she has never heard you sound so good. I said maybe you hit your head. She said she asked. Not once on the head. She counted the falls off a doctor's sheet and said the number to me like a proud person.*

Meridian's laundry was in the basement under the Commons, four machines and two dryers and a long folding table scarred by a decade of belt buckles, and at nine on a weeknight it belonged to Sera because she had decided it did.

She was on top of the dryer that worked, boots off, with Kade's duffel across her knees and a curved needle in her teeth.

"I didn't give you that."

"You left it on the stairs with a training bag in it that smelled like a locked room." She took the needle out. "Handle was going. I said it would. Sit."

He sat on the folding table. His thigh objected and he let it. The machine with his clothes in it was on its second cycle, and the smell of the clinic's soap came out of his own shirt when he moved.

Sera sewed. She was using waxed thread and going through the old stitching rather than around it, doubling the seam where the handle met the body.

"You went and asked him," she said.

"I asked Park. Then I asked him."

"You asked Mom first. I know. She told Darius, Darius told the whole planet." She pulled a stitch tight. "I'm not complaining. It's the first door you've opened with three people standing in it. I'm noticing."

Taren came down the stairs with a laundry basket that had, as far as Kade could tell, one towel in it.

"Don't stop for me." He set the basket on a machine he did not open. "How are the mornings?"

"Loud."

"Six o'clock in Rehab Two, behind Park's front desk, with the fields locked. That's a great deal of trust from a woman who wouldn't clear you to walk to breakfast last week." He said it with real admiration, and Kade heard the admiration and, under it, the room number.

"I didn't say Rehab Two."

"Didn't you?" Taren considered it. "Then it's on the physio booking board. I had a shoulder last spring, I still read it out of habit. A block marked *Cross / Mercer* at six is not exactly a cipher."

Sera did not look up from the handle. "The physio board's inside the clinic."

"So's the waiting room, and I've spent enough of my life in it." He smiled at both of them. "You're doing the thing where you look at me like I've said something."

"You have," Sera said, and let it go, and Kade did not, quite. He put it in the file behind the roommate and the Sol thing and the Kepler feed and Pike's note an hour before the wall, and the file was getting a spine, and a block on a board was a perfectly good explanation, and he wrote that down beside it too.

Mara came down last, with a mesh bag of precisely folded things she intended to make more precise, and put them in the machine next to Kade's without a word, and set it going, and only then turned around.

"Eleven," she said.

"Everybody knows the number."

"Everybody knows the number because Cross told his House the room was booked for a fortnight and Eli told ours. The number was arithmetic." She leaned on the machine. "What did you see?"

"Him. All of him. Eli's got eleven rows."

"Not what he did. You went in owing him something. Have you got it?"

"No." It was easier to say to Mara than it had been to Julian, because Mara did not want anything from the answer but the answer. "I looked for it in his body. Where his weight is. What he loads. He doesn't load. There's nothing in his body to find."

"Then it isn't in his body." She said it the way she said a footwork correction, as though he had crossed his feet. "You spent eleven falls looking at what he does. Try what he's never had to do."

The dryer clicked over. Sera bit the thread, knotted it, and turned the duffel over to check the handle from the underside.

"What's he never had to do?" Kade said.

"That's your fee, not mine." Mara pushed off the machine. "Bay One, usual hour. I have my own work."

Taren watched her go up the stairs and then looked at the basket he had brought down and appeared to remember it. "I should wash that."

"You should," Sera said.

He did not. He went up the stairs after Mara, and Sera and Kade listened to his footsteps cross the Commons above them.

"Here." She tossed him the duffel. The handle sat flat and doubled and looked like a thing that would outlast the bag. "Reinforce it before it goes. It's not a prophecy. It's thread."

"You said that on the roof."

"I'm saying it about the whole of you." She got down off the dryer and put her boots on. "Roster meeting Sunday. Come. You can hold a clipboard with a red bar on your name; the clipboard doesn't care."

She left him with the machines. He sat on the table with the duffel across his own knees and thought about a man who never planted a foot.

What had Julian never had to do?

Everybody on that arena floor committed. Kade had watched it for two evenings from the fourth row. The fifth-year committed to the wall being far away. Pike had committed a lead leg every time he struck. People promised things with their bodies, and Julian read the promises, and arranged for them to be broken, and had never once had to make one of his own. Not because he was hiding it. Because nobody had held a line long enough to make him answer it instead of read it.

And the thing that Kade had never done, the thing Eli had eleven rows of, was hold a line after it started to work.

He looked at the duffel handle. Doubled. Through the old stitching, not around it.

The machine buzzed. He took his clothes out warm and folded them badly, and went up the stairs already saying the plan to himself, because if he was going to hand it to Julian on Thursday morning he had better find out whether it survived being said.

*Thirty-seven. Mom says you've started telling people what you're going to do before you do it. I said that's how you lose at everything. She said you've lost eleven times already and sound fine. I said that's not an argument. She said she's a nurse, everything's an argument.*

Julian was late.

He was late by four minutes, which Kade knew because the clock in Rehab Two told everyone everything, and when he came in he had his band in his hand instead of on his wrist and he was looking at it the way a person looks at a bill they have already paid. He put it face down on the rail.

"Sorry."

"You're never late."

"I've been asked about Saturday." He walked onto the mat and stood at center and did not do anything with his hands for a moment. "Public bout. I finished it in thirty-one seconds and spent four of them not at center. Someone wants to know about the four."

"Faculty?"

"No." He looked at Kade then, and the tightening Kade had seen once on a roof went through him and was put away so quickly that if he had not been learning what people did before they moved he would have missed it. "I'm telling you so that when I'm short with you this morning you know what it is. It isn't the fee. Don't go easy on me because of it. If you go easy on me I'll know, and I'll have got up early for nothing."

Kade thought of a woman in a parked car with his chart already open on her phone before he called, and of whoever was on the other end of that band, asking about four seconds. The top of the ladder came with family the same way the bottom did.

"I'm not going to go easy," he said. "I'm going to tell you my plan."

Julian's attention arrived all at once, the way it had in the fourth row when the fifth-year's back foot ran out of floor.

"Say that again."

"Every fall. Before I move, I tell you what I'm going to do. Out loud. Then I do it, and I don't leave it until you've made me pay for it."

"That's the worst idea I've heard on this island."

"It's the only one I've had that you haven't." Kade took the center of the mat, since Julian had stepped off it to listen. "You've never had to answer a plan. Only read one. Everyone you fight hides theirs, and you find it anyway, and you break it before they know they've made it. I'm going to hand you mine. Then you can't read it. You have to do something about it."

"You'll lose."

"I lost eleven times not telling you."

Julian looked at him for the length of two dry sounds from the wall.

"All right," he said. "Pay."

"Glass side. Low. Under your right arm. I take center and I keep it."

He went. Julian's right arm was not where it had been and the underside of it was already an answer; Kade was turned, courteously, and set down. One. He got up.

"Rail side. High, to bring your guard up. Then under."

Julian's guard came up before he had reached it, so plainly it was a dare. Kade went under anyway, and Julian was under first, and the mat was where it always was. Two.

Three was the same shape as one and lasted a second longer. Julian said nothing between them. He was not enjoying it. He was working, with his mouth closed and his eyes on Kade's hips, and Kade realized somewhere in the third fall that this was what the fourth row looked like from the floor.

"Glass side, low, center. And I keep it."

He got center. He felt Julian's weight go looking for the line he would leave by, the way it had on the tenth fall, and felt his own body start to give it to him, a better line that was not better, and he heard himself say *keep it* inside his head in Mara's voice and did not go. One beat. Julian's reach came back empty.

Then Kade went anyway, a beat later, because a beat was all he had in him, and Julian was ready by then. Four.

"You left it," Julian said. First words in four falls.

"I held it."

"For one second. Then you left. I felt you decide to stay and I felt you decide to go, and the second one is the one I hit." He walked back to center. "Again. Same plan. Hold it until I hurt you for it, not until you get bored."

Five, he held it two beats, and lost on the third. Six, three beats, and Julian took him at the rail, and the second hand had gone a quarter of the way around the clock, which was longer than any fall of the first morning. Kade's shirt was through. His hip had rediscovered the rail. He got up more slowly each time and each time Julian waited without offering the hand, because the hand had not been earned yet and both of them knew what it would mean when it was.

Seven he changed the plan on purpose, to see, and told Julian he was changing it, and Julian answered the new one as easily as the old and put him down and said, "Don't. You're proving something you've already proved."

Eight was long. It was long enough that Kade stopped hearing the clock. He had center, glass side, low, and he did not leave it, and Julian could not read a promise that was being kept in plain sight, because there was nothing to read. It was simply there. Julian had to move his own feet to deal with it. He moved them well. He moved them the way he did everything, without weight, and got Kade turned toward the rail on borrowed force, and finished it, and the finish took two full seconds longer than any finish of either morning.

Kade lay on the mat and breathed and looked at the ceiling rail with the harness parked on it, for people whose legs could not yet be trusted, and thought: nine.

"Once more," Julian said. He was breathing too. It was the first time Kade had heard it.

Kade stood. The two fingers on his right hand were doing their thing and had been for a while. Headache at one, maybe a shade over. The light in the room was the light in the room. He checked all of it against the card in his head because Park had made him, and it was all still weather.

"Glass side. Low. Center. I'm not leaving. If you go to the rail I follow you to the rail, and I still don't leave."

"That's not a plan. That's a threat."

"It's both."

He went, and got center, and kept it.

Julian read him. Kade watched him do it. He watched rank one look for the promise that was about to break, and not find it, and look again, and not find it, and the second hand made its dry sound five times while a man who had spent two years never being asked a question was asked one. Then Julian did the thing Kade had come into this room four days ago braced to see and had stopped believing existed.

He settled onto a foot.

It was small. It was a quarter of a second, the left heel taking weight it had not taken all morning, because there was no borrowed line to travel and Julian needed his own force for once. And Kade, who had spent two weeks learning to see that exact quarter-second in Jonah Pike's lead leg, saw it. The ask. Plain as a hand going up in a classroom.

He went on it.

He was too slow. Seeing a thing and arriving in time to use it were two different skills and he had only ever practiced the first. Julian felt him come, recovered his own weight with something that was almost hurry, and took Kade past the plant and down, and it was not courteous this time. It was quick and a little hard and the mat came up to meet Kade's shoulder with a sound the room might actually have cared about.

Nine.

He lay there. Above him the ceiling rail had acquired a faint edge, a brightness along its length that it had not had a moment before, and the two fingers were not doing their small thing any longer, they were doing a larger one, and the headache had left one without telling him.

"Bench," he said.

The room said it back, louder, in a voice with no person in it, and somewhere behind the mirror wall a lock clicked open.

Julian had stopped. Not slowed, stopped, one foot still lifted from the finish, and he set it down with great care and did not come closer. "Which one?"

"Light. Hand. Two. Maybe more."

"All right." He did not ask if Kade was sure. He went to the door and opened it, and Park was already in the corridor because she had been coming since the room spoke, and she came in and knelt on the mat in her clinic shoes and looked at Kade's eyes and said nothing at all for ninety seconds while the clock did its work.

"Number."

"Two. It was two when I said it."

"Hand."

He held it up. It was slower now. She watched it slow.

"Cold?"

"No. Light and hand, and I said the word."

Park wrote on her tablet and then, because she had done it twice before and he had come to need it, turned the screen so he could read. *Stop called by subject at threshold. No event. Session complete.*

"That's three," Kade said.

"That is three." She stood. She looked at Julian, who had not moved from the door, and Kade watched her decide something about him and not say it. "He was half a step into a throw when the room spoke. He stopped in the air. I have the record and I will be keeping it." To Kade: "You are done for today and you know that without my telling you. Water. Sit. Do not get interesting on my mat."

She went, and left the door open behind her, which in Park was a compliment.

Kade sat against the rail. Julian brought him the water and then sat down on the mat a body's length away, which he had not done in two mornings, with his back against the mirror so that neither of them could see anybody's reflection.

"You planted," Kade said.

"Yes."

"Left heel. Quarter second. You've never done that. Not once, two mornings, twenty falls. Nobody makes you, because nobody stays long enough for you to need your own weight." He drank. "That's the fee. That's what you're bad at. You've never had to commit, so you've never practiced what happens after. When you had to, you planted like a first-year and recovered like a fourth-year, and if I'd been any faster you'd have been on the rail."

Julian was quiet for a while. The clock went on.

"Say the number," he said finally.

"Nine."

"Nine, and the last one went eleven seconds, and I had to use my own legs." He was not looking at Kade. He was looking at the tile at the end of the mat, where the room stopped. "I've had upper-years beat me several times this term. Not one of them could have told me that. They don't know it either. They beat me from above, where it doesn't show." He turned the water bottle in his hands. "That's the first thing anybody's given me on this island that I couldn't have bought."

"You got up early for it."

"I did." Something moved in his face that was not a smile and had a smile somewhere behind it. "It's inconvenient. I now have to fix a thing in front of you."

"Then I get to watch you do it."

"That's the prize. It's the only one in here." He got up. He held out his hand, and this time it was the hand, and Kade took it and was pulled up, and the two fingers did their small separate thing inside Julian's grip, and Julian felt it and did not look, which was what a person did when they had decided a piece of weather was yours to report and not theirs to remark on.

"Next Thursday," Julian said. "Same plan. I'll have an answer for it."

"I'll have told you the plan."

"You'll have told me, and I'll still have to answer it instead of reading it, and if you hold it I'll have to plant again, and we'll both be watching my heel." He picked his band up off the rail, looked at the message still lit on it, and put it on his wrist without opening it. "That's what a rival's for. I'd forgotten. Nobody here has been one."

He stopped at the door.

"Your House trial. Sunday's the roster."

"I'm on a clipboard."

"Whoever's beside you on that floor, if you ever get on it, tell them the plan the way you told me. Then hold it long enough for them to be somewhere useful when it works." He said it looking down the corridor, not at Kade. "That's not advice. That's the rest of the fee. You still owe me a heel."

He went. The door stayed open.

Kade sat on the rail side of a room built for people learning to walk again, with a headache at two and going down and three rehearsals on a doctor's record and a folded wheelchair by the door, and did not look at the mirror. He took out his band. The Conduit mark was still gray under its lock. He wrote to Mara.

*Bay One, usual hour. Four steps and a turn, rear foot late. I'm going to hold it the whole circuit, including the part where it works. Telling you first.*

# Chapter 13 — Cross Examination

*Thirty-two. Mom says Park signed you into something called a contact tier and read it to her twice. I said what's a contact tier. She said one person is allowed to throw you and nobody is allowed to be interesting. I said that's not medicine. She said it is when she's the one reading it.*

Park had signed it at four the afternoon before, on the tablet with his three signatures on it, and she had read it to him aloud so that he could not later claim to have skimmed.

"Fields locked at the door. The room records position and impact, enough to call a medic, and nothing else. One partner. He has read your card and put his name under it. No ladder. No audience. The word is still bench, and in that room the word is wired. You say it, the room says it back louder, and the door opens for me." She looked at his right hand, where the last two fingers were behaving for the moment. "This is not a clearance. It is a tier. You are permitted to be thrown by a person. You are not permitted to be interesting."

"Who asked?"

"He did. I did not go looking." She turned the tablet so he could read the name under his own thumbprint, in a slanted hand that did not waste ink. CROSS, J.

Side Room B sat off the service corridor behind Arena Two, and at six in the morning nobody used the service corridor but cleaning units and people who did not want to be seen using it. The door read his band, said CONTACT TIER, and laid a small gray lock over the Conduit icon on his wrist. It was the first time he had watched Asterion switch him off and felt lighter for it.

Twelve meters a side. White composite, a strip of window along the top of the east wall showing a courtyard that had not gotten its light yet, and a mat with a blue square painted on it well inside the walls. At each corner of the square stood a padded column as high as his hip. Straight back was not a direction in this room. Every retreat bent.

Julian was already on the mat in training black, doing nothing, weight in the middle of his feet, the way he had sat on a couch arm holding a plate of dumplings.

"You read the card," Kade said.

"Four triggers. Open hand up. If the hand can't go up, the word." Julian did not move. "I signed it because Park would not open the door without it, and because it was correct. I don't expect to need it."

"Confident."

"Arithmetic. I don't throw the kind of thing that puts your hand up. You'll see what I throw."

He saw. It took nineteen seconds.

The second took twenty-three. The third took fifteen, because he was annoyed by the first two and tried something ambitious, and the ambitious thing turned out to be a description of where he would be standing when it failed.

On the fourth reset he tried the columns. He circled toward the nearest one and waited for Julian to close. The space narrowed the way he wanted. One lane left, one entry, no room for the longer reach to matter.

Julian stepped into the lane.

Kade came off the column and drove his shoulder at Julian's center, and Julian was no longer at his center. A hand found Kade's forearm. A heel found the place his recovery step wanted to go. The mat took him across the back, and the room made no sound about it, because nothing about it was an injury.

"You gave me the wall," Julian said, above him.

"I used the wall."

"First. Then you left it to me."

The distinction was still bothering him on the fifth reset, which was why the fifth took eleven seconds. By then he understood why one was one.

Julian was not fast. Kade had watched Pike, whose body asked the floor for permission a quarter second before it became dangerous, and had learned to move on the ask. Julian never asked. Nothing loaded early. Nothing committed until the answer was already known. He walked, and every step took a door off the room. He showed a hand, and the hand was not a strike, it was a question about which way Kade would lean, and Kade leaned, and the lean had been priced. By the time the finish arrived, the losing had happened three or four seconds earlier, somewhere Kade had not been looking, and the finish was only the room agreeing with it.

It was deeply annoying.

He had come in braced for the other thing, too. He would not have said so, but some part of him had walked through the door waiting for the cold line, for the moment a big transfer landed and the room stopped being separate objects. It never came. Julian did not throw big ones. He barely threw at all. He borrowed Kade's weight, turned it a few degrees, and gave it back somewhere Kade had not planned to receive it, and there was nothing in that to resolve anywhere. The rare thing he could do was worth exactly nothing against a person who never handed him a load to redraw.

On six he tried waiting. Julian waited better, and the waiting was not stillness. It was a slow removal of places to stand.

On seven he attacked first, and Julian used the attack to decide where Kade would be three exchanges later, and then went and stood there.

On eight he tried to be unpredictable. He was swept in fourteen seconds.

"You looked pleased," Kade said from the mat.

"I like it when an opponent announces the plan with his face."

"I did not."

"You did. Left eyebrow."

"Traitor."

Nine lasted thirty-one seconds. Ten lasted forty-two, and for six of those seconds he could not have said who had the room. Eleven ended when Julian put him, with real courtesy, exactly where he had spent the whole exchange trying not to go.

On the twelfth reset, Kade stayed on the mat.

Julian offered a hand.

Kade looked at it. "Is this part of it?"

"Yes. I exhaust people through manners."

He laughed, and took it, and the last two fingers of the hand did their thing while Julian was holding it. Julian felt it. He did not look down.

"Again?"

"No."

"Good."

Kade blinked. "You're supposed to tell me this is where character gets built."

"Tired people build habits. Bad ones. I'd rather you didn't build them in my room." Julian sat against the wall under the window strip, and after a moment Kade went and sat beside him, because standing had become a project.

The room smelled of mat cleaner and warm composite. Outside, the courtyard was coming up gray. Neither of them said anything for a minute, and Kade found he liked the silence more than he had expected to. Julian did not treat it as an invitation to ask about the floor.

"You're trying to work out why I'm doing this," Julian said.

"I was being subtle."

"No."

"Tragic."

Julian drank water. "I said a number on a roof. Four thousand at midyear, ordinary climb. I don't say numbers I don't mean. You're on a line, and the line comes off eventually, and when it does I'd like to already know what you do with your feet." He set the bottle down. "Everybody here needs somebody who can beat them. If nobody's exposing your weaknesses, you start guarding them instead of fixing them. It's the fastest way there is to stop."

Kade thought about Northline. Ms. Alvarez, who had known the difference between compliance and understanding and said so out loud. Sera, saying toes. Mara's shoe beside his on a taped mat. Park reading his deficiencies off a tablet like a shopping list.

"Who beats you?"

"Upper-years. Often."

"That's comforting."

"It should be."

"Do you enjoy it?"

"No." It came at once, with nothing behind it to soften it. Julian glanced over. "I enjoy learning. Losing is how most of it arrives. I'm not required to enjoy the delivery."

There it was. Competitive, not saintly. He found it a much easier thing to sit beside.

"Why me? Not why anyone."

"Because your curve is steep, and I'd like to understand you before I have to meet you in a bracket." Julian's mouth moved a fraction. "That's flattering and inconvenient in the same sentence. Pick which one to hear."

"Finally. Selfishness."

"Did it help?"

"Considerably."

Julian stood, which took him no visible effort, and touched the wall panel. The morning came up on it in a grid. Eleven small rectangles.

"You said no more."

"No more fighting."

He opened the first one. Two figures took their marks inside a top-down drawing of the square, Kade's path in orange, Julian's in white, the columns as four gray dots. It ran nineteen seconds and stopped.

"When did you lose?"

"When you hit me."

"Earlier."

Kade dragged it back to Julian's final entry. "Here."

"Earlier."

Two seconds further. His orange line bent toward the north column. Julian's white one shortened the angle. "Here?"

Julian shook his head.

He took it back to the start and let it run, and for five seconds nothing looked like anything. They traded ground. Kade tested the left lane. Julian gave up a hand's width of center.

Kade stopped it.

"You wanted me to take that."

"Why?"

"Because the column was going to narrow my exit."

"And?"

He walked it forward a frame at a time. His own lead foot turned toward the gap. Julian had not yet moved to close it.

"I committed before you did."

"That's where you lost."

The counter said six point four seconds. He looked at the other thirteen and they were a different thing now. Not a fight. A bill coming due.

"Then why not finish it there?"

"Because you still had choices. None of them got you the position you wanted. Some of them made me pay more for it." Julian opened the second rectangle. "Again."

He found it at eight seconds. The third was humiliating. Four point one, the instant he had launched the ambitious thing. The fourth took longer. On the fifth he blamed the column again, and Julian said nothing, and made him keep looking until he found the frame where he had given up a full step of distance to guard against a strike Julian had never intended to throw.

By the eighth he had stopped watching for attacks. He watched promises. A planted foot promised a direction. A raised guard promised which opening the fighter thought mattered. A retreat toward one lane promised that the next retreat still existed. Julian did not predict the future. He collected promises, and then arranged for Kade to break the wrong one.

Reset nine. The long one.

Kade stopped it at twenty-six seconds. "There."

Julian leaned in. "Why?"

"You stepped wide."

"I stepped wide four times."

"Not like that." He enlarged the white line. At twenty-six, it went farther from center than it had gone in any exchange of the morning. "I made you go and protect the edge."

Julian's eyes narrowed at his own path. "Go on."

Kade played three seconds. Julian took the center back. The opening shut like a door on a spring.

"You still won. But that's the first time you had to spend position to answer me."

Julian ran the three seconds twice. In the corridor outside, somebody's shoes squeaked past and faded.

"I should have changed level," Kade said.

"Probably."

"You didn't expect me to find that."

"I expected you to find where you lost."

"That isn't where I lost."

"No."

"That's where you nearly did."

Something sharpened in Julian's face, competitive and pleased, the same thing that had crossed it on the roof when he said he would like something to scare him. The reason for the invitation, showing through.

Julian copied the frame and sent it to Kade's band. "Bring me an answer to that next time."

"To your footwork?"

"To those three seconds."

Kade got up. His legs filed a complaint. "What do I get if it works?"

"Three more seconds."

"Generous."

"At your current rate, extremely."

At the door he looked back. Julian still had reset nine open, and he was not watching Kade's mistake. He was watching his own wide step, frame by frame, with the expression of a man who had lost.

They trained twice more that week, both times before the courtyard had its light.

Julian never asked about Zero Protocol. He never asked about the floor, or the yellow tape, or what the two fingers were doing when they did it. He asked why Kade stepped left under pressure. Why he held his breath before a counter. Why his first plan in almost every exchange lasted less than two seconds. Why he treated structure as a trap.

The last one got under his skin.

The scenario was simple. Julian held center. Kade had to make an opening without leaving the blue. He improvised three entries, and Julian stopped all three, and on the third he did it without moving his back foot.

"Again."

"I need a different entry."

"No."

"You stopped that one."

"Yes."

"So I need—"

"Use it again."

"That's stupid."

"Use it differently."

Same first step. Julian answered it the same way. Kade changed the second step, and Julian adjusted and stopped him. Again. Same first step, same answer, and Kade changed the third choice instead, and got half a meter before Julian turned him out.

"Again."

By the sixth he was angry, and the anger was clean and familiar and he did not trust it.

"Plans break."

Julian had not left his starting spot. "So?"

"So improvisation doesn't."

Julian looked at him for a while. "That is the most confident wrong sentence I've heard this month."

Kade smiled.

Julian pointed at him. "That."

"What?"

"You do that when you don't want to answer."

The smile went away on its own.

Julian stepped out of stance, which he had not done all week, and the room changed size. "You're good at adapting because you've spent years assuming the room will turn on you. I don't need to know why. But you plan as if trusting the plan is the part that gets you hurt."

Kade's shoulders went up before he could tell them not to. "Are you a therapist now?"

"No. I fight people."

"Comforting."

Julian waited, and did not follow it with anything. No second question. No help. It was worse than a question.

Kade looked at the blue square.

"At school a plan was somebody else deciding what I was supposed to do. Then grading me on it."

Julian nodded once. "That explains not liking other people's plans."

"Thank you."

"It doesn't explain refusing your own."

Kade stared at him.

Julian lifted his hands. "Again."

This time he used the same opening seven times. On the eighth, Julian shifted early to meet the adjustment Kade had used on the sixth, and Kade did not make it. He stayed with the original line one beat longer than he wanted to, and felt the wanting, the way he had felt it at the top of a stair with a full cup sliding toward him, and held. Julian's weight went toward an answer Kade never gave him.

For the first time, Julian had to change his feet instead of Kade changing his.

Kade went in. Julian blocked, rotated, and still put him on the mat. It took two minutes.

He lay there breathing. Julian stood over him and nodded, once, and Kade had learned enough of this island's language to hear praise when it was trying not to be.

"You almost had me."

Julian laughed. "Never say that after losing."

"I'm experimenting with confidence."

"Calibrate it."

Julian went to the wall and drew three white marks in a row. "Tell me your next opening."

Kade pushed himself up. "That ruins the opening."

"Only if it needs surprise to work."

"Surprise is useful."

"So are floors. You shouldn't need either of them to cooperate."

That landed somewhere under the ribs, and Julian had said it with no weight on it at all, looking at the marks.

Kade looked at them too. "Step outside your lead foot. Force the guard high. Enter under the right arm."

Julian drew a branch under the second mark. "If I keep the guard low?"

"Change level and turn you toward the blue."

Another branch. "If I yield center?"

"Take it."

Julian capped the stylus. "That's a plan."

"That's a list of things you now know."

"Correct. Use it anyway."

They reset, and Kade took the first step, and Julian's guard came up before Kade had touched him. The prepared answer sat there so visibly it looked like a dare. Every instinct he owned said abandon it.

He remembered a man moving the same way seven times and changing on the eighth. The plan was not a tunnel. It was a place to stand that had several roads out of it.

He forced the guard higher. He entered under the right arm, and took center, and Julian gave it, and Kade followed too fast, and Julian turned him and drove him toward the blue. Third branch. He stopped fighting for center, changed level, caught Julian's hip, and used the turn Julian had already started.

For one bright second, rank one had both feet outside the square.

Then Julian twisted in the air, touched one hand to the mat, and came down with a single foot inside blue. The room, which had been listening for exactly this, said nothing. He was up and behind Kade before Kade had finished being pleased, and locked it.

"Out," Kade said, into the mat.

"One foot in."

"Spiritually out."

Julian let him go. They checked the wall. One foot in, by three centimeters.

Kade enlarged the last exchange. His own orange route bent twice and never broke. Julian's white one had changed direction four times.

"You changed more than I did."

"Yes."

"Because I told you the plan."

"Because you kept it long enough to make me answer it."

He ran the turn again. The fall was still Julian's. The exchange belonged to Kade in a way none of the week's losses had, and not because he had been unpredictable. Because he had been legible, and dangerous anyway.

*Thirty-three. Mom says you've been thrown thirty times this week by one person and you sound better than you have in a month. I said that's the strangest sentence she's ever said. She said wait until you're a parent.*

Instructor Grae opened Tactical Systems by taking the highest-ranked student off every team.

Twenty first-years explained to him that this was unfair, unrealistic, and would ruin the exercise. Grae stood beside the simulation table in tactical gray and let them, with the patience of a man who had arranged all three complaints on purpose.

"The scenario doesn't know your rank," he said when they ran down. "Neither does a bridge that's coming down, or a habitat with a hole in it, or an opponent who read your roster before you did."

Five practice fields lit across the table, five versions of the same problem. A platform, drawn in blue, standing on four supports that would fail in an order the table would not reveal. Six civilian markers on it. A safe zone across two bridges. No combat scoring. No points for control. One condition. All six across before the platform went.

Julian stood up from Kade's table.

"Where are you going?"

"Highest ranked."

"Convenient moment to become obedient."

Grae pointed him to a chair along the wall, and across the room Mara was being pointed to the chair beside it with the expression of someone who had just been told the architecture of reality was suboptimal.

Kade's team was Priya Shah, two Faraday students he knew by sight, a quiet Perception user from Kepler, and a Storage specialist from Sol who began, immediately, working out whether the platform could be held up instead of emptied.

The clock started. The platform shivered.

Priya crossed first, clean, one marker to the safe zone in under four seconds.

"Again," Kade said.

The west support went. Seven degrees of tilt. The Storage student spread both hands over the controls and his field caught part of it, amber bleeding across the load display.

"I can hold it."

"How long?"

"Forty seconds."

A second support cracked. The number rewrote itself. "Twenty-six."

One of the Faraday students grabbed a marker and ran the north bridge, and the north bridge folded under him halfway, and the marker went back to the start, and the clock lost twelve seconds for it.

"That's not fair. We didn't know the bridge would go."

"You knew the platform would," Kade said.

"That's different."

The argument cost six more seconds, and then Kade started giving orders, because somebody had to. Priya on east. Storage on west. Kepler calling failures. Faraday carrying two at a time. Everyone moved, and for nine seconds it worked, and then east went early, and Priya doubled back into a Faraday student who was trying to cover for her and who blocked the Storage student's line to the west support, and the Kepler student called three changes at once, and Kade changed Priya's route and changed the carry order and sent Storage to the center brace and sent him back when the north edge started to shear.

Zero. Two markers still on the platform.

The field went dark. Julian, at the wall, said nothing, which was considerate of him.

Grae walked the room while each failed field showed its last frame. One team had six across. Sera's.

Her highest-ranked student was in the chair on the other side of Mara. Sera had given nobody a fixed bridge, nobody a fixed job, and nobody the work of calling every change. Her replay showed the team starting slow, in pairs, and shifting routes whenever her Perception user called a color instead of a diagnosis. Green, keep going. Amber, finish the crossing you are on and stop. Red, turn around. The last marker reached the zone with eight tenths of a second left.

Grae froze it. "Why this team?"

"Vale planned for failures," somebody said.

"Every team planned for failures."

Kade looked at the five last frames. His own team was clustered around the center, where he had stood rewriting everybody's job. Sera's was spread in pairs across three routes.

"Hers could lose pieces without losing its shape," he said.

Grae turned. "Explain."

"I gave people solutions. When a solution failed I had to give everybody a new one, and they stood there while I did it." He pointed at Sera's field. "She gave them conditions. When the condition changed, they already knew what they were doing."

Sera glanced across from the next table and did not smile, which from Sera was a compliment.

Grae nodded. "A plan is not a promise the future makes to you. It is the agreement you make with each other about what happens when the future doesn't keep it."

Kade looked at Julian. Julian was studying the ceiling with enormous dignity and a completely pleased face.

Mara's hand went up at the wall. Grae nodded to her.

"Vale also left capacity unused," she said. "Her Redirection operator finished with thirty percent in reserve."

Sera turned. "We moved everyone."

"You could have moved them faster."

"Fastest wasn't the condition."

Mara's eyes went to the frozen clock. Eight tenths. "It nearly became one."

"But it didn't."

They looked at each other across the room, and Kade understood both of them, which was inconvenient. Mara wanted every unit spent against the measured limit. Sera wanted a margin for the failure the table had not announced yet.

"A success condition tells you when you may stop," Grae said, to all of them. "It does not tell you how much margin tomorrow will ask for. Vey is right that unused capacity can be wasted capacity. Vale is right that a result with nothing left is fragile. Your job is to know which risk you bought."

Sera sat back. Mara nodded once. Neither looked convinced. Good class, Kade thought.

"Again. Highest ranks stay out."

This time he did not start by talking.

He asked the Storage student what overload looked like before the display said so. He asked Priya how much run-out she needed to stop with a marker in her arms. He asked the Kepler student which calls she could make while moving, and the Faraday pair whether they carried better together or apart.

They built three conditions. If a support crossed amber, Storage let it go instead of spending his reserve on a platform that was going anyway. If Priya lost a bridge, the nearest carrier finished her route while she cleared the next one. If two routes failed together, everyone left the edges and went through the center in pairs.

The clock started. Kade carried the first marker himself.

At fifteen seconds west crossed amber, and Storage let it go, and the platform lurched hard enough to slide two markers, and his reserve was nearly full. Priya lost east. Nobody waited for Kade. The nearest Faraday student finished her crossing. Priya cleared north. The Kepler student called red and ran with the last pair.

Two routes went together. Everyone moved through the center.

Kade reached for something to say, and there was nothing to add, so he carried.

Four seconds left when the last one crossed.

Nobody cheered. The Storage student bent over the table with both hands on it. Priya was breathing as if the table had asked her actual legs for the sprint. The Faraday pair bumped shoulders once.

The Kepler student looked at him. "You talked less."

"Cruel. Accurate."

In the corridor after, Julian fell into step beside him.

"How does it feel?"

"How does what feel?"

"Using the same plan twice."

"The first one failed."

"The first version failed."

Kade stopped. Students parted around them. "You arranged this."

Julian looked honestly puzzled. "The class?"

"Being pulled. The exercise. The lesson."

"I have Tactical Systems at the same hour every seventh day. I don't write the curriculum."

"That's what somebody who wrote the curriculum would say."

Julian started walking again. "You solved the next problem."

Kade caught up. "We moved six dots across a table."

"You stopped treating every failure as a reason to become the whole plan."

It caught under his ribs and stayed there. Julian kept his eyes on the corridor ahead. No personal question. No invitation to explain. Just the observation, set down and left where it was.

Kade disliked him slightly less. It was becoming a problem.

The next morning Julian ran an open clinic on Field Three, and Kade went, because the clinic ran on a locked field under Hale's sign-off, which put it inside the tier, and he had checked, because the card was in his pocket.

He had assumed an open clinic meant a crowd watching rank one perform. It meant Julian spending twenty minutes with a second-year ranked under six thousand who crossed her feet every time she was driven left.

The small bleachers held twelve. Three had come to train. The rest had come for footage, or to stand near a name.

Julian ignored the bands pointed at him. "Again."

She reset, went left, and her trailing foot crossed behind the lead. Julian's hand touched her shoulder before she could uncross it.

"Dead."

Her face tightened. "I know."

"Then why?"

"Because you keep driving me left."

"I'll keep doing what works."

She reset harder than she needed to. On the next exchange she overcorrected, went wide, and froze in the wide stance like something photographed.

"Dead."

The student pulled off one glove. "This is pointless." She started for the gate, and two people in the bleachers pretended not to be recording her leaving.

Julian did not call her back.

Kade stood up off the rail. "You took too many decisions away."

Julian looked at him. "That's usually the object."

"For an opponent."

The student had slowed at the gate.

Kade stepped onto the field. "You told her what not to do. Then you kept building the exact problem that makes her do it."

Julian's expression cooled a degree. "Do you have a suggestion?"

"Yes." Kade pointed at the second-year. "Come at me."

She looked between them. "Which of you?"

"Me. He's had enough joy today."

Julian stepped aside.

She pulled the glove back on. Kade took center. Going right she was fine. Going left, her rear foot crossed the instant he pressed inside her shoulder, and he stopped before it finished.

"Again. You're not defending the whole field." He marked out one square of mat between two seams with his heel. "Keep me out of that."

"That's smaller."

"Very observant."

She reset. He drove left, and her feet started to cross, and then she gave up the outer lane, planted wide, and kept him out of the square.

"Again."

Three tries later the step held. He widened the square. The fourth try broke. They narrowed it again. By the tenth she could give ground across half the field without crossing.

Julian stood with his arms folded and watched her feet, not Kade.

She tried once against him. He drove left. She gave the lane, held the square, stayed upright, and Julian stopped short of the finish.

"Good."

She looked wrung out and furious and pleased. "Can I go now?"

"Yes."

She went with her head up, and the bands followed her for a different reason.

Julian waited until the gate closed. "You were right."

Kade inspected him for signs of illness. "Could you say that near a recording device?"

"No."

"Expected."

Julian picked up his water. "I was solving the error. Not the person who was making it."

"Apparently everybody needs somebody who can beat them."

Julian's mouth moved. "You didn't beat me."

"I improved your clinic. Social victory."

"Not recognized by the ladder."

"Weak ladder."

Julian laughed, which he seemed to resent, and tossed Kade a practice band. "Your turn."

"I already trained."

"You crossed your feet twice while demonstrating."

"Slander."

Julian took the center. Kade fastened the band. The bleachers leaned in, and for once he did not mind being examined.

The third session ended with nine losses instead of eleven. Progress, of a kind.

They walked to breakfast together, and people noticed. He could feel it in the way glances lasted a beat long. Rank one and Zero Mercer crossing the same courtyard was a sum people wanted to finish.

"Do you like this?" Kade asked.

Julian looked around. "People watching?"

"Being known."

Julian took the question seriously, which was a thing he did. "No. I like winning. Fame is the tax on it."

It sounded practiced. Kade filed it.

They came into the Meridian Commons together, under the ladder wall, where Kade's line still wore its red bar. That alone produced another wave of looks.

Taren was at the long table with Sera and Eli. He saw Julian, then Kade, and visibly rewrote whatever he had been about to say.

"Interesting," Taren said.

Kade set his tray down. "No."

"I said nothing."

"You were going to."

Julian took the end of the table. Sera looked at him over her cup. "You know there are nineteen other Houses."

"I was invited."

Kade stared. "I didn't invite you."

Julian took an orange from the bowl. "Your roommate did."

Eli looked pleased with himself. "I thought cross-House tactical exchange would be healthy."

"You wanted to ask him about his acceleration model," Sera said.

"Also healthy."

Breakfast became, unexpectedly, breakfast. Eli waited until the orange was half peeled before he put his tablet beside Julian's tray.

"Your public acceleration data has a discontinuity."

Julian kept peeling. "Good morning."

"Between first commitment and second correction. The model says you change direction before your opponent changes load."

"Then the model's wrong."

Eli looked wounded. "That's one possibility."

"What's the other?" Sera said.

"Cross violates causality."

"Rank-one privilege," Kade said.

Eli opened two graphs. One was Julian's acceleration. The other tracked an opponent's center of mass. A narrow blue band sat between them. "There. Ninety milliseconds."

Julian looked, finally. "You're measuring the wrong decision."

Eli leaned in. "Movement is the decision."

"Movement is evidence the decision already happened." Julian touched the opponent's line two hundred milliseconds earlier, at a wobble so small Kade could barely tell it from noise. "His rear heel lifts here. He can still go either way. He can only make useful force in one."

Eli shifted the model. The discontinuity disappeared. He looked at the graph as if it had borrowed money from him.

"So public telemetry can model decision quality," Kade said.

"No. It can model some of the physical commitments that follow decisions."

"That's decision quality with defensive phrasing."

"The difference matters. If Navarro trains on outcomes, the model will call every move before a win intelligent."

Eli stopped with his stylus in the air. Then he started writing.

Sera stole one of Julian's orange segments. "The ladder does that already."

Julian looked at the missing segment, then at her. "The ladder doesn't measure intelligence."

"It measures what the academy values and then pretends the number is the person."

"It measures competitive performance."

"Exactly."

She pointed with the segment at the wall display cycling the week's rank changes. "Put those same people in a smoke-filled residence hall with somebody unconscious on the floor, and half of them lose a thousand places to whoever knows how to carry a body down stairs."

"That's a different ranking."

"There isn't one."

Julian thought about it. He did not have a fast answer, and did not manufacture one, and Kade marked the pause.

Taren reached across and took a second segment while Julian was distracted. "Cross won a junior regional once after throwing up behind the officials' table."

Julian turned. "Food poisoning."

"Nerves."

"Food poisoning."

"My source was present."

"Your source was eight and lost in the first round."

Taren smiled. "So you remember him."

"He told everyone in the medical bay I was afraid of the final."

"Were you?" Kade asked.

Julian looked at him as if the question were strange. "Of course."

The table went quiet for half a beat. Rank one had said it with no shame in it at all.

Taren recovered first. "There's also the Marseille podium."

"False."

"And the mascot."

"Technically accurate. Maliciously framed."

Eli looked up from the telemetry. "What happened to the mascot?"

Julian ate the last segment before anyone could take it. "Nothing you can prove."

Kade mostly listened. It was the first time he had seen Julian inside a group rather than at the top of a board, and the group had absorbed him the way Meridian absorbed everything, by arguing with it.

At seven forty-two Julian's wrist lit.

He looked down, and his posture changed. Barely. A degree of tightening through the frame and then control laid over it, the same thing Kade had watched happen on a roof when a second-year said the words House office. He saw it now because he had spent a week learning what people did before they moved.

Julian dismissed it.

"Family?" Taren said.

Julian looked at him. "How do you know that."

Taren lifted both hands. "You make a face."

"I do not."

"Everyone has one."

Julian stood. "I have a call." And went.

Kade looked at Taren.

"That was precise."

"I notice things."

"So do I."

Taren's smile stayed exactly where it was. "Then we'll both be annoying."

Kade put it in the file. Laundry at midnight. Hale's review in the dark. Pike pulling the open an hour before the wall said so. A face Taren could name from across a table. The file had a spine now, and he still could not read the title.

---

Later that week he found out what the face meant.

He was returning borrowed wraps to Side Room B and heard the voice through the half-open door before he saw the screen. Julian stood in front of the wall panel. A man occupied it. An older cut of Julian's face, sharper, in a suit that fit the way the posture fit.

"You gave him the inside line twice."

"It was a training session."

"You lost thirty-one seconds to a fourth-year in a training session. Why did that happen?"

Julian's jaw set. "Because he's good."

Silence.

"That is not an answer a Cross gives."

Kade stopped in the corridor. He should have gone. Instead he stood there, because people were most honest when they thought nobody was listening, and he had learned that in kitchens.

Julian's voice went cold. "It's the answer I'm giving."

"You are number one."

"Today."

"You have been given every advantage there is. Do not discover humility now."

The screen went dark.

Kade stepped back a second too late. Julian came through the door and saw him.

For a moment neither of them said anything. Kade held up the wraps.

"Terrible hiding place."

Julian looked at him. "Very."

"I was returning these."

"I believe you."

That made it worse, somehow.

Kade looked past him at the blank panel. "Your father seems cheerful."

Julian laughed once, with nothing in it. "He thinks rank is a moral category."

"Sounds exhausting."

"It is."

Kade waited. He remembered a girl in a greenhouse saying her father liked systems that agreed with him. The top of the ladder came with family the way the bottom did, apparently, only better dressed.

"Don't make this a reason to go easy on me," Julian said.

Kade blinked. "You think I would?"

"No." Julian took the wraps from him. "I think you might mistake understanding for pity. They look alike from inside."

That was fair, and Kade said so.

They stood in the corridor.

"Thursday?"

Julian looked almost relieved. "Thursday."

---

On Thursday Julian beat him seven times, and then changed the format.

"Three," he said, and touched the panel, and the room's counter changed from one fall to three. "A single fall teaches you nothing about what happens after I've read you. You can be read and still be a problem. That's the part I want to see."

Kade went in with an actual plan. Not a clever opening. A plan. Give Julian center. Let the pressure come toward his weak left retreat, the one Julian had been mining all week. Refuse the retreat. Force a second decision at the blue.

It worked for forty seconds. Then Julian recognized it. Kade felt him recognize it, the way he had felt Pike get interested, and every old instinct said change everything now, be somebody else, be unreadable.

He stayed. He changed the details and kept the shape. Julian took the first point at fifty seconds and Kade reset into the same plan, and Julian took the second at a minute forty, and at two minutes Julian stopped smiling, because the plan had not gone away when the points had. At three minutes Kade refused the left retreat for the fourth time, and Julian, committed to the answer that had worked twice, found nobody there, and Kade turned him at the blue and put both his feet across it.

The room said so. One clean point, the first he had ever taken from rank one, and nothing in it had been rare. That was the point. It had cost him nothing he had not planned to spend.

At three fifty-one Julian trapped him against the edge and finished it.

Kade sat on the mat breathing hard. Julian offered a hand.

"Better."

"There's that word."

"You made me leave the first plan."

Kade took the hand. "Only the first?"

"Don't become unbearable."

"Too late."

They reset once more, and he lost, and he enjoyed that one. Not the losing. Knowing why.

His band updated on the way out. Mara.

*You trained with Cross.*

*Your surveillance network is impressive.*

The dots sat there a while.

*Taren. He is very good. Learn everything. Bay One is still the usual hour.*

Kade stopped walking. No jealousy. No performance. Advice, and a door left open.

Julian glanced over. "Problem?"

"Mara Vey told me to learn everything from you."

Julian's eyebrows went up. "That's nearly a compliment."

"Everybody here communicates through technical hostility."

"It saves time."

Kade typed. *Already getting thrown efficiently.*

*Progress.*

He smiled. Not the one that closed doors. The other kind, which was getting easier, and he put the band away.

Julian held the dining hall door. "Saturday? Same time."

"You want another seven?"

"I want to see whether you make me leave the plan sooner."

There it was. Not charity. A challenge, made by somebody who expected to be paid back.

Kade went through the door. "Fine."

"Good."

For as long as he could remember, every person better than him had asked him to be less difficult. Julian was asking him to be difficult in a more disciplined way, and to hold it long enough that somebody else had to answer. That was a kind of respect Kade knew what to do with.

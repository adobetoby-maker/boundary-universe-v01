# Chapter 13 — Cross Examination

*Thirty-two. Mom says one person is allowed to throw you now and she's read his name. I asked if she liked it. She said it's a good name for somebody who reads the whole card before he signs it. I said that's a low bar. She said you'd be surprised how many doctors trip on it.*

Park did not hand the card back so much as set it on the desk between them with one finger on it, the way she set down a chart she was not finished with.

There was a fifth line on it now. Four triggers in her hand and Hale's, and under them, in a slant that did not waste ink, a name and a date and four words. *Read and accepted. J. Cross.*

"He came to the clinic," she said. "Not to me. To the desk. He asked for a copy of your stop conditions, and the desk told him they were medical, and he said yes, that was why he wanted them. Then he sat in my waiting room for forty minutes until I had time to say no to him."

"And you didn't."

"I said no. Then he showed me what he was asking for, and I said something else." She turned the card so the new line faced him. "A room with the fields locked at the door. One partner, and only that partner. Position and impact recorded, enough to send me running and nothing more. No ladder. No spectators. The stop word is wired into that room. You say it, the room says it after you, louder, and my door unlocks itself." She took her finger off the card. "That is not a clearance. You are permitted to be thrown by a person. You are not permitted to be interesting."

"He knows what the card's for?"

"He read it twice and asked me what the fourth trigger looks like from the outside. I have had faculty not ask that." She looked at his right hand, where the last two fingers were behaving for the moment. "You have said the word twice for nothing. Say it in there for something and I will write *complete* beside it, the same as before."

He picked up the card. Somebody had read the whole thing before putting a name under it. That was the low bar, and he was still oddly glad to find a person standing on the right side of it.

Side Room B was at the end of the service corridor behind Arena Two, past the cleaning units parked nose to wall, and at six in the morning the corridor belonged to nobody. The door read his band, showed him CONTACT ONLY with a small gray lock laid over the Conduit mark on his wrist, and let him in. He had been switched off by this island before. It was the first time it had felt like being handed a coat.

The room was a square, twelve meters to a side, with a strip of window along the top of one wall showing a courtyard that was still waiting for its light. A blue square was painted on the mat well inside the walls, and at each corner of the square stood a padded post about hip high. He had been told the posts were there so nobody backed into a wall. Julian was walking the square when Kade came in, heel to toe along the blue, the way a man measures a room he intends to buy.

"Eleven paces," Julian said, without looking up. "Any side. If you ever need a twelfth, you've already lost." He finished the lap and stopped, weight in the middle of his feet, exactly as he had once sat on a couch arm with a plate on his knee. "Park told you the room says the word back."

"She told me."

"Good. Then you'll know it isn't me." He nodded at the panel by the door. "I've turned the counter off. The room keeps score whether we look or not, and I'd rather we didn't. You'll want to count. Don't."

Kade had a plan, and did not say it, because that was what plans were for. He had spent two weeks learning to watch Jonah Pike load a lead leg a quarter second before the leg became dangerous, and to move on the load instead of the strike. Julian was bigger than Pike and slower to look at, and Kade meant to give him nothing to load against and go the instant Julian settled onto a foot.

Julian never settled onto a foot.

That was the first thing, and it took three falls to believe it. He waited for the ask, the way Pike's body had always asked the floor for permission, and it never came. Julian walked. That was all it looked like. He walked, and each step made one of the four posts matter a little more, and when Kade finally went, because standing still had started to feel like a decision too, he went at a man who was already half a pace from where his eyes had put him. A hand found his sleeve. A heel found the place his recovery step wanted. The mat took him along the shoulder blades without a sound, because nothing about it was an injury, and the room only cared about injuries.

The second thing was the posts. He had thought they were his. Four columns meant lanes, lanes meant corners, and a corner was where reach stopped mattering and speed started. On the fourth reset he worked Julian toward the nearest post and felt the room narrow exactly the way he wanted it to. One entry. One line. No room for the longer arm. Julian stepped into the line as if he had been invited. Kade drove off the post at his center and hit the air where the center had been, and the post caught his own hip on the way down.

"That one was yours until you looked at it," Julian said, from above.

"I built it."

"You built it, and then you told me where the door was." He did not offer a hand yet. "Every time you make a room smaller, ask who it's smaller for."

The third thing was the one he had not admitted to bringing. He had walked in braced for it, under everything else: the cold line, the second when a load came in and the room stopped being separate objects. Some part of him had wanted to find out whether it would come in here, with the fields locked and Park's door wired to a word. It did not come. It had nothing to come for. Julian did not throw the kind of thing that could be redrawn. He borrowed Kade's weight, turned it a few degrees, and handed it back somewhere Kade had not arranged to receive it, and there was nothing in that for the rare thing to resolve. Two floors and a wall had made him a curiosity. In this room his curiosity was worth exactly nothing, and it was the most useful thing he learned all morning, and it took him until the seventh fall.

On the eighth, Julian stopped a reset himself. He had a hand on Kade's forearm from the finish and he did not let go of it.

"Your hand."

Kade looked. The last two fingers were doing their thing, small and separate, the way they did when he had been upright a while.

"That's not the card," he said. "That's just what it does now."

"Which trigger is it nearest?"

"None. Park's got a line for it. It's a weather report."

Julian let go. "Then I'll learn what the weather looks like, so I know it when it's something else." He went to the panel and drank from a bottle without turning around. "Ask it. The thing you haven't been asking since you came in."

"Why."

"That's the one."

"You said a number on a roof. Four thousand at midyear. You said don't make you look stupid."

"I don't say numbers I don't mean." Julian turned. "That's some of it. Here's the rest, since you'll get it from Vale otherwise and she'll make it sound kinder than it is. Nobody here is hitting the things I'm bad at. Upper-years, sometimes. Not often enough. A weakness nobody hits stops being a weakness after a while. It turns into a stance, and then a style, and then you're a hundred and eighty-three telling first-years on a roof that they won't." He capped the bottle. "Your curve is steep and you don't move like anybody I've filmed. I'd like to understand you before I meet you in a bracket. That's flattering and inconvenient in the same breath. Pick one."

"The inconvenient one."

"Sensible."

"Do you like it? Losing to the upper-years."

"No." It came without a pause and with nothing laid over it. "I like what it teaches. I'm not required to like how it's delivered."

That was better than kindness. Kindness he would have had to watch. He got up off the mat, and the room, which had been told not to count, went on counting.

He lost three more. Somewhere in the middle of them he stopped trying to be a surprise and started trying to be a problem, and the last one ran long enough that for a stretch of it he could not have said who owned the square. It ended with him exactly where he had spent the whole exchange trying not to be, put there with real courtesy, and he stayed down.

"Eleven," Julian said, to the ceiling. Then: "I told you not to count."

"I didn't. You did."

"I'm allowed." He put a hand down, and Kade took it, and the two fingers did their thing while Julian was holding them, and Julian felt it and did not look. "Again?"

"No."

"Good. Tired people build habits, and I don't want yours built in here."

---

The panel by the door was not a wall of footage. Julian brought up a top-down drawing of the square, four gray dots for the posts, and eleven thin strips along the bottom, each the length of a fall.

"Don't watch them yet," he said. "Sort them. By how early you lost. Not how long they took. Earliest first."

Kade looked at the strips. He had assumed the two orders were the same order. "Earliest is the fifteen-second one. The ambitious one."

"Put it there."

He dragged it to the front. He put the first fall second, because he had still been learning the room. He put the long one last, because it was the long one. Julian let him finish, then took the strips apart and laid them out again, and the fifteen-second one was still first, and the long one was not last. It was fourth.

"Play the first," Julian said. "Tell me when you're dead."

Kade played it. Two figures on the drawing, his path in orange, Julian's in white. At four seconds and change the orange line launched, and the white one had already moved to meet it. "There. Four. That's where I went."

"Yes. Now this one." Julian pulled the nineteen-second fall up beside it. "Where."

"When you hit me."

"That's when you fell. Where did you die."

Kade dragged it back to Julian's final entry. Julian shook his head. Back two more seconds, to the bend toward the north post. No. He took it to the start and let it run, and for five seconds it was two people walking. Then Julian gave up a hand's width of center on the left, and Kade's lead foot turned toward the gap, and Julian had not yet moved to close it.

He stopped the frame himself. "I took what you gave me."

"Before I'd decided to give it."

"Six seconds." There were thirteen more on the strip. They did not look like fighting anymore. They looked like the room collecting a debt. "Why not finish me there?"

"Because you still had choices. None of them got you the square. Some cost me more than others, and I wanted to see which you'd pick." Julian moved the strips again, so the order was the order of the commitments. Four seconds. Six. Eight. In the long one, the one Kade had put last and Julian had put fourth, he had died at nine and gone on for thirty more because Julian let him. "That's the ladder you're actually on. Not the wall in your Commons. This."

Kade went through them. By the sixth strip he had stopped looking for Julian's attacks, because Julian did not really make any. He looked for what his own body had promised. A planted foot promised a direction. A guard promised which opening he thought mattered. A retreat toward one post promised he believed the next retreat still existed. Julian collected the promises as they were made and arranged for him to break the wrong one, and the finish, when it came, was only the room agreeing.

"The long one," Kade said. "All of it."

"You died at nine."

"I know when I died. I want the rest."

Julian gave him the rest. Thirty seconds of a dead man walking, and at twenty-six the white line did something it had not done all morning. It went wide. Not a step wide. A whole body's width off center, out toward the east post, and it stayed there a beat before it came back.

Kade stopped it. "What's that."

"A step."

"You haven't stepped wide once. Eleven falls. You stand in the middle and make me come around you." He enlarged the white line. "I made you go and protect the edge."

Julian did not answer. He ran the three seconds, and ran them again, and out in the corridor somebody's shoes squeaked past the door and faded.

"You still had me," Kade said. "But that's the first time you had to spend position on anything I did."

"I expected you to find where you lost."

"I found where I lost. That's not it." He pointed. "That's where you nearly did."

Something crossed Julian's face that Kade had seen once on a roof, when a man had said he would like something to scare him. It was not pleasure, exactly. It was interest with teeth in it.

Julian copied the three seconds to Kade's band. "You went low there and did nothing with it. Bring me what you should have done."

"To your footwork."

"To those three seconds. Nothing else in the eleven is worth your time."

Kade stood, and his legs registered a formal objection. "And if I bring you something that works?"

"Then I'll have to change something, and you'll get to watch me do it." Julian was already back at the panel. "That's the only prize in this room."

At the door Kade looked back. Julian had the long one open, and he was not watching the orange line. He was watching his own step go wide, frame by frame, with the face of a man reading a bill he had not expected.

*Thirty-four. Mom says you're being thrown by the same person before breakfast and she can hear you eating on the phone. I said that's disgusting. She said it's the best sound she's heard off that island. I don't understand her. I've stopped trying.*

Julian did not ask about the floor. Not the second morning, not the third. He never said Zero Protocol, or the yellow tape, or asked what the fingers were doing when they did it. He asked why Kade retreated left when he was pressed. Why he held his breath before a counter, so the counter arrived a little late and a little short. Why the first plan in any exchange lasted less than two seconds before he threw it away for a second one. Why he treated every post and wall and line in the room as something built to catch him.

The last one Kade did not answer, and Julian did not ask it twice. He built a morning around it instead.

"Your first step is mine for the hour," he said, and stood in the center of the square with his hands loose. "Outside my lead foot, low, the way you did on the third strip. You don't get to change it. Everything after it is yours."

"You'll stop it."

"Yes."

"Then it's a bad first step."

"It's a fine first step. You threw it away the second I answered it." Julian nodded him in. "Again."

Same step. Julian's answer came where Kade knew it would come, and Kade changed his second step, and Julian adjusted and turned him out. Same step, same answer, and Kade kept the second and changed the third, and got half a meter of center before Julian took it back. Same step. By the sixth he was angry, and the anger was clean and familiar, and he trusted it less for being both.

"A plan's just the list of things that haven't gone wrong yet," he said, from his knees.

Julian had not moved from center. "Of everything you've said this week, that's the one you believe."

"It isn't a belief. It's a description."

"You said it like physics." Julian stepped out of stance, which he had not done in three mornings, and the room changed size. "You're good at changing course. That's real, and I'd keep it. But you don't change course when the plan fails. You change it when the plan starts working, because a plan that's working is a thing somebody could take from you."

Kade's shoulders went up before he had voted on it. "Are you a therapist now?"

"No. I hit people. It's a narrower field." Julian waited, and did not put a second question after the first, and did not help. It was worse than being asked.

Kade looked at the blue square. It was eleven paces on a side and had never done anything to him.

"At Northline a plan was a sheet with my name at the top," he said, "in somebody else's handwriting. And a grade at the bottom for how well I'd been it."

"Nobody wrote this one." Julian lifted his hands. "You did. You're still grading it like theirs. Again."

Same step, seven times. On the eighth, Julian moved early, to meet the adjustment Kade had used on the sixth, and Kade felt the change coming up through his own feet and did not make it. He stayed on the original line one beat longer than every instinct he owned wanted him to, the way he had once held a table steady on a roof with a full cup sliding toward the far end, and Julian's weight went toward an answer that was never given.

For the first time in four mornings, Julian moved his feet because Kade had not moved his.

Kade went in. Julian blocked, turned, and put him down anyway, and it took long enough that the courtyard had its light by the time Kade was looking at it from the mat.

"Say it," Julian said.

"I nearly had you."

"Don't say that after losing."

"I'm trying confidence. It's new."

"Calibrate it." Julian went to the panel and, instead of the drawing, opened a blank page and held out the stylus. "Now write the next one down."

"That's the whole opening gone."

"Only if it needed to be a secret." Julian did not lower the stylus. "You needed a floor to cooperate once. Don't need surprise to cooperate too."

It went in under the ribs, and Julian had said it looking at the panel, with no weight on it at all. Kade took the stylus. He wrote: *Outside the lead foot. Force the guard high. Enter under the right arm.*

"If I keep the guard low."

He wrote it underneath. *Change level. Turn you toward the blue.*

"If I give you center."

*Take it.*

"That," Julian said, reading it, "is a plan."

"That's a list of things you know."

"Both. Use it anyway."

They reset, and Kade took the first step, and Julian's guard came up before there was anything to guard, so visibly it looked like a dare. Every instinct he had said abandon it. Be somebody else. Be nobody. He remembered a man doing the same thing seven times and changing it on the eighth. A plan was not a tunnel. It was a place to stand with more than one road out of it.

He forced the guard higher and went under the right arm and took center, and Julian gave it, and Kade followed half a beat too fast, and Julian turned him and drove him toward the blue. The third road. He stopped fighting for center, dropped his level, caught the hip Julian had already turned, and finished the turn Julian had started.

For one bright second rank one was off the ground with nothing under him but the line.

Julian came down with one hand flat on the mat and one heel on the blue, and the room, which had been listening for exactly this, said nothing at all.

"Out," Kade said, from the position he found himself in a moment later, which was face down.

"The line's in."

"The line's a rumor."

They checked the drawing. The heel was on the paint by less than the width of the paint. Kade enlarged the last four seconds. His own orange line bent twice and never broke. Julian's white one had changed direction four times.

"I told you the whole thing," Kade said, "and you moved more than I did."

"You told me the whole thing and then kept it long enough that I had to answer it." Julian closed the page. "Legible is fine. Legible and gone in two seconds is what I've been putting on the mat all week."

The fall was Julian's. The four seconds were Kade's, and not because he had been hard to read. Because he had been easy to read, and had made the man move anyway.

Instructor Grae killed the highest-ranked student on every team in the first second of the exercise.

He did it with the table, not a speech. Five copies of the same problem lit across the simulation surface: a platform on four supports, six civilian markers on it, two bridges to a safe zone. The opening frame showed the first support failing and one figure on every team going gray. The gray figure was, on every field, the top rank.

"Your best operator was standing on the platform when it started," Grae said, in the dark gray of the tactical faculty, to twenty first-years who had not finished objecting. "That's where the best operator usually stands. Bridges don't check the ladder. Neither does a habitat with a hole in it, or anyone who read your roster before you did."

Julian stood up from Kade's table without being asked.

"You're gray," Kade said.

"I'm gray." He took the chair along the wall that Grae's hand had indicated, and across the room Mara was taking the one beside it with the expression of a woman who had just been informed that the physical constants were poorly chosen.

The condition was one line long. All six across before the platform went. No score for control, none for style, and no clock but the platform's own.

Kade's team was Priya Shah, who had not forgiven him the wall and said so with her whole posture; two Faraday students he knew by sight; a Perception user from Kepler who had said perhaps eleven words since the term began; and a Storage specialist from Sol who was already asking whether the platform could be held instead of emptied. Kade thought that was the wrong question and said so, and lost four seconds saying it, and then the second support went and the argument was over.

He would have told anyone that he did not give orders. He watched himself give them. Priya to the east bridge, because she was fast. Storage to the west support, because he wanted to hold something. Kepler calling failures. Faraday carrying as a pair. For nine seconds it was a good plan, and then the east bridge failed a full beat before the table had said it would, and Priya doubled back with a marker in her arms into a Faraday student who was trying to cover her, and that student was standing in the Storage specialist's line to the support he was holding, and the Kepler student called three things at once because three things had happened at once. Kade rewrote Priya's route. He rewrote the carry order. He sent Storage to the center brace and sent him back when the north edge began to shear.

The platform went with two markers on it. The field went dark, and along the wall Julian said nothing, which was considerate, and which Kade minded more than if he had.

Grae walked the fields with the last frame frozen on each. Four dark. One lit. Sera's.

She had not been at the center of hers. That was the first thing Kade saw, before Grae said a word. His own last frame had five figures bunched around the middle of the platform, where he had stood rewriting everybody's job, and Sera's had hers in pairs at the mouths of three routes, and her Perception user had said a single word each time something changed. Green. Amber. Red. Not what had failed. What to do about it. Her last marker had crossed with under a second left on the platform.

"Somebody tell me why that one," Grae said.

"She planned for failures." One of the Faraday students.

"Every one of you planned for failures. I watched you do it. Mercer."

Kade did not look away from the frames. "Hers could lose a piece without losing its shape. I gave people solutions. When a solution broke I had to hand everybody a new one, and they stood there while I did it. She gave them conditions. When the condition changed, they were already moving."

Sera looked across from her table and did not smile, which from Sera was a decoration.

"A plan is not a promise the future makes you," Grae said, to the room. "It's the promise you make each other about what happens when the future doesn't keep it."

Kade looked at the wall. Julian was studying the ceiling with a face of complete innocence and complete satisfaction, and it was not possible that a student had arranged a faculty exercise, and Kade decided to resent him for it anyway.

Mara's hand went up from the wall. Grae nodded to it.

"Vale's Redirection operator finished at seventy percent," Mara said. "She left thirty on the table."

Sera turned in her chair. "I got six across."

"You could have had six across sooner."

"Sooner wasn't on the sheet."

Mara's eyes went to the frozen clock. Eight tenths of a second. "Sooner was about to be."

"And then it wasn't."

They looked at each other across two tables, and Kade found that he understood both of them, which was inconvenient. Mara wanted every unit spent against the measured limit, because the limit was the truth. Sera wanted something left in the tank for the failure the table had not bothered to announce, because the table was not the world.

"A success condition tells you when you're allowed to stop," Grae said. "It doesn't tell you how much margin tomorrow will ask for. Vey is right that capacity you didn't use may be capacity you wasted. Vale is right that a result with nothing behind it is a result on credit. Your job is to know which of the two you bought. Again. Gray stays gray."

This time Kade did not start by talking.

He asked the Storage specialist what overload looked like before the display admitted it, and got a real answer about the color at the edges of his hands. He asked Priya how many steps she needed to stop with a marker in her arms, and she told him, without forgiving him. He asked the Kepler student which calls she could make while running and which needed her to stand still. He asked the Faraday pair whether they carried better together or apart, and they said together, at the same time, and looked at each other.

Grae wanted the plan on the table's slate before the clock. The slate had a line at the top for who would call the changes. Kade left it blank, and then, because a blank line looked like an oversight, wrote *the conditions* in it, and Grae read that upside down and made a small mark on his own tablet that Kade could not see.

Three conditions. If a support crossed amber, Storage let it go and kept his reserve, because a platform that was going was going. If a carrier lost a bridge, the nearest carrier finished her route while she cleared the next one. If two routes failed together, everybody left the edges and came through the center in pairs.

The clock started, and Kade picked up the first marker himself, and carried.

West crossed amber at fifteen seconds and Storage let it go. The platform lurched hard enough to slide two markers, and his hands stayed the color of hands. Priya lost east, and nobody looked at Kade. The nearer Faraday student finished her crossing while she cleared north. The Kepler student said one word, red, and ran with the last pair, and two routes went together, and everybody came through the middle.

Kade opened his mouth to change something, found nothing waiting to be said, closed it, and carried.

The last marker crossed with four seconds on the platform.

Nobody cheered. The Storage specialist had both hands flat on the table. Priya was breathing as if the table had asked her real legs for the sprint. The Faraday pair bumped shoulders once without looking at each other. The Kepler student looked at Kade and spent three of her words.

"You talked less."

"Cruel. Accurate."

Grae ended it, collected the slates, and looked at Kade's a moment longer than the others. "Six across," he said. "The exercise doesn't care who calls it. The form does. We'll discuss that when I grade the form."

In the corridor afterward Julian fell into step beside him without any sign of having chosen to.

"How was it?"

"We moved six dots across a table."

"With the same plan twice."

"The first one failed."

"The first version failed." Julian kept his eyes on the corridor ahead. "You let a thing break without going to stand in the middle of it. That's all I've been trying to throw you into all week. Grae got there in an hour."

"You arranged this."

"I have Tactical Systems every seventh day at this hour. I don't write it."

"That's what somebody who wrote it would say."

Julian did not answer, and did not ask what it had felt like, and did not offer to explain what it meant. He left the sentence where he had set it down and walked. Kade found that he disliked him slightly less, and that this was starting to be a problem.

The clinic was on Field Three the next morning, under Hale's sign-off and a locked field, and Kade checked that twice against the card in his pocket before he went. He had said the word for nothing twice. He did not intend to say it for something in front of a crowd.

The crowd was fourteen people on the low bleachers. Four had come to be taught. The rest had come to be near a name, or to hold up a band and record one, and Julian looked through them the way a person looks through a window.

He was on his fifth student. A second-year, somewhere under six thousand by the color of her band, who crossed her feet every time she was driven to her left. Julian drove her left. She crossed. His palm touched her shoulder before she had uncrossed and he said, "Dead," in a voice with no weight in it.

"I know."

"Then why."

"Because you keep going left."

"I'll keep doing what works. Again."

She went again, and this time she did not cross, because she did not move at all. She planted both feet wide and stood in the stance like a photograph of a stance, and Julian walked around it and touched her shoulder from behind. Dead. She did not take the glove off. She did not go anywhere. She simply stopped, hands down, looking at the mat, the way Kade had seen kids stop in Room Four when the third correction in a row had convinced them there was no version of themselves that passed.

Julian said, "Again," and she did not move, and he did not know what to do with that. Kade could see him not knowing. It was the first thing all week he had watched Julian not know.

Kade came off the rail. "You've taken all her choices away."

Julian looked over. "That's the object."

"With an opponent." He stepped onto the field. "You told her what not to do and then built the one problem that makes her do it, ten times over, and now she's got nothing in her hands but the mistake."

The second-year had lifted her head.

"You've got a better way," Julian said. It was not a question, and it had cooled a degree.

"I've got a smaller one." Kade pulled the roll of tape out of his jacket, the one he had carried since Bay One, because Mara taped everything and it had turned out to be catching. He laid a square on the mat between two seams, a meter on a side, and stood beside it. "Come at me. Not the field. Keep me out of that."

She looked between them.

"Me," Kade said. "He's had his turn."

Julian stepped aside and folded his arms.

Going right she was fine. Going left, her rear foot went behind the lead the instant he pressed inside her shoulder, and he stopped before it finished and pointed at the tape. "Nothing else matters. Not the field, not the lane, not him. That."

She reset. He pressed left. Her feet started to cross, and then she gave up the whole outer lane without a fight, planted wide, and kept him off the square. He widened it a hand's width. She held. He widened it again and the step broke, and they took it back in, and by the tenth try she could give ground across half the field with the tape behind her and her feet in the right order. She was breathing hard and furious, and she had not once looked at the bleachers.

"Once against him," Kade said.

Julian drove her left. She gave the lane, held the square, stayed on her feet, and he stopped short of the finish and said, "Good," and it had weight in it this time.

"Can I go?"

"Yes."

She went with her head up, and the bands on the bleachers followed her out for a different reason than they had been waiting for.

Julian waited for the gate to close. "That was right."

Kade examined him for signs of illness. "Say that near a recording device."

"No."

"Thought not."

"I was fixing the foot." Julian picked up his water. "You fixed the person it was attached to. I'd have got the foot eventually."

"You'd have got the foot after she'd left."

"Yes." He said it flatly, without defending it, and Kade filed that beside the *no* about liking to lose. "You didn't beat me."

"I improved your clinic. That's a social victory."

"Not a category the ladder has."

"The ladder's missing categories."

Julian laughed, and seemed to resent it, and threw him a practice band. "Your feet crossed twice while you were demonstrating."

"That's a lie."

"Twice. Take center."

Kade fastened the band. The bleachers leaned in, and for once he did not mind being the thing they were examining.

The third morning in Side Room B ended at nine, which was a number Julian said aloud and Kade pretended not to hear. Then they walked to breakfast together, and the walk was its own event.

He could feel it in how long the looks lasted. Rank one and the boy with the red bar, crossing the same courtyard at the same pace, was a sum people wanted to finish before they reached the dining hall.

"Do you like it?" Kade asked.

"Being watched?"

"Being known."

Julian took the question seriously, which was a thing he did. "No. I like winning. Being known is the tax on it."

It sounded like a sentence that had been said before. Kade filed it and did not say so.

They went into the Meridian Commons together, under the ladder wall, where MERCER, KADE. 8,159. RESTRICTED. still wore its red bar, and that alone turned heads at three tables. Taren was at the long one with Sera and Eli. He saw Julian first and Kade second and visibly rewrote whatever sentence he had been about to say.

"Interesting," Taren said.

"No," said Kade, and put his tray down.

"I said one word."

"It was a loaded word."

Sera looked at Julian over her cup. "There are nineteen other Houses."

"I was invited."

Kade stared at him. "I didn't."

"Your roommate did." Julian sat at the end of the table, and Sera, without looking up, slid the empty toast rack toward him with two fingers. He looked at it. He understood it. He got up and went to fill it, because that was the rule at this table and he had been invited to the table.

Eli was delighted with himself. "Cross-House tactical exchange. It's healthy."

"You want to ask him about his acceleration curve," Sera said.

"That's also healthy."

Julian came back with toast, and breakfast became, against the odds, breakfast. Eli waited for the toast to be half gone before he set his tablet beside Julian's plate.

"There's a hole in your public acceleration data."

Julian buttered toast. "Good morning."

"Between the first commitment and the second correction. Ninety milliseconds. The model says you change direction before the other person changes load. Either the model's wrong or you're breaking causality."

"Rank-one privilege," Kade said.

"Then the model's wrong," Julian said.

Eli looked wounded on the model's behalf and opened two graphs. One was Julian's acceleration. The other was an opponent's center of mass. A narrow blue band sat between them, and Eli tapped it. "There. Ninety."

Julian looked, finally, then put a fingertip on the opponent's line two hundred milliseconds earlier, at a wobble so small Kade could barely tell it from noise. "You're measuring the wrong decision. Movement is the receipt. The decision's back here. His rear heel lifts. He can still go either way, but he can only make useful force in one, and I've already gone there."

Eli slid the model back. The band closed. He looked at the graph as if it had borrowed money from him and moved house.

"So telemetry can score decisions," Kade said.

"It can score some of the things a body does after it's decided." Julian went back to the toast. "The difference matters. If Navarro trains this on outcomes, it'll call every move before a win intelligent, and half of them were luck."

Eli's stylus stopped in the air, and then started writing.

Sera reached across and took a piece of Julian's toast. "The ladder does that already."

Julian looked at the missing toast, then at her. "The ladder doesn't score intelligence."

"It scores what the school values and calls the number the person." She pointed the toast at the wall display, where the week's rank movements were cycling. "Put those same people in a stairwell full of smoke with somebody unconscious on the landing, and half of them drop a thousand places to whoever knows how to carry a body down stairs."

"That's a different ladder."

"There isn't one."

Julian thought about it. He did not have a quick answer, and he did not make one, and Kade marked the length of the pause.

Taren took a second piece of toast while Julian was still thinking. "He threw up behind the officials' table at a junior regional and won the final anyway."

Julian turned his head. "Food poisoning."

"Nerves."

"Food poisoning."

"My source was in the medical bay."

"Your source was eight years old and lost in the first round."

Taren smiled. "So you do remember him."

"He told the whole bay I was afraid of the final."

"Were you?" Kade said.

Julian looked at him as if the question had been asked in the wrong language. "Of course."

The table went quiet for half a beat. Rank one had said it with nothing in his face to protect.

Taren recovered first. "There's also the thing with the trophy."

"That's false."

"And the coach you thanked who wasn't your coach."

"Correct and unkind."

"What happened with the trophy?" Eli said, looking up.

Julian ate the last of his own toast before anyone could take it. "Nothing anyone can document."

Kade mostly listened. It was the first time he had seen Julian inside a group rather than on top of a board, and the group had taken him the way Meridian took everything, by arguing with it and handing it the toast rack.

Julian's wrist lit a little before eight. He looked at it, and the thing Kade had watched happen on a roof happened again, smaller, and Kade saw it because he was learning what people did before they moved: a tightening through the whole frame, and control laid over it so fast it was almost not there. Kade looked at Taren.

Kade put it in the file, behind the roommate and the Sol thing and the Kepler feed and Pike's note an hour before the wall. A face read from across a table, correctly, first try. The file had a spine now. He still could not read what was written on it.

*Thirty-eight. Mom says you told her about the person before she asked, and then about the person's father, and then you said never mind about the father. She says never mind is a whole sentence with you. I said it's a whole sentence with anybody. She said not the way you say it.*

The father was an accident of timing. Kade came down the service corridor on Thursday at two minutes past six, because Julian had said six and Kade had learned that six meant the door, and the door read his band and opened the way it always did, and there was a man on the panel.

An older cut of Julian's face, sharper at every edge, in a suit that fit the way the posture fit. Julian stood in front of it with his back to the door and did not turn.

"You gave him the inside line twice," the man said. "In a training session. To a fourth-year."

"It was a training session."

"You lost half a minute to him. I want to know why that happened."

Kade stepped back out of the door's eye, into the corridor, and the door closed. He should have kept walking. He stood in the corridor instead, because people were most honest when they thought the room was empty, and he had learned that in a kitchen at Northline before he had learned anything else, and it was not a thing he was proud of and not a thing he had ever managed to stop.

The panel's voice came through the door, faint. "Why."

"Because he's good," Julian said.

"That is not an answer a Cross gives."

"It's the one I've got."

"You are number one."

"Today."

The panel went quiet. A moment later the door opened, because Julian had opened it, and he stood in it and looked at Kade, and neither of them said anything for as long as it takes a cleaning unit to turn around.

"Then I was here at six oh two."

"You didn't come in."

"No."

Julian looked at him a moment longer, then stepped back and let him in, and the panel behind him was blank.

Kade thought of a girl in a greenhouse saying her father thought a system was honest when it agreed with him, and of a woman in a parked car who read his chart before he called. The top of the ladder came with family the same way the bottom did. It was only better dressed.

"Don't make it a reason to go easy," Julian said.

"No. I think you might mistake understanding for pity. From inside, they look the same." He picked up the water bottle. "I'd rather have the eleven."

That was fair, and Kade said so, and took his shoes off.

Julian changed the room before the first reset. He touched the panel, and the blue square on the mat went from a line to a score: a point each time one of them put the other across it, first to three.

"Falls teach you what you did wrong once," he said. "I want to see what you do after I've read you. Anybody can be a surprise for forty seconds."

Kade had brought a plan. Not an opening. A shape.

He gave up center on the first exchange without a fight and let Julian have it. He let the pressure come toward the left retreat that Julian had been mining for a week, the one that had cost him more falls than the posts had. He let Julian believe in it. And at the line, with a post behind his right shoulder and the retreat open behind his left, he refused it. He stood, dropped level, and made Julian decide something at the paint instead of a pace before it.

It worked for about a minute. Then he felt Julian recognize it, the way he had once felt Pike get interested, a change in the quality of the pressure, and every old instinct he had said: change everything, now, be somebody else, be nobody.

He kept the shape and changed the details. Julian took the first point at the line, and Kade reset into the same plan, and Julian took the second by walking him straight off it, and somewhere past the second minute Julian stopped smiling, because the plan had not gone away with the points. The third time Kade refused the left, Julian was already there. The fourth time, Julian had committed to being there, because it had worked twice, and Kade was not, and Kade turned him at the paint and put both his feet across it.

The room said so. One point, in a flat voice, off a number that had never given him one before, and there had been nothing rare in it. That was the point. He had spent exactly what he had planned to spend.

Julian took the third point a while later, against a post, cleanly, and the room said that too.

Kade sat on the mat and breathed. Julian put a hand down.

"Better."

"That word again."

"You made me leave the first plan."

Kade took the hand. "Only the first?"

"Don't become unbearable."

"Late."

They ran it once more, to a single point, and Julian took it, and Kade found that he enjoyed that one. Not the losing. Knowing where.

His band went on the way out. Mara.

*Taren says a great many things.*

The dots sat there a while.

*He is very good. Learn everything. Bay One is still the usual hour.*

Kade stopped walking. No jealousy in it, no theater, no claim. An instruction and a door left open, in that order.

Julian glanced over. "Problem?"

"Mara Vey says I'm to learn everything from you."

"That's nearly a compliment."

"Everyone on this island talks in technical hostility."

"It saves time."

He typed. *Learning to be thrown efficiently.*

*Progress.*

He put the band away, and the smile he did it with was not the one that closed doors. The other one was getting easier to find.

Julian held the dining hall door. "Saturday. Same hour."

"You want another nine?"

"I want to see if you make me leave the plan sooner." Julian let the door go once Kade was through it. "And I want the three seconds. You've had them a week."

There it was again. Not charity. A bill, presented by somebody who expected it paid.

For as long as Kade could remember, every person better than him had wanted him easier. Less difficult, less argumentative, less trouble to have in the room. Julian wanted him harder, on purpose, for longer, and had a wall panel and a wired word and a father on a screen to prove he meant it. That was a kind of respect Kade knew what to do with. He owed a man three seconds by Saturday, and for the first time since a floor had cracked under him, the thing he owed was something he could pay with his feet.

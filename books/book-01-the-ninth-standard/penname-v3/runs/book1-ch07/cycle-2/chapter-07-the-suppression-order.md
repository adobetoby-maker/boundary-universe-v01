# Chapter 7 — The Suppression Order

*Fourteen. The comments found out you're last. They're mad about it. I don't get that. If I was a wall I'd want you last too.*

Nobody at breakfast said the number forty-seven, and that was how he knew every person in the room had watched it.

Anton talked about the eggs. Nessa Kim read something on her band and turned her wrist over when he sat down. Taren Holt came in with a tray of coffee from the far side of campus and made a five-minute case for the South Hall roast that had, Kade noticed, no room in it anywhere for a question. Sera ate standing, by the door.

Anton broke first, because Anton's hands could not keep a secret. He put his fork down and built a box in the air above the table. "Fourth-year on the bridge says Hale's pulling your whole field line. The year. Medical withdrawal."

"He said review," Nessa said. "Not withdrawal."

"He said it like withdrawal."

"Who told the fourth-year?"

Anton looked at his hands as if they might know. "Somebody."

Mara Vey passed behind his chair on her way out and looked once at his right hand where it lay beside his plate. She did not say anything. He put the hand in his lap.

"That was an answer," she said, and was through the door.

At nine sixteen his schedule went white.

He had been watching it, because Sen had said *my office, tomorrow* and not said when, and so he saw the next six days of field instruction go out of the grid one block after another, like windows going dark down a street. Where they had been, three gray lines.

TRAINING STATUS: RESTRICTED. ACTIVE FIELD AUTHORIZATION: SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW. RANKED CHALLENGE AUTHORIZATION: SUSPENDED.

Yesterday's word had been SUPERVISED. He had disliked it on the bridge. He wanted it back.

Under the gray lines: REPORT TO DIRECTOR SEN. 09:30. No sentence to go with it. Half a word was worse than none. She had told him that herself, and here was the half.

Eli read it over his shoulder and made the small sound he made when a drone met steel. "Want me to come?"

The no was in Kade's mouth before the question had finished, where it had lived for seventeen years, and he heard it sitting there and made himself wait until it was his.

"No," he said. "I'd have wanted you to ask, though."

Eli nodded like the distinction had been on a syllabus. It had not. It counted anyway.

---

Sen's office was on the top floor of the oldest building on the island, where the walls were stone instead of glass and nobody had asked the furniture to be comfortable. He was early, and did not like what that said about the summons.

Sen stood at the window. Dr. Park sat at the table with a tablet face down in front of her. Instructor Hale sat beside her with his slate. One chair was empty. Kade sat in it.

"Good morning."

"Anton Bell says I've been withdrawn for the year."

"Anton Bell is not on the order."

"Is there an order?"

"There is now." Sen came away from the window. "Yesterday I told you supervised. Dr. Park spent the night with your telemetry and this morning she changed my mind. I wanted you to hear that from me and not from a bridge."

Park turned the tablet over. The replay ran without sound. Forty-one. The foot, the pulse, the palm. Six frames of nothing where the room's telemetry should have been, and then the wall already carrying what it carried. She froze it there.

"Measured field output from your Conduit across the event. Zero. Excess structural load at the west wall footing, four point eight kilonewtons above the model. The model includes every pulse Shah put into that floor. It does not include you, because there is nothing of you to include."

"Is four point eight a lot?"

"For a student who registered nothing," Hale said, "it's four point eight too many."

Kade looked at the six frames. "So the plan is to keep me away from the one thing on this island nobody can explain. Which is me."

"The plan is to stop adding unknowns until we can count the ones we have."

"Say the short version."

Sen looked at him and did not fill the pause with anything. "I think it could happen again. I do not know what it does to you when it does."

It took the next joke out of his mouth, whole. She sat down across from him.

"Afterward you had a headache, a tremor, unequal pupils, and a door you looked at and could not keep."

"One door."

"I have run experiments on a great many things. I will not run one on your head."

"Shame. It's the interesting part."

"Kade."

Not louder. That was what made it hard to push against. At Northline they raised their voices, and a raised voice was a handle you could take hold of.

"This is not a punishment."

"It's wearing the uniform."

"I know what it's wearing." For a second she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. Then it was gone.

Hale slid a schedule across the table. It had things on it, which surprised him. Conditioning. Standard academics. Tactical theory. Combat observation. Zero-field footwork and grappling. Conduit Theory. Then the other list. No field activity of any kind. No attempt to reproduce the event. No ranked challenges until medical and field clearance.

"No ranked challenges."

"Correct."

"I'm ten thousand four hundred eighty-two."

"We're aware."

"Out of ten thousand four hundred eighty-two."

"We can also subtract," Hale said.

"So I stay where I am."

"Until you are cleared," Sen said.

The heat arrived then, late, the way it always did with him, and he stood up with it. The chair went back hard enough that Hale looked at the chair.

"Anything else?"

"One thing." Sen's eyes narrowed by a degree. "A fence is not a verdict. Try not to read it as one."

"People keep building me fences and telling me that."

He was at the door when Park said, "Mercer."

He stopped.

"Your footwork is poor. Your conditioning is below cohort median. Your defensive timing is inconsistent. Your formal physics is incomplete. You have almost no trained combat habits." She had not looked at anything to say it. "None of that needs a field to fix. If the only part of you that you think is worth training is the part we just fenced, you will stay easy to beat, and it will not be our doing."

"How long have you been saving that?"

"Since your baseline. It kept." She stood. "And you're coming with me. Medical. Now."

"I was in medical yesterday."

"Yesterday you couldn't find a door."

---

Park did not take him to the examination room. She took him down the service corridor under the seats, the one with the pipes, and stopped him at the first door.

"Same as yesterday. Left and right. Out loud."

"You're testing me with a hallway."

"I am testing you with the hallway you failed. Go."

Left, one. Right, one. Left, two, with the fire panel. The corridor bent, the pipes went from four to three, and he said, "Right, three," and put his palm flat on the door as he passed, to make it real. Right, four. Left, three. The far door with the keypad. Nine.

"Nine," Park said, and wrote nothing down, which he understood was the point.

The room was the small one from the afternoon before, minus Priya Shah in the chair. Park stood him on the floor plate and leaned it three degrees. He stayed. She took it to five. He stayed, and swallowed, and she brought it level and wrote a one for nausea without asking, because she had been watching his throat.

She put a tablet in his hands, blank page, stylus. "Your name. The street you grew up on. The date, in numbers."

The name came out fine. The street number shook on the four, and he went back over it until it was dark enough to look decided. She took the tablet before he reached the date.

"Don't fix it. I need the first one." She put the page on the wall beside one from yesterday. His name from the hour after the fight looked like it had been written on a moving tram. Today's looked like his, unless you looked at the four. "That is the difference between improved and resolved."

"When am I resolved?"

"When I can't find the four. I've guessed at that in both directions before, and I no longer guess out loud." She sat on the end of the bed with her own tablet. "Symptoms. I'm going to write exactly what you say."

"Headache one. Hands are fine."

She typed while he watched. HANDS ARE FINE.

"That's going in like that?"

"You said it." She turned the screen so he could see the time beside the line and the small lock beside the time. "This record keeps what people say in the order they say it. Nobody edits it afterward. Not Sen, not me, not you. If you want something different in there, you say something different, and it goes underneath."

At Northline you found out what had been written about you a year late, from a person who was using it. Here it was, in front of him, and it was wrong, and he had made it wrong.

"Right hand," he said. "Mild. Worse when I'm trying to hide it."

She typed it under the first line and did not touch the first line. Both entries, one over the other, for as long as the record existed. It was a strange thing to be steadied by.

"Who ends this?"

"Three people, and none of them alone. The order is Sen's. She will not lift it until I tell her your brain has finished doing whatever it is doing, and Hale tells her you can be stopped on a floor without a negotiation. Medical is mine. Field is his."

"What does medical want?"

"A baseline that holds. No lost time. No headache or tremor that comes back on its own. Controlled activity with nothing in the room that the room cannot account for." She tipped her head toward the corridor. "The doors and the four are how I take today's reading. They are the thermometer. They are not the fever."

"And Hale?"

"A training plan he can stop in the middle of, and a student who stops with it." She scrolled down. Under each name, a plain line: request review by someone who did not make the decision. "Most students never read that far."

"Can I have it? All of it?"

"It's your record. The order, the traces, the criteria, and every line anyone writes after today, with a name and a time on each one." He sent a copy to himself, and then, before he had decided whether it was too private, a second copy to an address at St. Vincent Medical.

"Every honest line I give you keeps me here longer."

"Some of them will. A line you leave out does not make you better. It makes the record wrong. And the record is the thing that is going to let you out."

The order was still a wall. It had names on it now, and a door where the names were, and the first thing written on his side of it was a wrong answer with the right one underneath.

---

It stayed private for about forty-seven minutes. He was aware of the number.

The wall panel outside Meridian Commons carried duty rotas, lost property, and whatever the fourth-years thought was funny. When he came off the bridge it was showing the ladder. One line of it, his, blown up to the height of a door. MERCER, K. 10,482 / 10,482. Somebody had drawn a neat glass case around the line, the kind a museum sets over an object that must not be handled, and lettered a card beneath it. PLEASE DO NOT TOUCH THE EXHIBIT.

He read it twice. It was better than he would have done. It was also exactly true. Nobody could challenge him. He could not challenge anyone. The order had built the case, and whoever this was had only drawn what was already there.

Eli came down the stairs with his tablet open. "I can put the numbers under it. Four point eight. Six frames. Let them argue with the room's own gauges."

"You don't have the numbers."

"I have Park's sentence and a very good guess."

"Then you'd be putting Park's sentence on a wall." Kade did not look away from the case. "It's my chart, Eli."

Eli's thumb stopped above the panel. He looked at the card, and at Kade, and put the tablet under his arm. "Okay."

The thing that went through Kade then was gratitude, and he disliked it, and not for the reason he would have guessed. Eli had been ready to fight for him with arithmetic, the only weapon Eli owned. That was not it. It was that Kade had asked him to put it down, and he had.

Two second-years came out of the Commons behind them and slowed the way people slow near an accident.

"Sen's not sitting on him for his sake," one said. "She's sitting on him for ours. You saw the wall."

"You saw the floor. He made a Standard Three drill look like a toy and they benched him for it. That's not safety. That's embarrassment."

They went on down the corridor, having settled it. Neither had looked at him. Both versions were more flattering than the truth, which was a shaking hand and a door.

Taren found them at the foot of the stairs with his band already lit. "The forum named it. THE SUPPRESSION ORDER. Four hundred replies and climbing. You're either a weapon they're keeping in a drawer or a mistake they're keeping in a drawer. Nobody in there has said the word pupils."

"How do you know about the pupils?"

"Park said it on the floor. A hundred people heard her." Taren smiled the smile that made rooms easier. "I read faster than most of them. That's all."

Kade filed it. The file was getting a shape.

---

Conduit Theory met at eleven in a room that had a rig where the lectern should be.

A lifting platform the size of a door sat inside a steel frame, and the frame stood on a floor plate, and all three wore gauges. Professor Amadi did not begin with a sentence. She began with a list on the wall, and the list was the gauges, and she told them to choose one.

"Every number in this room has an owner. Today you are the owner. When the platform moves you call your gauge, and you are responsible for it being right."

Nobody wanted the floor plate. The plate, a girl in the second row said, never did anything. Kade, whose band was gray and who was not permitted within a meter of the operator's mark, took the plate. It was the only part of the room he was allowed to touch.

A first-year with Standard Three clearance stepped onto the mark and lifted the platform one meter. Numbers went around the room. The platform's owner called mass and height. The frame's owners called their strain and sounded pleased with themselves.

"Plate."

"Down," Kade said. "By the whole lift. Minus whatever the frame ate."

Amadi turned her head. "What did the frame eat?"

"Whatever it flexed. It's steel. It gave a little. The rest went into the floor, and the floor went into the building, and I don't own a gauge for that."

"No," she said. "Facilities does." She put a second document up beside the gauges: one line from the academy's maintenance ledger, the yearly cost of replacing the plate under this rig. "The platform rose one meter. Someone paid for the meter. The Conduit did not pay. It only told us who did."

They spent the hour on it: paths, limits, losses, and owners. Every transfer went somewhere. Every somewhere belonged to someone. He understood perhaps sixty percent and wrote forty, and the margin of his page filled with question marks until it looked like a rash.

Near the end she zeroed the gauges, and to zero them she opened the rig's calibration matrix, a table older than the paint on the frame. Reference profiles, she said, from the first generation of Standard Eights. The rig had been built to read against them, and nobody had seen a reason to teach it otherwise.

Twelve narrow columns filled the wall. VIII-01 through VIII-12.

He had been counting things for an hour, because that was what the plate's owner did. He counted the columns twice.

"Who's twelve?"

Amadi did not look up from the zeroing. "Nobody."

"There's a column."

"There are eleven recognized first-generation Standard Eights and a reserved identifier. The schema is older than the classification. They left room."

"Room for what?"

She looked at the matrix then, half a second longer than a professor needs to look at a table she has shown a hundred times. "For a number that was never assigned. A calibration table is not a census, Mercer. Zero your gauge."

He zeroed his gauge. He also counted again, and it was still twelve.

Then she put the balance for his plate on the wall in notation and asked him to finish it. He could draw it. Boxes, arrows, the platform, the plate, the building, the meter going up and the load going down. He could not write it. The symbols sat there like a language whose alphabet he had been shown once, and he stood at the wall with the stylus while the room waited.

"That is a picture," Amadi said. "I asked for a sentence." She sent him a foundations sheet, forty lines of the notation, due at Study. "You have the physics. You do not have the grammar. The second is faster to learn than the first, and less fun."

Nobody laughed. Nobody watched him sit down. That made failing stranger than it had ever been in Room Four, where he could have decided the sheet was stupid. It was not stupid. He did not know enough yet, which was a worse problem, and a more solvable one.

Dining Close was steam, a sanitizer that shrieked when it reached temperature, and Sera Vale walking the line every ten minutes with a towel over her shoulder and nothing kind to say about anyone's stacking. Eli talked to the dish machine the way he talked to the drone. Kade did trays.

Priya Shah came through the kitchen door at twelve forty with her braid down and a plate she had already eaten from, and handed it to him.

"Lunch," she said. "I keep my word. I didn't say who'd be washing."

She leaned on the steel counter where Sera could not object to it and told him the floor. The plates were densest at center, so a pulse there traveled cleanest and farthest. She had put him at the edge on purpose, where the lattice went soft and his own weight would do most of her work. Anton had been right with his hands. He had just lacked the words.

"I asked Park for the load numbers this morning," she said then.

"She won't give you those."

"She didn't. She gave me a look and asked how my shoulder was. Which told me the interesting part wasn't my shoulder." She watched him rack the plate. "They fenced you."

"Yes."

"I'm glad."

The tray in his hands went still.

"If they'd cleared you this morning I'd have turned down the rematch, and I want the rematch. Your hands were going for an hour. I put people down for a living, and I'd like to keep doing it to people who are all there."

She went out. Eli said, from inside the steam, "She's very reasonable."

"I know."

"It's hard to build a grievance around."

"I know."

---

Hale's afternoon was a zero-field mat, and Nessa Kim was already on it, band gone gray for the hour by choice instead of order.

"Kim. Perception, four thousand one hundred eighteen. Mercer. Nobody lights anything." Hale sat on the bench with his slate. "Kim. Tell him."

"I'm going to say what I'm about to do before I do it," Nessa said. "Every time. Then I'm going to do it."

"That seems generous."

"It isn't. Left ankle."

He moved the left foot back, and she took the right one instead, and the ceiling lights went past and the mat arrived.

"I said left."

"You moved it. To move it, you put your weight on the right." She did not offer a hand; she was waiting to see whether he would ask for one. He got up without it. "I don't need the ankle. I need where the weight goes. Telling you the ankle just makes you send it there."

It went on like that, ten more announcements, ten more visits to the mat, and he stopped being surprised somewhere around the seventh, which was not the same as staying up. Then she said, "Hip," and he heard the word and did not answer it. He watched her weight instead. It moved onto her front foot, the way Mara's had, the way Priya's had, the way it had to, and he moved on that and not on the word, and for five full seconds she had to work. She got him. But she changed her grip twice to do it.

"That one," Hale said. "What was different."

"I stopped answering her."

"You stopped taking her word for it. The rest of the hour you were fighting the sentence. Sentences don't have hips."

Nessa picked up her towel. "You made it to five. Your weight talks. I listen. You can learn to say less."

"How?"

"Slowly." She went through the far doors toward the acceleration lab with the students whose bands were still lit, and he stood on a mat that did not need him for anything.

Hale handed him the slate. "North rail. One line. Something I can use. Not something you can't sense."

The rail looked down on a long floor with a pressure plate at each end. A ten-second window, start whenever you like inside it, touch the far plate and stop. The clock ran from the first movement, not the tone, so the run belonged entirely to the student's choice of when.

Priya took the floor, settled, and was gone, and was standing on the far plate with her head at exactly the height it had started. He watched the second run for her feet, because everyone did. On the third he watched the rest of her, because he had spent forty-seven seconds yesterday looking up at the rest of her, and it was the only view of Priya Shah he had ever really had.

Her ribs dropped. Then she went. Fourth run, the same. A long breath out, the settle at the bottom of it, and the launch off the bottom.

He wrote: SHE BREATHES OUT, THEN SHE CHOOSES.

Hale read it over his shoulder. "You can't sense her load."

"I can see her ribs."

Nessa came up the rail with a trace open across her wrist; she had been at the far plate the whole time, which he supposed was the point of her. Hale waved her over.

"The load starts eleven hundredths after the ribs finish dropping. All four runs. She's launching off the end of the exhale."

"So the breath is the tell."

"The breath is the constraint. She has to empty before she loads. Everyone does. She just does it the same way every time, and you were lying underneath it."

"Correction," Hale said.

*Don't breathe* was advice for a statue. "Breathe out on the reset. Not on the go. Then she's launching off nothing an opponent can watch."

Hale went down and said four words to Priya. She looked up at the rail with both eyebrows raised, then went to the mark and tried it. Exhale, settle, a beat with her lungs empty, and go. The run was slower; he could see it without the clock. She overran the far plate by half a step and had to come back to it.

She came to the bottom of the rail. "Who."

Hale pointed.

"I've had a year of people watching my feet. Nobody watches my ribs."

"I was underneath them."

"You were," she said, and did not seem to like how good the answer was.

Nessa held up the trace. "He couldn't have measured it. I can. He could only see it because you put him on his back and stood over him breathing."

That was the line, and it was Nessa's, not his. He had not found a weakness in Priya Shah. He had noticed one thing she did every time because she had done it while hitting him, from about a meter away.

Priya ran it again, slower again, and clean, and pointed at her own ribs, then at him, then at the floor. He was fairly sure the third gesture was a promise.

He wrote a second line under the first. SLOWER. HARDER TO READ. HER CHOICE.

Hale took the slate. "I said one line."

"You said something you could use."

Hale looked at it a moment longer. "Both of these are."

Meridian Study had a rule, and the rule had a board.

A fourth-year rang a brass hand bell the size of a teacup from the top of the Commons stairs at nineteen hundred and said, "Two hours. Stuck goes on the board. One line each, no talking, no exceptions." Then every table filled, and nobody had to tell him Study was a ritual, because the room did.

The board was an old whiteboard on wheels with a dent in one corner. Anyone who was stuck put the problem up. Anyone passing could fix exactly one line, in silence, and walk on. By quarter past there were four problems on it, and three had strangers' handwriting in the margins.

Eli had cables across half a table. Sera was drawing exit routes over a floor plan with the focus of someone planning an actual evacuation. Taren sat with three people arguing about economics, and won, and let them think it had been close. Mara had the window and nobody near her.

Kade nearly went upstairs. Eli pushed a chair out with one foot, not an invitation exactly, just space, and Kade sat in it and fought the foundations sheet, and the sheet won. Line twelve would not balance. He did it three times and got three different wrong answers.

He did not put it on the board. He had never once in his life put a thing he could not do where people could see it.

At nineteen fifty he put it on the board.

Nobody looked up. That was the rule. He went back to the table and did not watch it, and at nineteen fifty-four Eli walked past on his way to the water, wrote one line in the corner in his small engineer's hand, and kept walking. *Twelve: your transform is upside down.* Nothing else. Eli sat down and did not meet his eye, because that was also the rule.

Kade turned the transform over. The line balanced. So did the twenty-eight after it, in a rush, the way a jammed drawer opens all at once.

Elena called at twenty hundred exactly, and he took it on the bridge, because the Commons had ears and the roof had last night in it.

She was at a nurses' station with a cup of something that had gone cold hours ago. She had read the record. He could tell because she did not ask him what had happened.

"Who wrote hands are fine?"

"Me."

"And the line under it?"

"Also me."

"Good." She turned the cup a quarter turn on the counter. "That's the only line in the whole thing I'd have signed."

"You've read all of it."

"I read charts for a living. Yours is short." A pause with a corridor in it. "Here's what I know about hold orders. A good one has three things. A name, a reason, and a clock. Yours has three names and a reason I can't argue with. I've seen unequal pupils. I've seen what happens to people who ignore them."

"So you agree with them."

"I agree with the reason. Tell me the clock."

"Until I'm cleared."

"That's not a clock. That's a door with somebody else's hand on it."

"There are criteria."

"Criteria tell you what the door needs. They don't tell you when anyone will come and look. Ask for a review date. There's a line in that record that says you can. Use it once, early, so they know you can read."

He looked at the black water past the breakwater, where the lights cut it. "And if the date comes and I'm still shaking?"

"Then you're still shaking, and you'll know it, and so will I. That's not the same as being left in a drawer." The cup went another quarter turn. "I saved the version from eleven o'clock. If a line moves, I'll see it move."

"You keep versions?"

"Since before you."

There was something in the way she said it that was not the nurse. He did not ask. He put the shape on the pile with the others.

"Tomorrow," she said, "you tell me the thing you'd rather leave out."

"And if there isn't one?"

"There's always one. Tonight's was the four."

He had not told her about the four. She had read it. He looked at his right hand on the rail and found it was steady, for the moment, and that he wanted to tell her that too, and did.

"Eat something."

"Priya Shah brought me a plate."

"The girl who hit you?"

"To wash. She's very reasonable."

"Good," Elena said, and was smiling when the screen went dark.

Back at the table, the sheet was finished. Forty lines, twelve of them wrong the first time. No number changed anywhere. No seat made a sound. At twenty-one hundred the hand bell rang and he wiped his own problem off the board, which turned out to be the last part of the rule.

The message came at twenty-one oh six, one line, the way she wrote them. *Bay One.*

The lights were at half. Mara was on the padded floor in training black with nothing on her wrist and nothing in her hands, running a pattern. Four steps, a turn, a reset. She was counting under her breath, and she did not stop when he came in.

"Stand there. Again. With me."

He was bad at it. The turn came late and the reset came early, and his shoulders rose on the third step the way they had risen for the drone, and she said, "Shoulders," without looking, and he put them down.

"The kitchens have you withdrawn for the year."

"The kitchens are ahead of the Director."

"Are they wrong?" Four steps. Turn.

"About the year." Reset. "Not about the hand."

"So you agree with it."

"I agree with the doctor. I don't agree with the glass case."

Somewhere in the sixth pass she said, in the same voice she used to count, "Last night you told four people in this room that the place where one thing stops being another is somebody's decision."

"I said it about a wall."

"A restriction is a boundary. Whose decision?"

"Sen's. Park's. Hale's." He had the turn now. "I have the names. I have where to appeal each one. My mother wants a date on it."

"Then you know exactly where the line is." She stopped and looked at him. "Most people never find that out. They stand in the middle of a rule and call the whole floor forbidden."

"And you?"

"I read the rule." She started again, and after a beat he went with her. "The first time Shah came at you, you were watching her hands. The last time, you were watching her weight. That was the only thing in that room they did not take from you today. It doesn't show on a gauge, so nobody thought to."

Four steps. Turn. Reset. He got all of it.

"Mara. Why are you doing this?"

She kept her feet moving. "I do this every night at this hour. Ask anyone. It is quieter with two people in the room, and you are the only person in this House who cannot lose rank by being bad at it."

"That's the meanest kind thing anyone's ever said to me."

"It was accurate." She picked her jacket up off the mat. "Shoulders."

The door closed behind her, and he stood in the half-light with the pattern still in his feet, and found he was smiling, and had not planned it.

---

Eli was awake when he got back, cross-legged on his bed with the drone in pieces on a towel, and did not ask where he had been.

Kade lay down with the tablet and read the challenge rules the way he had once read a chamber wall, looking for the place where the drawing stopped matching the thing. It took thirty-three minutes. Ranked challenges needed active-field clearance. Nothing else on Hale's schedule did. Not the footwork. Not the conditioning benchmarks, which were worse than Park had made them sound. Not the unranked simulations, or the match recordings, or grappling with nothing lit, or forty lines of grammar. Park had fenced off one square, the one he had been planning to jump to, and left the whole staircase standing.

He did not enjoy noticing that. He noticed it anyway.

"You've stopped breathing," Eli said, not looking up. "Either you found something or you died."

"Ranked needs clearance. Nothing else does."

"You were busy being an exhibit." Eli fitted a rotor and spun it with a fingertip. "So what do you want?"

"Your model. The one you're not supposed to have."

"I don't have that one. Somebody revoked me." A pause, and then the grin that arrived whenever Eli's pride and Eli's caution came to terms. "I have a different one. Public recordings only. Every first-year bout since arrival, and nothing from any sensor I'm not allowed to read. It's boring and it's mine."

"Put the six frames in it?"

"I could." Eli looked up. "I want to."

"Don't." He said it before he had finished thinking it, and then thought it, and it held. "I can't train the six frames. I can't do them on purpose, and I'm not allowed to do them by accident. Model the other forty-one."

Eli looked at him a long moment, then nodded, and Kade watched him mean it.

The ladder sat in the corner of the tablet where it always sat. 10,482 / 10,482. He waited for it to do what it had done every morning for a week, and it did not. It was a measurement. Measurements moved, or they were not measurements. Somebody had drawn a glass case around one, and a case was only glass.

"They want me last," he said.

"Nobody said last." Eli set the drone down. "They said until."

"Until is a long word from the bottom."

"Then walk up under it."

He took the pencil out of the drawer, and a Meridian duty sheet, and turned it over. Under yesterday's line, *Forty-seven. Wall held. I want to know what I did*, he wrote Hale's conditioning benchmark for the cohort median. Beside it he wrote his own time. Then the gap between them, because the gap was the part that was his.

"That's a big gap," Eli said.

"It's a measurement." He put the pencil down. "They can keep the six frames. I'll take the other forty-one."

They put Priya Shah's first strike on the wall at twenty-three eleven, frame by frame, a whole person behind it. He did not sleep until one thirty-six. The bell over the courtyard did not ring, and had not for fourteen months and eleven days, and at six he got up anyway.

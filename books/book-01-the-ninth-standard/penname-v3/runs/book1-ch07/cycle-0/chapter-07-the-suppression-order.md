# Chapter 7 — The Suppression Order

*Fourteen. The comments found out you're last. They're mad about it. I don't get that. If I was a wall I'd want you last too.*

The band went gray on the bridge, halfway across, with the water going by underneath.

He felt it before he read it, a small cool pulse against the wrist bone, and he stopped walking, because he had been waiting since the covered way last night for Sen to tell him when. The line that had said SUPERVISED ONLY was gone. In its place, three lines in the gray of a form nobody had filled in.

TRAINING STATUS: RESTRICTED. ACTIVE FIELD AUTHORIZATION: SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW. RANKED CHALLENGE AUTHORIZATION: SUSPENDED.

Under them: REPORT TO DIRECTOR SEN. 09:30. LOCATION CHANGED: ASSESSMENT WING, TEACHING ARENA.

Not the office. The floor. She wanted him standing where it had happened, and she had wanted him to read the gray lines first, alone, with nobody watching his face. He stood on the bridge and let his face do what it did, and then he went to breakfast, because Sera would ask whether he had eaten.

Nobody in the Commons said forty-seven. That was how he knew every one of them had watched it.

Anton Bell was the one whose hands could not keep a secret. He had a fork in one and built a box in the air with the other before Kade had sat down. "Fourth-year on the bridge says Hale's pulled your line. The whole year. Medical."

"Review," Nessa Kim said, without looking up from her wrist. "It says review. I can read it from here."

"He said it like a year."

"Who said it like a year?"

Anton consulted the fork. "Somebody."

Taren Holt set a coffee in front of Kade that Kade had not asked for and made a three-minute case for the South Hall roast that had, Kade noticed, no room in it anywhere for a question. Sera ate standing at the door and watched him eat, and when he had done enough of it she nodded once and went.

Mara Vey passed behind his chair on her way out and looked at his right hand where it lay by the tray. He put it in his lap.

"That was an answer," she said, and was gone.

Eli read the band over his shoulder. "Want me to come?"

The no was in Kade's mouth before the question had finished. It had lived there seventeen years and knew its own way out. He held it and made himself wait until it was his.

"No," he said. "I wanted you to ask, though."

Eli nodded as if that distinction were on a syllabus. It was not. It counted anyway.

---

The teaching arena was smaller in daylight and without a hundred people breathing in it. Two people in maintenance gray knelt at the foot of the west wall with a panel open in the floor where the little sensor lights had blinked yesterday, lifting a bracket out with a sound like a dropped spoon. The starting circles were still there. Somebody had scrubbed his scuff off the composite and left Priya's.

Sen stood inside the red strip with her back to the wall, on the exact place. Dr. Park had a tablet. Instructor Hale had his slate and a foam cup.

"Good morning," Sen said. "Stand where you stood."

He did not move. "Anton Bell says I've been withdrawn for the year."

"Anton Bell is a rumor with a fork." She stepped off the place and left it for him. "Stand where you stood. I want to ask you something, and I want you standing there when I ask it."

He walked across the white and put his heel where the strip had been under it, with the wall behind him, closer than he remembered.

"Shah is at the far circle," Sen said. "She loads. She comes. Same as yesterday. Tell me what you do."

He looked down twelve meters of floor at nobody. "I move on the load."

"And the wall?"

"I don't know."

"Could you do it again? Don't try. Tell me whether you know how."

He stood there. The cold thumb at the base of his skull was exactly where it always was and did nothing at all. He had never reached for it in his life; he did not know which direction reaching was.

"No."

"Could you keep it from happening? If she came."

"No."

"No." Sen said it gently, and that was worse than the alternative. "That is the order. I told you supervised last night and I meant it. Dr. Park sat up with this room and changed my mind before six. I wanted you to hear that here, from me, and not from a bridge."

Park turned the tablet around. She did not play the clip. Two numbers sat on the screen and she let him read them. "Measured output from your Conduit across the event. Zero. Load at that footing above the model. Four point eight kilonewtons." She tipped her head at the open panel. "The model has every pulse Shah put into this floor. It does not have you. The wall took a hit it cannot account for."

"So the room's wrong."

"The room is incomplete. I would like it to stay incomplete for a while, because the last time it learned something, you could not find a door."

"One door."

"One door, two pupils, one hand. Something ran a test on your head yesterday, and nobody signed for it. I sign for the next one, or there is no next one."

Sen took the beat that was hers. "I think it can happen again. I do not know what it does to you when it does. That is the whole of what I know, and I am not going to let this island find out by accident."

The next joke went out of his mouth whole, and he stood there without it. At Northline they raised their voices, and a raised voice was a handle. She had not given him one.

Hale put his cup on the rail and held out the slate. "Here's what you get."

It had things on it, which he had not expected. Conditioning, five forty, with the cohort benchmark marked as timed. Standard academics. Tactical theory. Combat observation. Zero-field footwork and grappling. Conduit Theory at eleven. Then the short list underneath, in the gray. No field activity of any kind. No attempt to reproduce the event. No ranked challenges until medical and field clearance.

"No ranked challenges."

"Correct."

"I'm ten thousand four hundred eighty-two."

"We've read the board," Hale said. "It's the one number on this island everyone's agreed on."

"So I stay there."

"Until you're cleared," Sen said.

The heat came late, the way it always did with him, and he had a foot over the red strip before he had decided to leave. "There's a bell over my House that hasn't rung in fourteen months. You're all very good at until."

"Mercer." Hale had not raised his voice either. "Shah had you at three seconds, ten, and nineteen, and none of those was the wall. She had three years. You had a morning. Every one of those is on my list, and my list doesn't need a field. The part we fenced is the only part of you anyone finds interesting. Fine. The rest of you is what keeps getting beaten, and that won't be our doing."

Park stood. "And you're with me. Now."

"I was in medical yesterday."

"Yesterday you couldn't find a door."

---

She did not use the examination room. She walked him down the service corridor under the seats and stopped him at the first door.

"Left and right. Out loud."

"You're testing me with a hallway."

"I'm testing you with the hallway you failed."

Left, one. Right, one. Left, two, with the fire panel. The bend, the pipes going from four to three. "Right, three." He put his palm flat on it as he passed, to make it real. Right, four. Left, three. The keypad door. "Nine."

"Nine," Park said, and wrote nothing, which he understood was the point.

Same room, minus Priya Shah in the chair. The plate leaned three degrees and he stayed. Five. He stayed and swallowed, and she wrote a one for nausea without asking, because she had been watching his throat.

Tablet. Blank page. Stylus. "Your name. Your street. The date in numbers."

The name came out his. The street shook on the four and he went back over it until it looked decided, and she took the tablet before he reached the date.

"Don't fix it. I need the first one." She put it on the wall beside yesterday's, which looked like it had been written on a moving tram. "That's improved. It is not resolved. Symptoms. I write exactly what you say."

"Headache one. Hands are fine."

She typed while he watched. HANDS ARE FINE. Then she turned the screen: the line, a time beside it, a small lock beside the time.

"That's going in like that?"

"You said it. This record keeps what people say in the order they said it. Nobody edits it after. Not Sen. Not me. Not you. If you want something different in there, you say something different, and it goes underneath."

At Northline you found out what had been written about you a year late, from the person using it. Here it was in front of him, and it was wrong, and he had made it wrong.

"Right hand," he said. "Mild. Worse when I'm hiding it."

She typed it under the first line and did not touch the first line. It was a strange thing to be steadied by.

"Who ends this?"

"Three people, and none alone. Sen holds the order. She lifts it when I say your brain has finished doing whatever it is doing, and Hale says you can be stopped on a floor without a negotiation." She scrolled. Under each name, one plain line: request review by someone who did not make the decision. "Most students never read that far."

"What does medical want?"

"A baseline that holds. No lost time. No tremor that comes back on its own."

"Can I have it? All of it?"

"It's yours. The order, the traces, the criteria, and every line anyone writes after today, with a name and a time on it."

He sent a copy to himself. Then, before he could decide whether it was too private, a second copy to an address at St. Vincent Medical.

"Every honest line I give you keeps me here longer."

"Some will. A line you leave out doesn't make you better. It makes the record wrong. The record is the thing that lets you out."

---

It stayed private for about forty-seven minutes. He was aware of the number.

The panel outside Meridian Commons carried duty rotas and whatever the fourth-years found funny. When he came off the bridge it was showing the ladder. One line, his, blown up to the height of a door. MERCER, K. 10,482 / 10,482. Somebody had drawn a neat glass case around it, the kind a museum sets over a thing that must not be handled, and lettered a card beneath. PLEASE DO NOT TOUCH THE EXHIBIT.

It was better than he would have done. It was also true. Nobody could challenge him; he could challenge nobody. The order had built the case. Whoever this was had only drawn what was there.

Eli came down the stairs with the tablet open. "I can put the numbers under it. Four point eight. Six frames. Let them argue with the building's own gauges."

"You don't have the numbers."

"I have Park's sentence and a very good guess."

"Then you'd be putting Park's sentence on a wall." Kade did not look away from the case. "It's my chart."

Eli's thumb stopped above the panel. He looked at the card, and at Kade, and put the tablet under his arm. "Okay."

What went through Kade then was gratitude, and he disliked it. Eli had been ready to fight for him with arithmetic, the only weapon Eli owned, and Kade had asked him to put it down, and he had.

"There's another one," Eli said, lower. "Public recordings only. Every first-year bout since arrival, nothing from any sensor I'm not cleared to read. It's boring and it's mine."

"Are the six frames in it?"

"Not yet." A pause. "I want them."

"Don't." It was out before he had finished thinking it, and then he thought it, and it held. "I can't train the six frames. I can't do them on purpose and I'm not allowed to do them by accident. Model the other forty-one."

Eli looked at him a long moment, and nodded, and Kade watched him mean it.

Two second-years came out of the Commons and slowed the way people slow near an accident.

"Sen's not sitting on him for his sake. She's sitting on him for ours. You saw the wall."

"You saw the floor. He made a Standard Three drill look like a toy and they benched him for it. That's not safety. That's embarrassment."

They went on, having settled it. Both versions were more flattering than the truth, which was a shaking hand and a door.

Taren found them at the foot of the stairs with his band already lit. "The forum named it. THE SUPPRESSION ORDER. Four hundred replies. You're either a weapon they're keeping in a drawer or a mistake they're keeping in a drawer. Nobody in there has said the word pupils."

"How do you know about the pupils?"

"Park said it on the floor. A hundred people heard her." Taren smiled the smile that made rooms easier. "I read faster than most of them."

Kade filed it. The file was getting a shape.

---

Conduit Theory met in a room with a rig where the lectern should be: a lifting platform the size of a door inside a steel frame, the frame on a floor plate, gauges on all three. Professor Amadi did not begin with a sentence. She put the gauges on the wall as a list and told them to choose.

"Every number in this room has an owner. Today the owner is you. When the platform moves you call your gauge, and you answer for it."

Nobody wanted the plate. It never did anything, a girl in the second row said. Kade, whose band was gray and who was not permitted within a meter of the operator's mark, took it. It was the only part of the room he was allowed to touch.

A first-year with Standard Three clearance stepped onto the mark and lifted the platform one meter. Numbers went round. Mass and height from the platform. Strain from the frame.

"Plate."

"Down," Kade said. "By the whole lift. Minus whatever the frame ate."

Amadi turned her head. "What did the frame eat?"

"Whatever it flexed. It's steel. It gave a little. The rest went into the floor, and the floor into the building, and I don't own a gauge for that."

"No. Facilities does." She put a second line up beside the gauges, from the academy's maintenance ledger: the yearly cost of the plate under this rig. "The platform rose one meter. Someone paid for the meter. The Conduit did not pay. It only told us who did."

They spent the hour on it. Paths, limits, losses, owners. He understood perhaps sixty percent and wrote forty, and the margin of his page filled with question marks until it looked like a rash.

Near the end she zeroed the gauges, and to zero them she opened the rig's calibration matrix, a table older than the paint on the frame. Reference profiles from the first generation of Standard Eights, she said. The rig had been built to read against them, and nobody had found a reason to teach it otherwise.

Twelve narrow columns went up the wall. VIII-01 through VIII-12.

He had been counting for an hour, because that was the plate owner's job. He counted the columns twice.

"Who's twelve?"

Amadi did not look up from the zeroing. "Nobody."

"There's a column."

"There are eleven recognized first-generation Standard Eights. The schema is older than the recognition. The slots were laid out before the classification was finished, and nobody has rebuilt a working table to match a plaque."

"So what's in twelve?"

She looked at the matrix then, half a second longer than a professor needs to look at a table she has shown a hundred times. "A reserved identifier. Preclassification. A calibration table is not a census, Mercer. Zero your gauge."

He zeroed it. He also counted again, and it was still twelve, and reserved was not the same word as empty.

Then she put the balance for his plate on the wall in notation and asked him to finish it. He could draw it: boxes, arrows, the meter going up and the load going down. He could not write it. He stood at the wall with the stylus while the room waited.

"That is a picture," Amadi said. "I asked for a sentence." She sent him a foundations sheet, forty lines, due at Study. "You have the physics. You do not have the grammar. The second is faster to learn, and less fun."

Nobody laughed. That made it stranger than Room Four, where he could have decided the sheet was stupid. It was not stupid. He did not know enough, which was a worse problem, and a more solvable one.

---

Dining Close was steam, a sanitizer that shrieked when it reached temperature, and Sera Vale walking the line with a towel on her shoulder and nothing kind to say about anyone's stacking. Eli talked to the dish machine the way he talked to the drone. Kade did trays.

Priya Shah came through the kitchen door at twelve forty with her braid down and a plate she had already eaten from, and handed it to him.

"Lunch. I keep my word. I didn't say who'd be washing."

She leaned on the steel where Sera could not object and told him the floor. Plates densest at center, so a pulse there traveled cleanest and farthest. She had put him at the edge on purpose, where the lattice went soft and his own weight would do most of her work. Anton had been right with his hands.

"I asked Park for the load numbers this morning."

"She won't give you those."

"She didn't. She asked how my shoulder was, which told me the interesting part wasn't my shoulder." She watched him rack the plate. "They fenced you."

"Yes."

"Good."

The tray in his hands went still.

"If they'd cleared you this morning I'd have turned down the rematch, and I want the rematch. Your hands went for an hour. I put people down for a living. I'd like to keep doing it to people who are all there."

She went out. From inside the steam Eli said, "She's very reasonable."

"I know."

"It's hard to build a grievance around."

---

Hale's afternoon was a zero-field mat, and Nessa Kim was already on it, band gone gray for the hour by choice instead of order.

"Kim. Perception, four thousand one hundred eighteen. Mercer. Nobody lights anything." Hale sat with his slate. "Kim. Tell him."

"I'm going to say what I'm about to do before I do it. Every time. Then I'm going to do it."

"That seems generous."

"It isn't. Left ankle."

He moved the left foot back, and she took the right one instead, and the ceiling went past and the mat arrived.

"I said left."

"You moved it. To move it, you put your weight on the right." She did not offer a hand; she was waiting to see whether he would ask. He got up without one. "I don't need the ankle. I need where the weight goes. Telling you the ankle just makes you send it there."

Ten more announcements, ten more visits to the mat. Then she said, "Hip," and he heard the word and did not answer it. He watched her weight instead. It moved onto her front foot, the way Mara's had, the way Priya's had, the way it had to, and he moved on that and not the word, and for five full seconds she had to work. She got him. She changed her grip twice to do it.

"That one," Hale said. "What was different."

"I stopped answering her."

"The rest of the hour you were fighting the sentence. Sentences don't have hips."

Nessa picked up her towel. "You made it to five. Your weight talks. I listen. You can learn to say less."

"How?"

"Slowly." She went through the far doors toward the acceleration lab with the students whose bands were lit.

Hale handed him the slate. "North rail. One line. Something I can use. Not something you can't sense."

The rail looked down on a long floor with a pressure plate at each end. A ten-second window, start when you like inside it, touch the far plate and stop. The clock ran from first movement, not the tone.

Priya took the floor, settled, and was gone, and was standing on the far plate with her head at the height it had started. He watched the second run for her feet, because everyone did. On the third he watched the rest of her, because he had spent forty-seven seconds yesterday looking up at the rest of her, and it was the only view of Priya Shah he had ever really had.

Her ribs dropped. Then she went. Fourth run, the same. A long breath out, the settle at the bottom of it, the launch off the bottom.

He wrote: SHE BREATHES OUT, THEN SHE CHOOSES.

Hale read it over his shoulder. "You can't sense her load."

"I can see her ribs."

Nessa came up the rail with a trace open across her wrist. "The load starts eleven hundredths after the ribs finish dropping. All four runs. She's launching off the end of the exhale."

"So the breath is the tell."

"The breath is the constraint. She has to empty before she loads. Everyone does. She does it the same way every time, and you were lying underneath it."

"Correction," Hale said.

*Don't breathe* was advice for a statue. "Breathe out on the reset. Not on the go. Then she's launching off nothing an opponent can watch."

Hale went down and said four words to Priya. She looked up at the rail with both eyebrows raised, went to the mark, and tried it. Exhale, settle, a beat with her lungs empty, and go. Slower; he could see it without the clock. She overran the far plate by half a step and came back to it.

She came to the bottom of the rail. "Who."

Hale pointed.

"A year of people watching my feet. Nobody watches my ribs."

"I was underneath them."

"You were," she said, and did not seem to like how good the answer was.

Nessa held up the trace. "He couldn't have measured it. I can. He only saw it because you put him on his back and stood over him breathing."

That was the line, and it was Nessa's. He had not found a weakness in Priya Shah. He had noticed one thing she did every time because she had done it while hitting him, from about a meter away.

Priya ran it again, slower again, and clean, and pointed at her own ribs, then at him, then at the floor. He was fairly sure the third gesture was a promise.

He wrote a second line. SLOWER. HARDER TO READ. HER CHOICE.

Hale took the slate, read both lines, and did not say anything about having asked for one.

---

Meridian Study had a rule, and the rule had a board. A fourth-year rang a brass hand bell the size of a teacup from the top of the Commons stairs at nineteen hundred. "Two hours. Stuck goes on the board. One line each, no talking, no exceptions." Every table filled.

Anyone stuck put the problem up. Anyone passing could fix one line, in silence, and walk on. By quarter past there were four problems on it, and three had strangers' handwriting in the margins.

Eli had cables across half a table. Sera was drawing exit routes over a floor plan. Taren sat with three people arguing economics, and won, and let them think it had been close. Mara had the window and nobody near her. Eli pushed a chair out with one foot, not an invitation, just space, and Kade sat in it and fought the foundations sheet, and the sheet won. Line twelve would not balance. Three tries, three different wrong answers.

He had never once in his life put a thing he could not do where people could see it.

At nineteen fifty he put it on the board.

Nobody looked up. That was the rule. Four minutes later Eli walked past on his way to the water, wrote one line in the corner in his small engineer's hand, and kept walking. *Twelve: your transform is upside down.* He sat down and did not meet Kade's eye, because that was also the rule.

Kade turned the transform over. The line balanced, and the twenty-eight after it went in a rush, the way a jammed drawer opens all at once.

---

She called at twenty hundred exactly, and he took it on the bridge, because the Commons had ears and the roof had last night in it.

A nurses' station. A cup that had gone cold hours ago. She had read the record; he could tell, because she did not ask him what had happened.

"Who wrote hands are fine?"

"Me."

"And the line under it?"

"Also me."

"Good." She turned the cup a quarter turn. "That's the only line in the thing I'd have signed. Here's what I know about hold orders. A good one has three things. A name, a reason, and a clock. Yours has three names and a reason I can't argue with. I've seen unequal pupils. I've seen what happens to people who ignore them."

"So you agree with them."

"I agree with the reason. Tell me the clock."

"Until I'm cleared."

"That's not a clock. That's a door with somebody else's hand on it."

"There are criteria."

"Criteria tell you what the door needs. They don't tell you when anyone will come and look. Ask for a review date. There's a line in that record that says you can. Use it once, early, so they know you can read."

He looked at the black water past the breakwater. "And if the date comes and I'm still shaking?"

"Then you're still shaking, and you'll know it, and so will I. That's not the same as being left in a drawer." The cup went another quarter turn. "I saved the version from eleven o'clock. If a line moves, I'll see it move."

"You keep versions?"

"Since before you."

Something in the way she said it was not the nurse. He did not ask. He put the shape on the pile.

"Tomorrow you tell me the thing you'd rather leave out."

"And if there isn't one?"

"There's always one. Tonight's was the four."

He had not told her about the four. She had read it. He looked at his right hand on the rail and found it steady, for the moment, and that he wanted to tell her that too, and did.

"Eat something."

"Priya Shah brought me a plate. To wash. She's very reasonable."

"Good," Elena said, and was smiling when the screen went dark.

Back at the table the sheet finished itself. At twenty-one hundred the hand bell rang and he wiped his own problem off the board, which turned out to be the last part of the rule.

---

The message came a few minutes later, one line, the way she wrote them. *Bay One.*

Lights at half. Mara on the padded floor in training black with nothing on her wrist, running a pattern. Four steps, a turn, a reset. Counting under her breath. She did not stop when he came in.

"Stand there. Again. With me."

He was bad at it. The turn came late and the reset came early, and on the third step his shoulders rose the way they had for the drone, and she said, "Shoulders," without looking, and he put them down.

"The kitchens have you withdrawn for the year."

"The kitchens are ahead of the Director."

"Are they wrong?" Four steps. Turn.

"About the year." Reset. "Not about the hand."

"So you agree with it."

"I agree with the doctor. I don't agree with the glass case."

In the sixth pass she said, in the voice she used to count, "Last night you told four people in this room that the place where one thing stops being another is somebody's decision."

"I said it about a wall."

"A restriction is a boundary. Whose decision?"

"Sen's. Park's. Hale's." He had the turn now. "I have the names. I have where to appeal each one. My mother wants a date on it."

"Then you know exactly where the line is." She stopped and looked at him. "Most people never find that out. They stand in the middle of a rule and call the whole floor forbidden."

"And you?"

"I read the rule." She started again, and after a beat he went with her. "The first time Shah came at you, you were watching her hands. The last time, her weight. That was the one thing in that room they did not take from you today. It doesn't show on a gauge, so nobody thought to."

Four steps. Turn. Reset. He got all of it.

"Mara. Why are you doing this?"

Her feet kept moving. "I do this every night at this hour. It's quieter with two people in the room, and you are the only person in this House who cannot lose rank by being bad at it."

"That's the meanest kind thing anyone's ever said to me."

"It was accurate." She picked her jacket off the mat. "Shoulders."

The door closed behind her, and he stood in the half-light with the pattern still in his feet, and found he was smiling, and had not planned it.

---

The Commons was empty when he came back through. The panel had cycled off the ladder to the duty rota and cycled back, and the case was still there. Somebody had added a second card under the first, in a different hand: SUPPRESSED. Somebody else had struck it through and written SAFE. Neither had signed.

He stood in front of it with the pattern in his feet and read his own number.

They had drawn a case around it, and they had been right to. Nothing that had happened to him today was inside the number. Not nine doors. Not the four, darker on the second pass. Not five seconds of Nessa Kim changing her grip, or Priya Shah overrunning a plate, or twenty-eight lines opening at once because a stranger had written one. None of it showed on a ladder. The ladder was the only thing on this island anyone had thought to put glass around.

He opened Park's record on the band, found the plain line under Sen's name, and used it. Request for review date. He gave the reason the way Elena would have given it. Not a complaint. A clock.

Then he opened Hale's schedule to the first block. Conditioning. Five forty. Cohort benchmark, timed.

He sent Hale one line. *Time me tomorrow. I want a number that's mine.*

The reply came before he reached the stairs. *05:40. Bring the shoes.*

Eli was asleep with the drone in pieces on a towel beside him. Kade set the gray shoes by the door where his feet would find them in the dark, and lay down, and did not check the ladder, because it was going to say what it said.

If they wanted him last, they could have it. Last was one square. Nobody had fenced the stairs.

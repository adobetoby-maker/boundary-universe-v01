# Chapter 4 — Ten Thousand Four Hundred Eighty-Two

*Seven. You're up. I know because you haven't answered, which means you're standing somewhere being early at it.*

Five forty-one. Darius had set an alarm to send that. Kade put the phone face down beside the paper map, pulled the training set off the chair, and left the eleven-dollar shirt hanging where it was. The academy's collar sat higher than he was used to. He kept touching it on the way down eight floors, the way you touch a filled tooth.

Clinic One was open at six with the lights all the way up. A technician in blue scrubs found the vein on the first try and filled four tubes without looking at anything but the tubes.

She pressed a seal over the puncture and only then let her eyes go to the band on his other wrist. "You're the one from the board last night."

"I'm the one who hasn't eaten."

"Fair." She did not apologize, which he preferred, and handed him off to a machine.

By six forty-five he had been weighed, measured, photographed, and asked to stand very still inside three different things that hummed. Somebody put a bar of pressed oats in his hand between the second machine and the third, and he ate it in four bites, and it was the best thing he had tasted in a day.

At six fifty-eight a training aide with no expression at all walked him to an elevator that was not on the map, and the elevator went down for long enough that he stopped thinking of it as going to a basement.

"How far?"

"Forty-two meters."

"Why?"

"Field containment."

The aide watched the door. Forty-two meters of rock between the classrooms and this room, and nobody was going to say whether it was there to keep something in or out.

The doors opened on a corridor that had never been in a broadcast. Dark composite panels. Glass in the walls thick enough to bend the light coming through it. Every few meters a symbol, concentric rings with one diagonal drawn through them, and under it the words RESONANCE TESTING — CONTROLLED ACCESS.

Dr. Lian Park was waiting at the end of it. She was small and silver-haired, and she stood the way people stand when they decided years ago that motion is a cost.

"Shoes," she said.

"Off?"

"Conductive floor. Shirt as well. Thirty sensors, and none of them care about your modesty, Mr. Mercer, and neither do I."

He took the shirt off. The collar had been bothering him anyway.

---

The room was round, twenty meters across, rings painted on the floor in a color nearly the floor's own, glass walls from the waist up. Behind the glass sat a long console with six technicians at it, and behind the technicians, standing with a cup of tea, was Dr. Amara Sen.

He had seen her face more often than most of his teachers'. Flood footage with a river held back in it. One public announcement asking teenagers not to open their own Conduits with kitchen tools. In the flesh she was a woman in a dark jacket, not tall, and she looked at him through the glass the way you look at weather. One of eleven Standard Eights alive and public, watching a boy stand in his socks.

He lifted a hand. She lifted hers.

Thirty contacts went onto his back and chest and the base of his neck, cold, then not. The floor was warm under his feet. He had expected cold.

"Stage one," Park said, through a speaker somewhere above him. "Passive perception. You do nothing. You say so the instant you notice anything you cannot account for with the senses you were born with. Do not decide whether it counts. Deciding is my job."

"And if I notice nothing?"

"Then say nothing, and I will record that you said it."

A section of floor ten meters in front of him slid aside and a metal sphere the size of a head rose out of it on a stalk and hung there. A minute in, it began to vibrate, a hum he felt in his teeth before he heard it, and the lights dimmed by a third.

"Anything?"

"My left foot's cold."

A pause while somebody checked. "Unrelated."

"Then nothing."

The sphere sank. Two blocks of black material rose in a frame with a gap of air between them, and the air in the gap shivered like the road over a hot engine. His ears filled, ordinary pressure, the way they had on the aircraft, and he said so, and Park said, "Ordinary," and recorded it.

Then the room ran its list. Gravity differential. Electromagnetic gradient. Kinetic storage. Thermal transfer. Structural cohesion. Each one arrived, did whatever it did, and left, and the only thing in the room that changed was the temperature of his feet.

He had been in rooms like this his whole life. Not this room; the shape of it. Ten minutes of six adults watching every screen. Then one of them checking another station between trials. Then two heads together at the end of the console, and the small change in a room that has stopped expecting anything. Park did not change. Sen did not move. He had thought that would help more than it did.

On the seventh trial the pressure came back.

The apparatus was a flat plate this time with a faint pattern crawling over it. He was thinking about the pattern, about whether the plate was the thing or the pattern was, and the cold thumb pushed once at the base of his skull, through the glove, exactly as it had in the air.

He had made a deal with a white button and a kitchen. He kept it.

"Something."

The two heads at the end of the console came apart.

"Describe it."

"Base of the skull. Cold. Small. A second, maybe less. Same as—" He stopped. "I had it once yesterday. On the flight. I didn't report it."

"You are reporting it now. Where in the trial?"

"Just now. Right before I said something."

"Is it still there?"

"No."

"Do you believe it was mine?"

He thought about lying in either direction. "I don't know."

"Good. That is the correct answer and the only useful one." A pause. "Trial seven, subject reports a transient sensation, base of skull, onset uncertain. Eight."

The eighth was the seventh again, and the ninth a third time, and nothing, and he said so.

"Stand still," she said. "Control pass."

The door in the glass opened before he could ask. The technician who came through was the one who had kept going back to the same screen after everyone else had stopped, and she set a yellow marker on the fourth ring without looking at him. A clear partition slid down out of the ceiling between them, waist high, thin as a window.

"You are not being tested now," Park said. "The equipment is."

"That's a distinction built to protect the equipment."

"It is a distinction built to protect you from a wrong answer. Stand still."

The technician opened a narrow case. Six ceramic tiles, each smaller than his palm, laid in a line on the warm floor. She stepped back and touched two fingers to the silver plate at her wrist, and the first tile rose.

No sound. Nothing under it. It lifted the height of a hand and stayed, and the second followed, and the third, and on the far side of the glass six lines on six screens bent upward together in the same clean curve.

"Report anything past what your eyes give you."

He gave it everything he had. He waited for the thumb, for heat, for the sense of a line through the air between her wrist and the tile. He could see her tendons stand up along the back of her hand and the pale strip on her Conduit brighten. When she turned her wrist, three tiles slid apart and three held, a fan opening across the ring, and one scraped when it settled too near the floor, and he heard that, because it was sound.

Between her hand and the tile there was nothing at all. The effect was there, the cause was missing, and every screen behind the glass had watched the cause go by.

"Nothing," he said.

The lights went out.

For two seconds the only light in the room was the strip on the technician's wrist and the green lines behind the glass. A tile clicked against another, somewhere to his left and rising.

"Now?"

"Two moved. Three." He pointed at the click. "That's my ears."

The lights came up. All six tiles hung in a column on the far side of the partition, evenly spaced. The technician brought them down and packed them, and she did not look bored now, which did not help. On her screen, as she passed, a line read REFERENCE OPERATOR — FORCE TRANSFER — WITHIN TOLERANCE.

Not a student throwing a vehicle. Not a girl walking down a tower. A woman moving six pieces of ceramic a few meters under perfect control, every instrument in the room seeing all of it, and him seeing what was left over.

The partition rose.

"The control pass matters," Park said. "A null result from a broken sensor is noise. A null result from a working sensor is a fact."

He looked at the floor where the tiles had floated. "So we've proved I'm definitely not doing anything."

"We have proved the room can tell absence from failure. Those are different sentences."

"Yours is longer."

"Mine is the true one." She waited until he looked up at the glass. "The true one is not always the comfortable one, Mr. Mercer. I would rather hand you the first."

He nearly said he had not come for comfort. It would have been a lie, and she seemed unusually well equipped to measure those.

---

Stage two came on a slate, in the anteroom, with his shirt back on.

CONSENT TO ACTIVE OUTPUT TRIAL. Under the academy's paragraph, nothing of his mother's; her page had been clipped to the first consent, and this was a new one. *Further evaluation stages require separate consent.* Yesterday that line had been a door made narrower. This morning he was standing on the far side of it.

He read the page against the photograph on his phone, side by side. The name at the top had not changed. BASELINE EVALUATION, stage two of three. It asked him to attempt a field effect on request, with a stop available to him at any time and to the supervising physician. It put nothing in him. It asked him to put something out.

She had said *call before you agree to the name.* It was the same name. He called anyway.

She picked up on the second ring, with the echo of a parking structure behind her.

"You're off shift."

"I'm in the car. I haven't started it. Talk."

He read her the page, small type included. She did not interrupt. When he finished, she asked two questions, and they were nurse's questions.

"Who stops it."

"Me. Or the doctor. Either one, any time."

"Does it put anything into you."

"No. It asks me to put something out. I don't have anything to put."

A breath. "Then that one's yours. Sign it or don't."

"That's it?"

"You called. That was the condition. I didn't say I'd decide." A seatbelt clicked. "Twenty hundred. I'm going to sleep now, and you're going to go find out."

He signed.

The table rose out of the floor to the height of his waist with a steel puck on it, one kilogram, the weight stamped on the top. "Move it," Park said. "However feels natural. I am not grading style."

He looked at the puck. He concentrated, whatever that meant. He imagined pushing it, and it sliding, and the friction under it, and the table taking the weight and the floor taking the table. He put his hands flat on his thighs so nobody could say he had touched it.

Nothing.

He thought about the examination. The sphere in its chamber, the day the number had come up zero. The moment he had stopped treating the chamber's wall as the edge of anything and had let the floor under it, and the ground under the floor, into the problem. A line drawn through things that were supposed to be whole.

The cold thumb touched the base of his skull.

The puck moved.

Half a centimeter, toward him, with a small scrape. His heart went up into his throat, and he had the words *there, that* ready, and then the door in the glass opened behind him and a technician came through with a fresh tablet, and the air in the room shifted with the door, and the puck sat there half a centimeter from where it had been.

"Environmental artifact," Park said. "Door. Note the time, please. Subject reports?"

He made himself say it. "Same thing. Base of the skull. Just before."

"Noted. The puck moved with the pressure change. Your sensation is recorded separately. Reset."

They reset. Nothing. They changed the object. Nothing. A training bead made for people who had never made anything respond lay in his palm being a bead. A ribbon in a frame so fine the technicians walked around it went on hanging.

By nine thirty his output was zero. Not the three digits from the examination. A number.

He sat on the warm floor. Park came through the door with a bottle of water and, after a moment, sat down across from him, which he had not expected.

"You are dehydrated."

"I'm fascinating."

"You are mildly dehydrated."

He drank. "You expected more," she said.

"No."

She waited, and it was a good wait, one that was not trying to make him fill it, so he did.

"I expected something."

"That is different."

"Does Zero Protocol ever pick somebody with nothing?"

Her face did a very small thing. "Above my clearance."

"You run the test."

"I run today's test."

"What happened thirty-one years ago?"

She looked at him for a while. "I know that your questions have stopped being random."

"That isn't an answer."

"No." She stood in one motion, without her hands. "Stage three. Stress. Eat first. You will need it."

---

Breakfast was in a small room with a window onto the chamber: eggs, toast, fruit, a bowl of oatmeal that could have served as ballast. He ate it all. The one other person at the table, a boy his own age with a candidate band, looked at the blue lanyard and then at his face.

"You're him."

Kade pointed at the oatmeal. "I'm eating."

"Fair." The boy held up his wrist, where a slim silver plate curved along the bone. "Amplification. Family caught it when I was fourteen. Three years in a feeder program. Still doing baseline, because half the people who arrive knowing are wrong about what they know. Preliminary, I'm four seventy-two. Don't make the face."

Kade had made the face. "Out of ten thousand. And you're going to tell me rankings don't matter."

"For a month, they're useless. Last year a kid baselined at thirty-seven and finished eight hundred. Another one started past nine thousand and finished two hundred." The boy shrugged. "They're numbers with time on them. The number's not the thing. The time is."

Kade looked through the glass at the chamber, the puck gone, the rings on the floor waiting. *Numbers with time on them.* He put that somewhere he could reach it.

---

Stage three put him on a platform that moved, and then the room took him apart.

Light from six directions. Sound that was not quite words. The platform tilted without warning and a padded arm swung at shoulder height, and he ducked it, and a tool fell from nowhere and he caught it left-handed because his hand was there.

He was better at this, and he knew why, and it was not Resonance. A classroom asked him to hold still inside somebody else's frame. This was a problem that kept changing shape, and those had always made more sense to him than problems that sat there.

The room rebuilt itself. Three corridors, projected, dividing the floor. Smoke rolled low across all three. At the far end an orange case flashed under a countdown that read forty-three seconds.

"Retrieve the case. Return to the center mark."

"Rules?"

"The environment supplies consequences."

"That isn't a rule."

"Forty seconds."

Left was shortest, and its floor pulsed red in a pattern. Middle looked clear. Right had two barriers sliding across it and no exit he could see. He took the middle, and three steps in the floor dropped out from under his right foot. He grabbed the wall, and the wall was light, and his knee hit the platform hard enough to send a bright line up his thigh.

ROUTE FAILURE — FIVE SECOND PENALTY, the wall said, in red.

"The environment is smug."

"Thirty-one seconds."

He backed out. He did not force it. He stopped reading the red on the left as a warning and read it as a description, and it described a load, three panels wide, traveling from the outside wall toward the center and back. Predictable. He stepped onto the first panel after the pulse passed, and the next pulse started before he reached the gap, and he jumped sideways onto the base of the projected wall, and the base was real, a lip of actual floor, even if the wall above it was not. The pulse went under him. He dropped behind it and ran.

Nineteen seconds. The case sat on a pedestal. He took the handle and pulled, and the case did not move.

LOAD: THIRTY-EIGHT KILOGRAMS.

"You could have mentioned that."

"The environment did."

Small print under the countdown: ASSISTED EXTRACTION POINT — CORRIDOR C. The right corridor. He could drag thirty-eight kilos back across the red floor. Maybe. The clock would beat him. The two barriers on the right passed each other every four seconds, and between them, for less than a second, the corridor opened.

He pulled the case off the pedestal and let its weight take it to the floor, and wheels folded down out of its base, because that had been in the environment too. He ran it toward the first barrier and slowed instead of chasing the first gap.

The barrier closed. Eight seconds.

Every part of him wanted to go, because the time was going. He stood there. The next opening came and he let it go too, because the second barrier would have shut between him and the case. At four seconds both panels drew apart at once, and he shoved the case through, and went after it, and caught the handle as it rolled onto the center ring.

TIME EXPIRED, in red. Then, under it, in green: OBJECTIVE COMPLETE — LOAD SECURE.

He lay on his back beside the case and breathed. "That's inconsistent."

"The clock measured the ideal response. The objective measured the acceptable one."

"So I lost correctly."

"You rejected two unsafe openings under time pressure. The panel calls that judgment."

He sat up. Nobody at Northline had ever put *waiting* on a scoreboard. Waiting was refusing, or failing, or making an adult say the question twice. Here it had a line, and the line was green.

The case sank into the floor. Before he was standing, the wall offered him three lit routes to a target above the far wall, and he took none of them and went up the maintenance rail bolted to the platform's edge. UNAUTHORIZED PATH, in red. Then green under it.

"Do not look so pleased."

"The machine and I disagree about who's in charge."

"Shocking."

The numbers went by on the glass while he caught his breath. Reaction, seventy-third percentile. Then eighty-first. Adaptive route selection, ninety-sixth. Behind the console, heads came up. He hated how much he liked that.

Then the field trials came back in between, and the number that came back with them was zero, every time. Every time the room found him quick, or unusual, or good under pressure, the next line under it was a zero. It began to feel almost designed.

---

At eleven forty-seven Dr. Sen came into the chamber, and everyone else left. He noticed that. He was sitting on the edge of the platform in his shirt again with his knee stiffening, and she carried nothing at all. No tablet. The tea was gone.

"You look disappointed."

"People keep saying so."

"Are they wrong?"

"Yes."

"Good. Then neither of us has to do the reassurance." She walked to the center ring and stood on it. "Do you know why rankings exist?"

"To keep teenagers emotionally stable."

"Obviously." He blinked, and she went on before he could decide whether that had been a joke. "They make performance legible. Students read them. Coaches read them. Sponsors. Governments especially."

"Your baseline is being entered now."

"I'm not enrolled."

"Provisional students are ranked."

"Why?"

"Because everyone is."

"That's a terrible reason."

"It is also the true one."

The wall behind her lit. His name, and under it lines resolving one at a time, as if somebody were reading them aloud.

MERCER, KADE A.
YEAR ONE — PROVISIONAL.
PERCEPTION: UNCONFIRMED.
OUTPUT: ZERO POINT ZERO ZERO.
CONTROL: UNRATED.
COMBAT: UNRATED.

A pause, while the last line thought about it.

FIRST-YEAR RANK: 10,482 OF 10,482.

He waited for it to hurt. It arrived on schedule. He had come through the most selective school on the planet by a classified back door and had proved, inside five hours, that he was the least capable person in it.

"Efficient," he said.

"You are last," Sen said. "Last is useful."

"People in first say that."

"You cannot fall."

She might have meant it as comfort. It landed as something else.

His phone began to buzz on the bench beside him. Then again. Then it did not stop.

PUBLIC LADDER UPDATED, the wall said.

"What did you do?"

"Nothing at all."

*Eight. YOU ARE ACTUALLY LAST.*

*Nine. I'm not even going to make fun of you. Somebody already did it with math.*

Then a number he did not know. ZERO EQUALS 10,482. WELCOME TO ASTERION. Then a link to a campus forum with a thread already at the top of it: ZERO MERCER — CLASSIFIED PRODIGY OR ADMINISTRATIVE ERROR?

He shut his eyes.

"Friend?" Sen said.

"Formerly."

At Northline everyone had a file, and nobody could pretend theirs made them better. Here, ten thousand four hundred eighty-one people had a number that proved they were ahead of him, on a wall in every building. The old reflex came up fast, *doesn't matter,* the laugh that turned a thing into a joke before it could become real. He had it in his mouth.

He looked at the wall instead. Ten thousand four hundred eighty-two. He thought of Ms. Alvarez saying *try.* He thought of his mother at a red light, telling him he had tried to hand a real chance back before it could look at him, and that he was bad at that lie.

"What," he said, because Sen was watching him.

"I was waiting."

"For what."

"To see whether you would leave."

That was close enough to something true that he had to decide how to breathe. "You think I will."

"I think you have a history of leaving things before they can judge you."

"You read my file."

"I read every admitted student's file."

"I'm not admitted."

She picked something invisible off her sleeve. "Keep telling yourself that."

He stood, not to go anywhere. Sitting had started to feel like being observed. "What do you think Zero means?"

Her face went still. "Today?"

"What does that mean?"

"It means that today your measured output is zero, your perception is unconfirmed, and your adaptive cognition is unusual enough to justify further evaluation."

"That was careful."

"Yes."

"You know more than that."

"Yes."

He had not expected her to say it. "Will you tell me?"

"Not yet."

"Why not?"

"Because an incomplete explanation has a way of turning into an instruction."

"That's what people say before they do something suspicious."

"Probably." She walked toward the door in the glass. He let her get almost to it.

"Director. What happens next?"

"Tomorrow you move into House Meridian."

"I thought Houses came after testing."

"They do."

"Why Meridian?"

"Because its faculty lead volunteered."

"For last place?"

"For you."

That was worse. She had the door open. "And Mercer. Do not confuse being unusual with being important."

"Comforting."

"Good."

---

Park came in with his shoes in one hand and a thin glass tablet in the other and set both beside him.

It was his profile again, and it was not. Every trial had a row. Time, confidence, equipment status, the initials of whoever had verified it. Nine passive trials. Six active. One control pass. Zero was not a hole in a wall anymore. It was a column of small finished things, each one signed.

"You have ten minutes to review before certification."

"You let people argue with it?"

"You may challenge procedure, equipment, interpretation, or identity. Not liking the number is not a category."

"Your system lacks imagination."

"Your system has nine minutes."

He opened the adaptive metrics first, and knew what that said about him. Route selection, ninety-sixth percentile. Hazard discrimination, ninety-first. Recovery after false cue, eighty-eighth. His time had been ordinary. His objective score was high. His compliance score had grown a small red triangle, and he knew exactly which rail had put it there.

"Why doesn't any of this move the rank?"

"It does."

"I'm last."

"You have no rated control or combat result, and the first-week ladder weights demonstrated Resonant function heavily. Adaptive metrics break ties and inform placement. They cannot stand in for categories you have not performed."

He found the formula. It was there, which annoyed him more than if it had been hidden. The weighting was brutal and it was not broken. His best column could not carry five empty ones. Every one of the people above him had produced something the room could measure, and he had produced a scrape from a door.

"So it's accurate."

"Within the model being used."

He looked up. "That's a careful way to say something."

"It was a precise one."

He went back to the passive trials. Trial seven had a flag on it.

PHYSIOLOGICAL VARIANCE: ZERO POINT SEVEN PERCENT.
FIELD CORRELATION: BELOW REPORTING THRESHOLD.

"That one." He touched the base of his skull. "That's when I had the pressure. You called the trial negative."

"You reported a sensation you could not attribute. The trial asked for non-ordinary perception. The recorded answer is correct."

"But the variance is there. With the field."

"It is also here." She expanded the row. Two smaller bumps, one before the trial began, one while the apparatus was resetting, with no field in the room at all. "And here."

Not proof. Not nothing, either. Two things that had happened together twice and apart twice.

"Can I challenge interpretation?"

"On what basis?"

He read the three events again. "I don't know yet."

She nodded once. "That is not a basis." She did not sound pleased about it, which was something.

He closed the challenge form without submitting it.

The certification page had two lines at the bottom. ACKNOWLEDGE RESULT. DECLINE CERTIFICATION AND REPEAT BASELINE.

"If I repeat?"

"Tomorrow's result replaces today's only if a procedural fault is established. Otherwise both stand in the record."

"And Meridian?"

"House placement is independent of whether you enjoy your rank."

He looked at the second line for a while. Another morning in the round room, another zero, and a day spent on the far side of a door with the number not yet his. A way to not be judged for one more day. He knew the shape of that. He had been doing it in different clothes since he was nine.

The number hurt because it was public. It hurt more because, for today, in this model, it was right.

He put his thumb on the glass.

RESULT ACKNOWLEDGED — NOT A WAIVER OF APPEAL.

Park took the tablet back. "Most candidates do not read that far."

"Most candidates have better numbers."

"Higher numbers. Words are equipment, Mr. Mercer. Poor calibration causes accidents." She had the door open when he said her name, and she turned.

"Did the Director tell you to keep testing after the first result?"

"The Director authorized a full baseline."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No." She looked through the glass at the chamber, empty now except for the table and the puck. "I kept testing because a result is only useful once you know where it stops being true. That is my reason. It is the only one I am able to give you."

She went out. He stayed under his rank for a minute, and then two. Then he took a picture of the wall and sent it.

*Enjoy it while you can.*

*IS THAT A THREAT.*

The shame was there. So was the anger. So was the small, deeply inconvenient thing he had felt yesterday looking down at the island from the air, and he did not make any of them cancel the others. He let all three stand.

*No,* he typed. *It's a plan.*

---

The plan got harder at lunch, because the number followed him upstairs.

The arrival residence dining hall ran the first-year ladder on a strip above the food stations, and most of the room ignored it with the fierce concentration of people who knew exactly where their own names were. He came in with a tray. The strip refreshed. 10,482 — MERCER, KADE — PROVISIONAL, and the room went thin, the way it had last night, conversation losing its middle.

Imani Bell waved from a table by the window. "Last place," she said, at a volume that turned three heads.

He walked over. "Medical review."

"Sit. You're famous. Congratulations on having the easiest rank in the building to remember." Her own preliminary showed on her wrist. Six thousand seven hundred seventy-one. "Don't say it."

"You did better than me."

"I have functional Resonance." She looked offended on her own behalf. "It moves one paper clip. They let the cardiologist watch, and it didn't argue with anything, and I'm cleared for low-output work. Next week I do it again with a real load. So I'm not going home. For now."

The girl with the stylus set her tray down and, before she sat, turned her slate around. Beneath the lowest of her seven bands the pending line had been filled in. MERCER — 000 — 10,482 — OUTPUT ZERO — ADAPTIVE HIGH. She had underlined the last two words.

"That's what they said, and that's what I saw. They don't match. I said I'd know something if they didn't."

"What do you know?"

"That the column isn't finished." She sat down and started eating.

A boy at the next table leaned over. "Are you actually Zero?"

Kade took a bite. "No. I'm Kade."

The boy blinked and went back to his table. Imani laughed with her mouth full. Nobody at this table cared that she was six thousand seven hundred seventy-one. They cared whether a device in her chest would let her do the thing she had spent years learning. The strip hung over the food, and it was everywhere, and it was not everything, and he ate his lunch under it.

---

After lunch every baseline candidate sat in a lecture hall built around a screen three stories tall, and Kade took a seat beside Imani at the back and found that his name up there had gone from gray to white.

"You acknowledged it," said the Amplification candidate, turning around from the row ahead. "That's what white means."

"Everyone can see that?"

"You thought the public ladder was private?" Imani said.

"I was hoping for bad design."

The man on the stage wore a green sash with LADDER OFFICE along one edge and the posture of somebody assigned to explain numbers to ten thousand ambitious teenagers every autumn.

"Your first rank is a baseline, not a prophecy." Around the hall, hands went to slates. "It determines initial training bands. It does not determine class access. It may affect challenge eligibility after orientation. It does not determine housing, meals, medical care, equipment safety standards, or the value of your continued existence, despite last year's forum."

"What did the forum say?" the Amplification candidate asked, without raising his hand.

"It was wrong."

The hall laughed. Kade laughed, and caught himself looking at the white name.

Recalculation had windows. Full baseline at enrollment. Weekly updates once rated instruction began. Immediate changes only after a sanctioned challenge or a corrected procedural error. Older performance decayed in weight as newer evidence replaced it. *Numbers with time on them.* It was not a philosophy. It was how the thing was built.

A sample record went up: a student ranked twelve hundred overall and below eight thousand in raw output. "Every rank compresses unlike categories. Output, control, perception, tactical judgment, safety, recovery, rated application. The number makes comparison possible. It does not make comparison complete."

"Paper clip empire," Imani whispered.

"Your reign approaches."

A girl near the front asked whether family-sponsored training hours were adjusted for. "Recorded," the man said. "Not adjusted." Her mouth tightened. Somebody else asked why students with medical restrictions appeared on the ladder beside unrestricted ones.

"Because a restriction describes permitted exposure, not ability. Safety limits are visible to instructors and challenge officials. They are not public medical labels."

Imani stopped joking. Her fingers closed over the sleeve that covered her wrist, and Kade understood, watching them, why she had told a stranger in the terminal about her heart and why she might not want ten thousand strangers told for her. The screen could show a score without owning the reason under it.

The Amplification candidate asked what happened when someone had a category the ladder did not recognize, and the man on the stage looked, not at him, but at the back of the hall, and half the room followed his eyes.

"The academy evaluates demonstrated effects. A category is not created for a claim."

Kade put his hand up. The man looked resigned. "Mr. Mercer."

"What if the effect is real and the model is wrong?"

The hall went quiet enough that he heard a stylus hit the floor three rows down.

"Then evidence must show where the model fails."

"And until then?"

"Until then, the model reports what it can support."

Not fair. Not unfair. A condition. The system had made a claim about today. If he wanted it to make a different one, anger was not evidence. He would have to produce something the room could not mistake for a door moving a puck.

They were dismissed. Nobody left without one look at the screen, including the ones who checked their messages while facing it. Imani stayed in her seat.

"You asked my question."

"I thought it was mine."

"It can be both." She stood. "Tomorrow, Meridian."

"How do you know my House?"

"Also public."

"This place is hostile to mystery."

His certified rank stayed on the wall above them. He walked out under it instead of waiting for the screen to change.

---

At twenty hundred exactly he called from the top of the exterior stair, with the ladder scrolling across the courtyard, and she answered from the kitchen table with the silver folder in front of her and sleep still in her face.

"How bad."

"Why bad?"

"You called on the second. You only do that when it's bad."

He turned the phone around so she could see the panel across the way, and held it there long enough for her to find the bottom.

"Oh."

"That reaction lacked support."

"You are very last."

"I'm aware."

Her mouth pressed flat. "Are you laughing?"

"No."

"You are."

"I love you."

"That's unrelated." She laughed then, and he tried to be offended, and it lasted five seconds, and then he was laughing too, at the top of a metal stair on an island in the Pacific with his name at the bottom of a wall.

When it passed, he gave her the other thing. The pressure at the base of the skull. Once in the air yesterday, he told her, and he had not said, and he told her why, and she listened to that without a word. Twice today in a sealed room with thirty sensors on him. Zero point seven percent physiological variance, below reporting threshold, present twice with a field and twice with none. A doctor's number beside it, the way he had promised himself he would deliver it.

"You reported it."

"Both times. In the room. I said I would."

"You said you would to a button."

"I said it to you, too. You just weren't there."

She was quiet for a moment, and he could see her file it the way she filed a patient's line, not resolved, not dismissed, written down. "Does it hurt?"

"No. It's cold. It's over before I can look at it."

"If it starts to hurt, that's a different sentence. You call that one in."

"That's a deal."

Then she asked the question, and there was nothing hidden in it. No test. No disappointment folded up inside it. "Do you want to come home?"

He looked out past the phone. Students crossing the bridge below with their bags. An arena down the southern arm with its roof open, light coming out. Meridian's courtyard under him with its bell, where his band would open the gate tomorrow. The panel across the way with his name at the foot of it, last, in white.

"No," he said.

It came so fast it surprised him. She studied his face for a second on her small screen.

"Okay."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"You're not going to say you told me so?"

"I'm saving it for a more irritating moment."

"Good parenting."

"Thank you."

He looked back at the number after she was gone. Last place was public, and humiliating, and unfair in at least four ways he could already name, and for today it was also right. Tomorrow he was moving into House Meridian, and he had no idea what that meant.

He wanted to find out. He would settle for that as a plan.

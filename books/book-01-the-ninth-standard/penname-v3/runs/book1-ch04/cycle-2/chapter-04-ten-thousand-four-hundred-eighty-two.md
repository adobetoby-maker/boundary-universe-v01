# Chapter 4 — Ten Thousand Four Hundred Eighty-Two

*Seven. You're awake. You're early. You've already found something in that building to be suspicious of.*

Five forty-one. Darius had set an alarm to send it. Kade put the phone face down on the paper map and took the training set off the chair. The eleven-dollar shirt he left where it hung. The academy's collar sat higher on his neck than any shirt he owned, and he kept finding it with his fingers on the way down eight floors, the way a tongue finds a filled tooth.

Clinic One took his blood at six fifteen: four tubes, a seal over the puncture, a technician who looked at the tubes and not at him until the last was capped, and then at his band, and said nothing. He preferred it to the other thing. By six forty-five he had been weighed, measured, photographed, and asked to stand inside three machines that hummed, and somebody had put a bar of pressed oats in his hand between the second and the third. He ate it in four bites.

At six fifty-eight an aide in gray walked him to an elevator that was not on his map, and the elevator went down long enough that he stopped thinking of the bottom as a basement.

"Forty-two meters," the aide said, before he asked. "Containment. The rock is part of the instrument."

"For keeping what where?"

"Yes."

The doors opened on a corridor no broadcast had ever shown: dark panels, glass thick enough to bend the light coming through it, and every few meters the same symbol, rings inside rings with one line drawn through them. RESONANCE TESTING — CONTROLLED ACCESS.

Dr. Lian Park waited at the end of it. She was small and silver-haired and stood as if she had decided years ago that movement was a cost and she would pay it only on purpose.

"Shoes off. Shirt off. Conductive floor, thirty sensors, and none of them can be argued with." She was already turning. "You will be asked to do three things today, Mr. Mercer. Notice. Produce. Cope. In that order, because it is the order in which people fail."

---

The room was round and twenty meters across, with rings on the floor in a color so near the floor's own that he found them with his feet before his eyes. Glass from the waist up. Behind the glass a console with six people at it, and behind them, with a cup in her hand and no chair, Dr. Amara Sen.

He knew the face. Everybody who had ever watched an Asterion broadcast on a cracked gym screen knew it: the Director, at the edge of the frame, behind whoever was holding the trophy, never the one talking. She was shorter than the footage. She looked at him through the glass the way a person looks at a sky they intend to work under, and he lifted a hand because it was the only thing he could think of, and she lifted hers.

Thirty contacts, cold and then not. The floor was warm. He had braced for cold.

"Stage one," Park said, from somewhere above him. "Nine intervals of one minute each. Some contain an active field. Some contain nothing. You are not told which. When you notice anything you cannot account for with the senses you were born with, say so at once, and do not wait to be sure. Certainty is my department."

"And if I call one wrong?"

"You cannot call one wrong. You can only report or fail to report. Interval one."

Nothing rose out of the floor. Nothing hummed. A tone sounded, and a minute of the room happened to him, and the tone sounded again, and Park said, "Two."

It was the worst design he could have imagined, and inside the first minute he understood it was the correct one. A sphere on a stalk would have told him where to look. This gave him nothing but the inside of his own head, where he had always been least reliable.

He listened. His left foot went cold, which he did not report, and his ears filled and cleared, which he did, and Park said, "Pressure. Ordinary. Recorded as reported," and did not say whether the interval had contained anything. Three. Four. In five, a technician behind the glass leaned back and rubbed her eyes, and in six one of them went to check another station, and he knew the room's weather from a hundred classrooms: adults expecting something, then adjusting, then finding the clock. Park did not look at anything but him.

Seven.

Twenty seconds in, the cold thumb pushed once at the base of his skull, through the glove, exactly as it had in the air.

He had made a deal in a kitchen and a second one with a white button, and this was what both of them were for.

"Something."

"Describe."

"Base of the skull. Cold. About a second." He made himself finish. "I had it once yesterday. On the flight. I didn't press the panel."

"You are pressing it now. Is it present?"

"No."

"Do you believe it was mine?"

He wanted to say yes so badly that it had a taste. "I don't know."

"Correct. It is the only honest answer available to you, and I will not accept a better one. Eight."

Eight was a minute. Nine was a minute. The last tone sounded, and Park did not tell him what had been in any of them.

"How many were live?"

"You will see the sequence at certification. If I show you now, your memory will helpfully rebuild the morning around it, and I will have spent nine minutes measuring your imagination."

---

"Control pass," Park said. "You are not being tested. The room is. Stand behind the line."

A partition came down out of the ceiling, waist high, thin as a window, and the door in the glass let through the technician who had rubbed her eyes in interval five. She set a yellow marker on the fourth ring, walked to the center where he had stood, and turned her back to the console.

"Same protocol," Park said. "Different sequence. She has not seen it either."

The tones began. In the second interval the technician said "On," flatly, before the light on the console had finished changing, and Park said, "Confirmed." Off. On. She was never late. Twice she said "Off" into a silence Kade had already stopped attending to, and twice a line behind the glass agreed with her. Nine for nine, and she had not once looked as if she were trying.

"Now produce."

The technician put two fingers on the silver plate at her wrist and looked at the yellow marker. The pale strip along her Conduit brightened. The tendons stood up along the back of her hand. The marker rose off the fourth ring the height of a hand, crossed to the third, and set itself down without a sound, and on the far side of the glass six lines climbed and settled in the same shape.

"Mr. Mercer. Tell me when it is about to move."

She did it again. He watched her hand, her eyes, the marker, the strip at her wrist. He said "Now" when it lifted, which was too late, and "Now" when it settled, which was worse. The third time she said "Now" herself, in a bored voice, and then the marker moved, and the console had drawn the start of the curve before her mouth closed.

Between her wrist and the marker there was nothing at all. He could see the woman and what she did. The instruments could see how. He had been given the same nine minutes she had, and she had come out with nine answers, and he had come out with a cold spot on his neck.

The console read FORCE TRANSFER — REFERENCE — NOMINAL. She packed the marker and went out. She was not bored now. It did not help.

"So the sensors work."

"So a blank from this room is a fact and not a fault. You are entitled to hate the fact. You are not entitled to hate it as a fault."

---

Stage two came on a slate in the anteroom with his shirt back on.

CONSENT TO ACTIVE OUTPUT AND ADAPTIVE STRESS TRIALS. His mother's chart page was not clipped to this one; her page had been clipped to the baseline consent, and this was a new door. *Further evaluation stages require separate consent.* Yesterday that sentence had been a wall he liked. This morning he was being asked to open it himself.

He read it against the photograph on his phone, the way she had told him to. The stop belonged to him and to the physician, either one, any time. Nothing would be put into him. He would be asked to put something out, and then to solve problems on a moving floor while the room made noise at him. He had said he would call before he agreed to anything with a new name. This had a new name.

She answered on the second ring with a parking structure's echo behind her.

"You're off shift."

"I'm in the car. I haven't started it. Read it to me."

He read it, small type included. She let him finish.

"Who stops it."

"Me or the doctor."

"Anything go into you?"

"No. It's the other direction."

A seatbelt clicked. "Then that page is yours. I said to call. I didn't say I'd sign."

"You could tell me what to do."

"I could. You'd do the opposite to prove you could, and then I'd have signed it for you after all." The engine started. "Twenty hundred. Go find out."

He signed.

---

The table rose out of the floor with a steel puck on it, one kilogram, the weight stamped into the top.

"Move it," Park said. "By any means that is not your hands. I do not grade technique. I record whether the puck's position changes and whether the room can find a reason."

He tried. Whatever concentrating was, he did it. He imagined the puck sliding and the friction under it and the table taking its weight and the floor taking the table, and then he stopped imagining and simply wanted it, hard, the way you want a door to be unlocked. The puck sat.

They gave him a bead made for first-timers, a glass thing that was supposed to warm in the hand of anyone who could do anything at all. It stayed the temperature of his hand. They lowered a thread in a sealed box, fine enough that the technicians walked wide of it, and the thread hung.

They put the puck back. He went to the examination in his head, the sphere in its chamber, the day he had stopped treating the chamber wall as the edge of anything and let the floor and the ground under the floor into the problem. He held it there and tried to want the puck the way he had wanted that.

The puck moved.

Half a centimeter toward him, with a scrape he felt in his teeth. His whole chest went up. He had the words ready, and he looked at the glass, and nobody behind it had moved at all.

"Ventilation cycle," Park said. "The room breathes every eleven minutes. The puck breathed with it. Note the time. Did you feel anything?"

He checked, honestly, because she would know. "No."

"Recorded. The one time it moved, you felt nothing. The one time you felt something, nothing moved. Both are on the record. Both are true. Reset."

By nine thirty his output was a number, not the three digits of the examination. A zero, with two more zeros after a point.

He sat down on the warm floor, because standing had stopped meaning anything. Park came through the door with a bottle of water and, after a moment, sat down across from him, which he had not expected of someone who paid for motion on purpose.

"You are dehydrated."

"I've been told I'm a lot of things today."

"Today you are dehydrated. Drink." She waited while he did. It was a good wait, the kind that did not want filling, so he filled it.

"Does this protocol ever pick somebody with nothing in them?"

The change in her face was so small it could have been the light. "That question is older than my clearance."

"You run the test."

"I run this morning's. The last time this protocol ran, I was in school." She stood in one motion, without using her hands. "Eat. Stage three uses your legs, and I would like them to belong to you."

---

Breakfast was in a room with a window onto the chamber, and it was real: eggs, toast, fruit, and oatmeal in a bowl that could have anchored a boat. He ate all of it. The only other person at the table was a boy his own age with a candidate band and a silver plate curved along the bone of his wrist, who looked at the blue lanyard, then at his face, and said, "You're the sublevel."

"I'm the oatmeal."

"Fair." The boy turned his wrist over so Kade could read it. Amplification. A feeder program, three years. Preliminary rank four hundred seventy-two. "Don't make the face."

"I didn't."

"You made it with your eyebrows. Everybody does baseline, including people who arrive with a folder. Half the folders are wrong about what's in them." He went back to his eggs. "And the board forgets. That's the only kind thing about it. What you did last month weighs less than what you did this week. Whatever it says about you at noon, it's already started forgetting."

Kade looked through the glass at the empty rings. He put that where he could reach it, next to the sentence about facts and faults.

---

Stage three put him on a platform that moved, and then the room stopped being a room.

Light from six directions. Sound that was almost words. The floor tilted without warning and a padded arm came through at shoulder height, and he was under it before he had decided to be, and a tool dropped from nowhere into his left hand because that was where his hand was. He was better at this and knew why, and it was not Resonance. A classroom asked him to hold still inside somebody else's frame. This kept changing shape, and problems that moved had always made more sense to him than problems that sat.

The room rebuilt itself. The rings became the floor plan of a building section, projected: a gallery, a gap across it two meters wide, and a single plank bridging the gap with a number stenciled on its end. SIXTY KILOGRAMS. Beyond the gap, an orange case on a pedestal. Above it, a countdown. NINETY SECONDS.

"Bring the case to the center mark," Park said. "The environment supplies the consequences. It will not supply the rules."

He went for the plank, because it was there and it was short, and put one foot on it to feel it, and it took his weight, and the handrail beside it took his hand and was not there. Projection. He went down on the plank on one knee hard enough to send a white line up his thigh, and the wall said ROUTE FAILURE — FIVE SECONDS, in red.

"Seventy-four."

He backed off the plank and made himself look at it the way he had looked at a cargo tram once, in a classroom, with a number under it. Sixty kilograms. He weighed sixty-eight, and the plank had held him anyway, because that was what real structures did: a posted limit was not where a thing broke. It was where somebody had stopped promising. The case, when he got to it, would weigh something, and the plank had already told him he would not be carrying it back this way.

The long route was a projected stair down, along, and up, with a door in it. The door closed on a cycle. He watched it shut once and counted, and it opened at nine. Stair, gallery, the door open for him, the case on its pedestal on the far side. THIRTY-ONE KILOGRAMS when he lifted it, and it was every gram of that, and he ran it back toward the door with the clock at thirty-eight and the door beginning to close.

He could make it. He had the stride. He would be through with the case as the edge came down, or he would be through and the case would not, or he would be neither, on a projected floor with a real steel case and a real knee.

He stopped. The door closed in front of him. He set the case down and put his hand on it and watched the clock give away nine seconds he would never get back, and when the door opened he lifted the case and walked through.

He put it on the center mark at four seconds past. TIME EXPIRED, in red. Then, under it, green: OBJECTIVE COMPLETE — LOAD SECURE — LIMITS OBSERVED.

He sat down beside the case. "Those two lines disagree."

"The clock scores the ideal response. The objective scores the acceptable one. You declined a route the posted limit excluded, and you declined a closing door with a load in your hands. That is a scored behavior. It is called judgment."

Nobody at Northline had ever scored a boy for stopping. Stopping was refusing, or failing, or making an adult repeat the question. Here it had a line, and the line was green.

The case sank. The room offered him three lit paths to a target above the far wall, and he took none of them and went up the frame of the platform itself, hand over hand on the bolted edge that held the whole floor, because the frame was real and the paths were light. UNAUTHORIZED PATH, in red. Then green.

Numbers went by on the glass while he breathed. Reaction, seventy-third percentile, then eighty-first. Hazard discrimination, ninety-first. Adaptive route selection, ninety-sixth. Behind the console the heads came up, and he hated how much he liked it. Then the field trials resumed between the adaptive ones, and every line that found him quick or strange or steady came with a zero under it, until it felt like a rhythm somebody had chosen.

---

Sen rode up with him.

He had his shoes and shirt back on and a knee that had begun to stiffen, and the aide who had brought him down was gone. The Director of Asterion stood in the elevator with no cup and no tablet and nothing in her hands at all, and the doors closed on forty-two meters of rock.

"You are being entered now," she said.

"Into what?"

"The first-year ladder. Provisional students are ranked."

"Why?"

"Because a rank is the one sentence about a student that a coach, a sponsor, and a ministry can all read without a translator." She watched the numbers above the door climb. "If I exempted you, the exemption would become the most legible thing about you."

His band warmed. He turned his wrist over.

MERCER, KADE A. YEAR ONE, PROVISIONAL. PERCEPTION, UNCONFIRMED. OUTPUT, ZERO POINT ZERO ZERO. CONTROL, UNRATED. COMBAT, UNRATED. A pause while the last line thought. FIRST-YEAR RANK: 10,482 OF 10,482.

He waited for it to hurt. It kept the appointment. The most selective school on the planet had let him in through a classified back door, and in five hours he had proved, with signatures, that he was the least capable person in it.

"Five hours," he said.

"Signed and witnessed."

The elevator passed the surface and found the phone in his pocket, which began to buzz, and then buzz again, and then did not stop. He did not take it out. Sen watched him not take it out.

"What," he said, because she was watching.

"I was curious whether you would ask for the transit desk before the doors opened."

"You think I'm leaving."

"I think you have left things before, on the theory that a thing cannot judge you if you are not in it. Your file is candid on the subject."

"You read my file."

"I read every admitted student's file."

"I'm not admitted."

"Then you are a thoroughly read provisional." The doors opened on daylight and the clinic corridor, and she did not step out. "Tomorrow you move to House Meridian. Its faculty lead put in for you by name this morning, before the number posted."

"Before."

"Before. Make of that what you like. I have." She let him take two steps. "Mr. Mercer. What the room found unusual about you this morning is a fact about the room's instruments. It is not yet a fact about your importance, in either direction. People will try to sell you both. Buy neither."

"You know what it is."

"I know more than the wall does. I am not going to say it today."

"Why not?"

"Because a partial explanation has a way of becoming a set of instructions, and I do not yet know which instructions you would follow." The doors began to close. "Eat lunch."

---

Park found him in the corridor with a thin glass tablet and set it on the bench beside him.

"Certification. Ten minutes to review. You may challenge procedure, equipment, interpretation, or identity."

"Not the number."

"Disliking a number is not a category. It is a mood."

Every trial had a row. Time, confidence, equipment status, the initials of whoever had verified it. Nine passive intervals. One control pass. Six active attempts. Four adaptive scenarios. The zero was no longer a hole in a wall. It was a column of small finished things, each one signed by a person.

He went to the passive sequence first, because it had been sealed all morning. Five of the nine intervals had been live. He had called one. Interval seven, live, subject report at twenty-one seconds, physiological variance zero point seven percent, field correlation below reporting threshold.

"That's the one."

"It is. One report in nine, and it landed on a live interval. One report is not a rate."

"The variance is real."

"It is also here." She touched the row and it opened. "And here." Interval three, blank, a smaller bump, no report. The reset before interval nine, blank, a smaller bump, no report. Three flickers in the instruments. Once when he had spoken and a field had been on. Twice when he had said nothing and nothing had been.

"So it means nothing."

"It means the instruments saw three things and you saw one. I do not know what that means. I will not pretend to, and I will not let you pretend to in my record. Do you want to file on interpretation?"

He read the three rows again. He had nothing to put under them but a cold spot on his neck and a want the size of a building.

"No."

She nodded once, and looked as if she would have liked a better answer as much as he would.

He opened the formula, because it was there, and it annoyed him more than if it had been hidden. Adaptive metrics broke ties and informed placement. They could not stand in for categories he had not performed. Every one of the ten thousand four hundred eighty-one people above him had produced something a sensor could see, and he had produced a scrape from the ventilation.

"So it's right."

"It is correct. Correct is a property of the model. I would not confuse it with complete."

At the bottom of the page were two lines. ACKNOWLEDGE RESULT. DECLINE CERTIFICATION AND REPEAT BASELINE.

"If I repeat."

"Tomorrow's result replaces today's only if a procedural fault is found. Otherwise both stand."

He looked at the second line for a long moment. Another morning in the round room, another zero, probably, and one more day on the far side of a door with the number not yet his. One more day of not being judged. He knew that shape from the inside. He had been building it out of different materials since he was nine.

The number hurt because it was public. It hurt more because, today, inside this model, it was true.

He put his thumb on the glass.

RESULT ACKNOWLEDGED — NOT A WAIVER OF APPEAL.

Park had the tablet under her arm when he said her name.

"Did the Director tell you to keep going after the first blank?"

"The Director authorized a full baseline."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No." She looked down the corridor toward the elevator. "I kept going because a blank at nine in the morning is a guess about the afternoon. I do not certify guesses. That is my reason. I do not have another one to give you."

She went. He sat under his number and let the phone out of his pocket at last.

*Eight. You went to the fanciest school on the planet and came last. Out of everybody. I have never been prouder of anything.*

A number he did not know: ZERO EQUALS 10,482. A forum link, already at the top of the board. ZERO MERCER — CLASSIFIED PRODIGY OR ADMINISTRATIVE ERROR? He did not open it.

The shame was there. So was the anger. So was the inconvenient thing he had felt looking down at the island from the air, and he did not make any of the three cancel the others. He let them stand in a row, like signed trials.

He took a picture of his wrist and sent it.

*Bottom of the list. Scroll all the way down.*

*Nine. Is this a cry for help.*

*It's a floor,* he typed. *I've been told I can't fall through it.*

---

The number went upstairs with him. He had not expected it to be waiting in the dining hall before he was.

The arrival residence ran the first-year ladder on a strip above the food stations. The strip refreshed as he came in with a tray, and the room went thin the way it had last night, conversation losing its middle, wrists coming up. Somebody at a white-lanyard table said "Ten thousand," and somebody else finished it for them, and it went around like a song everybody knew the end of.

The boy from last night, the one with the hand on the table, stood at the end of the line with his tray. He did not look pleased and he did not look sorry.

"Seven o'clock came," he said.

"It did."

"For what it's worth, I lost money."

"On which side?"

The boy's face did the brief honest thing again. "Yours." He took his tray and went.

Imani had the window table and a blue lanyard and, on the inside of her wrist, six thousand seven hundred seventy-one, which she turned face down when he sat.

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to say I beat you." She started eating. "It moved one paper clip, with a cardiologist watching, and the thing in my chest didn't argue. I'm cleared for low-output work. Next week they hand me something heavier, and if my heart keeps its opinion to itself, I stay." A pause. "So I'm not going home. Yet. I'd like that not to be on a wall."

The girl with the stylus arrived and turned her slate around before she sat. Beneath the lowest of her seven bands, the pending line had changed. MERCER — 000 — 10,482 — TOLD: 0.00 — SAW: —.

"You're the only one on my sheet with a blank in that column," she said. "I fill it from watching. They put you under a rock. So tell me what you saw."

He thought about the marker on the third ring and the woman saying *now* before the console did. "Nothing," he said. "I saw exactly what everybody else in the room saw, and less than the instruments. Put that."

She wrote SAW: NOTHING and looked at it for a second, and did not cross it out, and started eating.

The boy in the knitted hat came in with two trays, read the strip, read Kade, and sat, and said only, "You ate," which was true, and the best thing anyone said to him at lunch.

---

Every baseline candidate sat in a hall built around a screen three stories tall for the ladder briefing, and Kade came in beside Imani and found his own name up there gone from gray to white.

"You certified," said the Amplification candidate, a row ahead, without turning around.

"Everybody can see that?"

"You thought the public ladder had a private side?" Imani said.

The man on the stage wore a green sash with LADDER OFFICE along one edge and the posture of a man who had given this talk every autumn and sat through it himself once.

"A first rank is a baseline. It is not a forecast." Around the hall, hands went to slates. "It sets your initial training band. It does not set class access, housing, meals, medical care, or equipment safety standards, and it does not set your worth, whatever last year's forum concluded."

Recalculation had windows. A full baseline at enrollment. Weekly updates once rated instruction began. Immediate changes only after a sanctioned challenge or a corrected procedural fault. Older evidence lost weight as newer evidence replaced it. The board forgets, the boy had said, and here was the forgetting written out as a schedule.

A boy near the front asked why students under medical restriction appeared on the ladder beside students who were not.

"Because a restriction describes what exposure a student is permitted, not what a student can do. Restrictions are visible to instructors and challenge officials. They are not public labels. The ladder shows a score. It does not show a diagnosis."

Imani had stopped joking. Her hand had closed over the sleeve that covered her wrist, and Kade understood, watching it, why she had told a stranger about her heart last night at a table of her choosing, and why ten thousand strangers were not going to be told for her.

The Amplification candidate asked, without raising his hand, how a category got onto the ladder that was not on it now.

Half the room turned toward the back before the man could answer. He waited until they had finished.

"I can see where you are all looking. Look at me. Demonstrated. Replicated. Reviewed. In that order. A category is not created for a claim."

Kade put his hand up, and the man looked at him like somebody who had known this was coming since lunch. "Mr. Mercer."

"And if the model's the thing that's wrong."

"Then the evidence shows where. Until it does, the model reports what it can support. That is not a judgment of you. It is a description of the model."

A condition. Not fair, not unfair. The system had made a claim about today and signed it, and if he wanted a different claim, anger was not evidence. He would have to produce something the room could not put down to ventilation.

They were dismissed. Nobody left without one look up.

"That was my question," Imani said. "You just had the wrist for it." She stood. "Tomorrow, Meridian."

"How do you know my House?"

"Also public."

"Is anything here not public?"

"Your feelings about it. So far."

He walked out under his own name.

---

At twenty hundred exactly he called from the top of the exterior stair, where the ladder scrolled across the courtyard and Meridian's bell hung under him in its frame.

She picked up on the first ring, at the kitchen table, with the silver folder and a mug and sleep still in her face.

"I looked," she said. "I want you to know I looked before I called, so you don't have to tell me."

"Everybody looked."

"Then I'm on a list with everybody. First time for that." She read the number off her own screen, slowly, all of it, the way she read a dosage back to a doctor. "Is it right?"

"Today it is. Inside the model."

"That sounded like somebody else's sentence."

"It's the doctor's. I'm borrowing it until I've got one."

She did not say anything comforting, and he loved her for it.

Then he gave her the other thing, now that it had a doctor's word next to it. The pressure at the base of the skull. Once in the air yesterday, and he had not called it in, and he told her why, and she listened to the why without a word. Once today, in a sealed room with thirty sensors on him, and he had said so out loud, in the room, before he could decide it was nothing. The instruments had seen a flicker when he spoke, seven tenths of a percent, below what they called a threshold, with a field switched on. They had seen two more flickers when he said nothing and nothing was on.

"So three on their side. One on yours."

"And one on a plane nobody was measuring."

"Does it hurt."

"No. It's cold. It's gone before I can look at it."

"If it hurts, that's a different sentence. That one you call in while it's happening." She wrote something on the folder, off screen. "Not at twenty hundred."

"Deal."

Then she asked it, and there was nothing folded up inside the question at all. No test. No disappointment held ready. "Do you want to come home?"

He looked past the phone. Students on the bridge below with bags. Down the southern arm an arena with its roof open. Meridian's courtyard under him, where his band would open the gate tomorrow. The panel across the way, and his name at the foot of it, last, in white, certified by his own thumb.

"No."

He had not known it was ready. It had been sitting in his mouth since the elevator.

"Then don't," she said.

"That's the whole conversation?"

"That's the whole conversation. I've got a shift at six, and I'm saving the rest for a more irritating night."

"Thank you."

"Thank you for calling on the second."

The line went. He stayed on the stair. Across the courtyard the ladder scrolled up through the thousands toward the names people checked, and nobody was standing where he was standing, looking at the bottom, because the bottom was one line long and the line was his.

Tomorrow there was a House, and a gate that would open, and people in it who had asked for last place by name, and he did not know what any of that meant yet. He intended to be there when he found out. It was the first plan he had made in years that did not include the way out.

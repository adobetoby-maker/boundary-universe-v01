# Chapter 6 — Forty-Seven Seconds

*Twelve. Today somebody gets to hit you and it counts as school. Don't stand there looking grateful.*

Five fifty. Under it, from the academy, sent at four in the morning by a system that did not sleep: EQUIPMENT CHECK — FOOTWEAR — FAIL. INSUFFICIENT LATERAL SUPPORT. A photograph of his own sneakers, taken from above in the corridor by something he had not seen, the worn outer edge of the right one circled in red.

The academy shoes sat in their box on the shelf where he had put them two days ago, unopened, a statement to furniture. He took the lid off. Gray, a blue seam along the sole, lighter than any shoe he had owned, and they fit the way things fit when a machine has measured your foot. He laced them and felt like a person wearing somebody else's decision.

"They're shoes," Eli said from his bed, where he was awake, which Kade had not expected, with a tablet in one hand and a piece of toast in the other. "They don't know who bought them."

"Everything on this island knows who bought it."

"The shoes are neutral. I've checked." He got up. "Bay Two's still closed because of me. Bay One's empty until seven. I'm going to teach you not to get hit."

"You've lost six of your last seven."

"Which is why I can identify the outcome faster than anyone you know."

---

Bay One was a low room under the near tower with a padded floor, a rack of equipment along one wall, and a camera in each upper corner. Eli propped the tablet against a water bottle and opened a timer.

"Rule one. Don't stand where the other person wants you."

"That isn't a rule. That's the whole idea of fighting."

"I'm building from fundamentals."

Sera came in with a cup of coffee and did not say good morning. She set the cup on the floor and moved Kade's back foot fifteen centimeters outward with the toe of her boot.

"You were standing on a line. Now you're standing on a floor."

"I had balance."

"You had hope."

He shifted his weight onto the new stance. It was less comfortable and it was more stable, and he disliked that those were both true.

Mara arrived at ten past, in training black, hair already tied back for her own session. She watched him run a defensive pattern Eli had found on the House server, once, then twice, and said, "Your shoulders tell the truth before your feet do."

"That sounds personal."

"You tense before you retreat. Both shoulders. Up." She took a padded baton from the rack, tested it against her palm, and stood two meters in front of him. "Most people tense before they get hit. Most people here are trying to stop."

Sera dropped four flat marker discs around him in a square that was not quite square. "Rule two. She touches your shoulder, you leave through a side she hasn't taken."

"Which side is that?"

"Whichever one's left."

"That's deliberately unhelpful."

"It's a fight."

"No field," Mara said. "No hard contact. I touch, you reset."

She came forward and he went backward and the foam touched his right shoulder before his second step had landed. Reset. She came again from the same angle and he fixed his eyes on the baton, and the baton went left, and he went right, and her empty hand was already in the space he had chosen. The foam tapped his ribs. Reset. On the third he waited, and she waited, and Eli looked at the timer as if it could be asked for a ruling.

"At some point this is just a tense meeting."

Mara flicked the baton toward his left shoulder. He flinched back before the foam had crossed half the distance, and she changed hands and touched his right.

"Your shoulders."

"Are traitors."

"They're early." She nodded at Eli, who turned the tablet around. Two stick figures, one built from Mara's band and one from the corner camera, dragged backward to where they had stood before anything happened.

"She starts here." A mark. "You raise both shoulders here." An earlier mark. "One hundred sixty milliseconds. You react before she's moved."

"You prepare to be hit before she's decided where," Sera said, from outside the square. "So she gets to decide both things."

He rolled his shoulders down. The next touch landed on his chest, then his arm, then his ribs, because he had crossed his feet and nearly walked himself out of the exercise unassisted. Every time he backed out of the square without choosing, Sera moved a disc inward, and Eli read out the time between Mara's commitment and Kade's first useful step. None of the numbers were kind, and Eli did not make them kinder.

On the ninth try Mara's weight moved onto her front foot and his shoulders started up and he put them down. He felt himself do it, a thing he had never once felt himself do. The baton came toward his left side. Her hip closed the gap beside it. The disc behind him offered distance and no angle, and the space past her empty hand stood wide open, and that was the one she wanted him to take.

He stepped toward the baton. Foam brushed his sleeve. He turned through it and came out of the square past her loaded foot, and Eli stopped the timer, and Sera left the last disc where it lay.

Mara looked over her shoulder. "Acceptable."

He was breathing as if the forty seconds had been a hill. "Your praise is a lot."

"Do it twice."

He failed the next three. On the fourth he came out a different side, not fast and not clean, but before she had chosen for him. Eli held out the surviving half of his toast, and Kade looked at the bite missing from it and told him to keep it.

Sera collected the discs. Four pale circles stayed in the floor coating, and Kade stood in the middle of them with the discovery that he could get measurably less bad at something before breakfast, which nobody at Northline had ever asked him to test.

Eli read from the tablet. "Priya Shah. Six thousand two hundred four. Standard One, confirmed. Primary strength, acceleration control and balance disruption. Three years of prep combat."

Kade looked at Mara. "You know her?"

"Same House. Different floor."

"Convenient."

"She's good."

"That sounded like a warning."

"It was information." Mara picked up her bag. "Watch her feet."

A month ago, getting up before seven to be hit by strangers would have been evidence that his life had come apart. He checked the wall clock and was annoyed that he had only forty minutes left, and that annoyed him more than the assessment did.

---

At breakfast Meridian treated his match as House property. Anton Bell had watched Priya's last three bouts and described them with his hands. Nessa Kim thought Kade should stay mobile. Sera thought Kade should stop listening to Anton and Nessa. Eli had built a model overnight that he would not show anyone, because its first result had given Kade a four percent chance and he said the sample was emotionally irresponsible.

Priya Shah came in halfway through it and set her tray down three seats away.

The file had not mentioned that she was small, or that she wore her hair in a braid pinned flat so nothing could be grabbed, or that she came down the crowded aisle without touching anyone, like a person who had decided in advance where every body would be. She looked at him. He looked at her. Eli became fascinated by his oatmeal.

"You're my assessment," she said.

"So I'm told."

"Sorry."

"For what?"

"You'll see." She started eating.

He looked at Sera. "She seems nice."

"That's bad for you."

"Why?"

"If she were awful you could get angry instead of learning."

---

At eight forty the assessment wing split them up. Observers went up a ramp to the seats; competitors went through a door into an equipment bay with gray lockers, a medical station, and a yellow line on the floor that ran toward the arena doors. Eli stopped at the foot of the ramp and said, "Remember the model," and Kade said, "The one you won't show me," and Eli said that was the one, and Sera caught him by the back of the shirt before he could follow Kade over the line.

Mara did not give him anything. She touched two fingers to her own right shoulder, and he lowered his, and the gate shut between them.

The equipment officer ran a scanner over the academy shoes without comment, tightened a sensor collar around his neck, and slid two impact strips under the training suit against his ribs.

"Current pain."

"No."

"Headache in the last twenty-four hours."

"No."

"Unexplained dizziness, visual disturbance, field echo, tremor, or loss of time."

He looked at the camera in the corner. "That list got worse as it went."

She waited with one hand above the screen.

"No."

She entered it and turned the screen toward him. His name, his band's lines, and a contract in three rows, each one gray, the way a form looks when a field is empty.

MEASURED SAFE MAXIMUM: UNRESOLVED. ACTIVE FIELD AUTHORIZATION: OBSERVATION ONLY. CONVENTIONAL CONTACT PROTOCOL: NOVICE.

"You can't initiate field action. If an event occurs, Instructor Hale stops the assessment. Shah's output is capped at twelve percent of her measured maximum, and her permitted contact force is keyed to your strips, not hers. Two amber readings end the match. One red ends it at once."

"Does she know that?"

"She has the same page."

Across the bay, Priya stood on a floor plate with another officer while blue calibration light climbed her from ankles to shoulders. She lifted both hands, turned each wrist, and let a small pulse go through the plate. It moved under her, half a centimeter. She moved with it so smoothly that her head did not change height. The officer reset it. Again, at six percent, the other direction. Her balance did not so much as flicker.

Kade looked at the plate under his own feet, which had done nothing all morning and would go on doing nothing.

"Acknowledgment," the officer said, and touched the box. "This is an assessment, not a requirement to absorb contact. You can withdraw now, surrender inside, or ask Hale to stop at any time. Withdrawal leaves your combat rating unresolved. It doesn't change your academic status."

An exit. A door in the wall with the handle on his side, offered before he had asked, the way this island kept doing.

"What does unresolved do to my rank?"

"Nothing, today. It prevents the system from calculating a combat component."

"So the line stays blank."

"Until there's evidence."

Beyond the doors a hundred conversations were shaking the seats, and on the display above them his empty combat line waited beside Priya's number. He could walk back up the ramp and the line would stay blank, and nobody would ever watch him fail to fill it.

He put his thumb on the glass.

"Then let's make some bad evidence."

The officer's face did not move. "The academy has extensive storage."

Priya met him at the yellow line. "They gave you novice contact?"

"Apparently I'm delicate."

"It means I control the exchange." The breakfast voice was gone; this was the one that went with the braid. "If you turn away, I disengage. If you go down, I give you room. If your collar goes red, I stop whether Hale calls it or not."

"You say that to everyone?"

"Everyone should hear it."

The doors opened, white floor first, then the clear walls, then two circles twelve meters apart. She let him go through first.

---

The arena was smaller than the silver discs along the southern arm, a room for instruction rather than broadcast. White composite floor, clear barrier walls with a boundary strip a long step inside them, sensors in everything. A hundred first-years in tiered seats, attending, orientation had said, because peer observation accelerated tactical learning. Kade had a simpler theory about teenagers and other teenagers getting hit.

His circle was at the west end. He stood in it in a gray training suit and a collar that felt too expensive to sweat in. Across the floor, Priya rolled her shoulders once, looked at the board, and looked at him.

"Sorry," she said again.

He glanced up toward the booth. "Which of us is that for?"

She smiled. "I like you."

"That's one."

In the front row Eli raised a hand. "Two."

"You don't count. Housing assigned you." Eli looked wounded. Mara sat beside him with her arms folded. Sera stood behind them against the wall. Three rows up, Taren Holt, who had no reason Kade knew of to be in this session, had a seat in it anyway, leaning back like a man who had found the chair by accident.

Instructor Hale stood at the control rail with a slate under one arm and the build of a man who had been hit for a living and had formed opinions about it. His voice came from the speakers.

"Assessment rules. Controlled contact. No head strikes. No intentional joint injury. Field output capped at twelve percent of measured safe maximum. Match ends on pin, surrender, boundary exit, or instructor stop."

Kade raised a hand.

"Mercer."

"What if my measured safe maximum is zero?"

Some of the seats laughed. Hale did not.

"Then I recommend creativity."

"First sensible advice all week."

Eli put his face in his hands. Mara had not looked away from Priya. "Her right foot," she said, through the open barrier, before it sealed. "It loads before she goes."

"That costs extra?"

"No. I dislike preventable stupidity."

The barrier closed with a soft tone. The signal went from red to amber. Priya bounced once on the balls of her feet. Kade did not.

Green.

Priya vanished.

Not really. He knew that even as his eyes lost her. Something hit his chest, the air went out of him, his feet left the floor, and he was on his back sliding, and the clock on the wall said three.

He looked at the ceiling. "Well."

She had stopped instead of following. "You okay?"

"Excellent hospitality."

"Up, Mercer," Hale said.

He got to a knee. His chest hurt, not badly; the suit had spread it. What bothered him was not being hit. It was not knowing how. She had crossed twelve meters in a blink, and she had not simply run. Where her right foot had been there was one sharp scuff in the coating, as if the floor had been asked for something and had given it. He could not see the field. He could see what it had bought.

He stood. She came back to ready. "Again?"

"I was hoping for the written portion."

She laughed and came, and this time he watched her feet, which was correct and too late, because her shoulder arrived from the side while his eyes were still on the floor. He spun. She hooked his ankle and put him down. Nine.

The seats had stopped laughing, and not because he was doing better. The novelty was wearing off. He knew that weather. At Northline being bad at a thing in public was ordinary; at Asterion it had statistics. Priya backed off. "Stay down if you need to."

"I'm starting to dislike your kindness."

"It isn't strategy."

"That's worse."

His ribs had an opinion about standing. The collar stayed green. Hale had not stopped anything. Kade looked past the barrier to the booth, and Dr. Park sat behind the glass with her hands folded, and standing beside her, without a cup this time, was the Director of Asterion.

Of course.

Priya came slower. It helped, and not enough. She showed him left and he took it, and she changed direction with a sharpness no unassisted body could manage, and her forearm found his shoulder and turned him, gently, the way you turn a child toward a door, toward the boundary strip. He stumbled and caught it one step short of the line. Eighteen.

She stopped. He stared at her. "You could have finished that."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

"It's an assessment. They need to see what you do."

*Nothing* got as far as his teeth. It was the word he had given Mara at dinner, and Sera had told him what it was carrying, and he held it there and did not let it out.

He breathed. For three exchanges he had been trying to solve Priya Shah, who had spent three years becoming a problem better people than him had failed to solve. Wrong problem. He looked at the arena the way he had looked at a plank with a number on the end of it. Floor. Walls. Boundary strip. Her feet. His weight. The scuffs where dozens of students had loaded a foot and gone.

He could not feel Resonance. Fine. He could see consequence.

Her weight shifted. He saw it before she went. Not the field, the decision: the right foot loading, the hip beginning to turn, the floor taking her force a fraction before her body did anything with it. Mara had bought him that. He moved first.

Her eyes widened. He stepped inside the attack instead of away from it, and her forearm crossed air where his chest had been, and his hand closed on her wrist.

For half a second the whole room seemed to stop.

Then she smiled. "Better." She turned her hips, and he went over them, and the floor came up. Twenty-seven.

This time the seats laughed with him. Priya leaned into his view. "You nearly had something."

"I had your wrist."

"That's technically something."

His left shoulder joined his ribs. He got up. Twenty-seven seconds had felt like ten minutes.

She came again at thirty-one. He slipped the first strike and took the second, controlled, in the ribs, got a handful of her sleeve and let it go when she slid sideways to break his stance. Thirty-six. He was learning; he could feel it, each exchange a little less of a surprise than the last. She had started three years ahead, and he was not going to beat her at the thing she had spent those years on. So he needed to stop doing her thing.

He backed toward the west wall.

Priya slowed. "You're cornering yourself."

"Maybe I like architecture."

She frowned. His heel found the boundary strip. One step behind him was elimination, and less than a meter past that was the clear wall, and she had exactly one obvious move, and it was to drive him out, and he had walked here to make her take it.

He was not sure why. That was true, and he would say it later to the people who asked. Some part of him wanted to see what the whole system did when the force had nowhere to go but through him, and that part had picked the ground before the rest of him had voted.

The clock said forty-one, and she went.

He watched all of it. Her body. Her foot. The floor under the foot. Himself. The strip. The wall. Force had to go somewhere; that thought was old, he had been thinking it since a cargo tram on a whiteboard. The sensation was new. The cold thumb pressed at the base of his skull, exact as always, and this time it did not let go. It spread. A line of cold went forward through his head to a place behind his eyes, and for one instant that had no length the arena stopped being objects and became relationships. Her foot to the floor. The floor to the lattice under it. The lattice to the wall. Her momentum into him. His body toward the line. A chain, a system, and a system ended where somebody decided it ended.

Something in him moved. Not a muscle. Not a thought with words in it. A decision about where one thing stopped and another began.

Her palm hit his chest.

He should have flown. Instead the wall boomed.

It was the sound of a door the size of a building being shut. The lights flickered. Priya stopped dead, not gently, the way a person stops when the thing they were pushing has stopped being there. Her momentum left his chest and arrived somewhere behind him. The clear wall flexed outward. White stress lines flashed through the floor supports at his feet and faded.

He was still standing. For half a second.

Then both knees went and he sat down hard on the composite, and his vision narrowed to a tunnel with Priya's face at the end of it, and Hale's voice said "Stop," once, and the clock froze at forty-seven.

Silence. Not the ordinary kind. The kind where a hundred people forget to make a sound at the same moment.

His hands were shaking. The cold line behind his eyes had turned into pain, as if a switch had been thrown. Priya crouched a body's length away, and he had never seen anyone look at him the way she was looking.

"What did you do?"

He looked at the wall. The stress lines were gone; the smart material was already spreading the load. "I have no idea."

That was not entirely true. He knew what it had felt like. A steel ball in a chamber, and a line through a platform, and a word white on black. His stomach turned over.

Park came onto the floor at a run. Sen walked. People who frightened rooms did not need to hurry.

Park dropped beside him with a light in her hand. "Headache."

"Yes."

"One to ten."

"Four."

"Nausea."

"Two."

"Tremor." He held his hand up and let it answer. She did not thank it. She looked into each eye, and her mouth did something small.

Sen crouched beside Priya. "Injured?"

Priya flexed both hands and rolled a shoulder. "No. Surprised."

Sen looked at the wall for a long moment. Then at Kade. No cup, no lifted hand, nothing at all in her face.

Behind the barrier the whispering had begun. Eli had both hands on the glass. Mara was standing. Taren had stopped pretending anything about the chair. Sera was watching him with an expression he could not read and would have paid to.

Hale checked the official record. "Match to Shah. Mercer unable to continue."

Kade laughed, weakly. Priya looked offended on his behalf. "I hit him and the wall broke."

"You remained combat capable."

"He did something."

"Yes," Sen said.

One word. The whispering stopped.

Kade looked up at her. "What?"

Her eyes had gone back to the support lattice. "Not here."

He tried to get up. Park's hand came down on his shoulder. "Sit."

"I'm fine."

"No."

"People are getting very comfortable with that word."

"You have a tremor, a headache, and your pupils are two different sizes."

He stopped. "That seems worth knowing."

"Sit."

He sat. A maintenance panel had lit on the barrier beside him. STRUCTURAL LOAD EVENT DETECTED. SOURCE: UNRESOLVED. Eli made a sound through the glass that was half a gasp and half a laugh.

The replay came up on the board on its own, and Priya's final charge ran in slow motion. Forty-one. The foot loading. The acceleration. The palm. And then the thing that was strange, which was not a flash or a flare or a wave, but an absence: the telemetry that tracked field exchange dropped out, and stayed out, for six frames, and when it came back the wall was already taking the load.

Six frames. The seats went from murmur to noise.

The replay froze. A red banner replaced it.

DATA FLAGGED — RESTRICTED REVIEW.

Kade closed his eyes. "Please tell me that doesn't mean a government vehicle."

Sen stood. "No."

He opened one eye. "That sounded promising."

"It means three."

He let his head fall back against the wall that had, apparently, caught a person for him. Priya laughed, and he looked at her, and she said, "Sorry."

"For what?"

"This is going to look bad."

---

Medical kept him for three hours.

Park scanned his head twice and then made him walk a line, touch his nose with each index finger, track a light, and answer orientation questions he found insulting until he missed one.

"What day is it?"

"Saturday."

"It is Sunday."

He stared at her. Behind her, five shapes stayed up on the wall display: a square, a circle, a triangle, two interlocking rings, and a black shape with seven sides, which he had named correctly a minute ago and had taken too long to name.

"That concern you?"

"Yes."

"Good."

It had escaped before he could put a joke on it, and Park did not seem to want one. She cleared the shapes. "Neurological injuries reward performance. People learn which answers get them out of the room. I need the wrong ones too."

"That's inefficient."

"For your discharge time. Not for your brain."

She laid a row of textured tiles on the bed table and had him name them blind, right hand and then left, and somewhere between hands the dotted one became the ridged one. She marked it. "Again." The second pass was clean. Then a balance plate that leaned three degrees and made his stomach try to continue without him, and he stepped off too fast, and Park held out a basin.

"Nausea."

"One."

"You said two in the arena."

"Improvement."

"Your skin says three."

He took the basin. "My skin is dramatic."

"Your skin is not trying to be discharged."

He waited until the room had finished moving. "Three."

She changed the record. The number did not become an accusation, and no lecture followed; she lowered the plate to one degree and went on. He had spent years treating pain as information other people could use to stop him. Park treated it as a condition of the problem. It did not make the room less frightening. It made the truth slightly more useful than the lie, which was more than most rooms had offered.

"I need my phone," he said, between tests.

"No screens."

"It's a call. I don't have to look at it." She waited, so he gave her the rest. "My mother. Cold goes at twenty hundred. Hurt goes right then. That's the deal, and this is hurt."

Park considered him. "Her page is on your file. This is care, not evaluation, so it's covered, and she is still called, by me, when I have something to tell her." She held out the phone and set a timer on the wall. "You may go first. Four minutes. You will not look at the screen."

Elena answered from somewhere with a corridor's echo behind it. "Are you hurt."

"Yes." He had never once opened with that word to her, and he heard her stop walking. "Hurt goes right then. It's a headache and my hands were shaking and my eyes were wrong and I'm in medical and the doctor's standing here with a timer. It was a four. It's a three."

There was a silence on the line that had a hospital in it.

"Imaged?"

"Twice."

"Who read them."

"Park. And a neurologist whose name I've lost."

"That's the headache. Not you." A breath. "Did you get hit?"

"I got hit a lot. That part's fine. This part's the other thing."

"I know which part it is." Something in her voice was being held very still. "I'm on until six. Twenty hundred. Exactly. You call me again with a doctor's word next to it, and I want the word."

"You'll have the word."

"Kade. You called."

"You made me."

"I made you promise. You called." The line went.

Park took the phone back and said nothing about any of it, which he was grateful for.

By the third hour his pupils matched. The tremor had gone somewhere and might come back. The headache sat at two. No structural injury. No conventional discharge, from him or from anything else in the arena. No explanation.

Priya came by before her afternoon session, in ordinary academy blue, and leaned in the doorway.

"You okay?"

"It's Sunday, apparently."

She smiled. "I watched the replay."

"Condolences."

"You moved the hit."

"I didn't move anything."

"The wall disagrees." She looked at his hands. "They say you had zero output."

"People really like telling me that."

"For what it's worth, you got better during the match."

"I lost in forty-seven seconds."

"You lasted forty-four seconds longer than you should have after the first one."

He looked up. "That's either encouraging or cruel."

"Both can be true." She pushed off the frame. "Different floor. Same House. Dinner's at seven."

He decided Priya Shah was going to be very hard to resent, and that this was going to be a problem.

His phone had three hundred and eighteen notifications on it. Park allowed him ten minutes with the brightness down, and stood where she could watch him not enjoy them.

Eli: ARE YOU ALIVE. Then, DON'T ANSWER THAT PARK SAID NO SCREENS. Then, eight minutes later, I HAVE THE TELEMETRY. Then, I DO NOT HAVE THE TELEMETRY. Then, SOMEONE REMOVED MY ACCESS WHICH IS RUDE AND ALSO INTERESTING.

Mara, one line: *Tell me when you're cleared.*

Sera: *Eat before you sleep. Not optional.*

Taren: *The forum's named it. You'll hate the name.* And a link.

THE FORTY-SEVEN SECOND FIGHT. Under it, a slowed clip of the last six seconds, which had crossed fifty thousand campus views while he was naming shapes. The comments were the comments. HE TELEPORTED FORCE. NO FIELD OUTPUT. SHAH GOT ROBBED. Priya herself had answered that one: I WON. LEARN TO READ. He smiled at that. Then another thread surfaced. ZERO IS CHEATING. He closed the forum.

"Good decision," Park said.

"I was being educationally enriched."

"The forum is contraindicated after unexplained neurological events."

"That should be a policy."

Park almost smiled, which was, he suspected, as far as she went.

*Thirteen. Everybody's watched you fall over. I've watched it nine times. The wall is my favorite character.*

Sen came at the end of the third hour with a tablet, which he took for a bad sign.

"You're cleared for housing."

"Good sign."

"Not for unsupervised field activity of any kind."

"Neutral?"

"No."

His shoulders came up, and he made them come down, and she watched him do it. "For how long?"

"Until we understand what happened."

"That could be forever."

"It could."

She did not soften it, and he found that he could hold appreciation and resentment in the same hand without either one spilling. "What did I do?"

"We know the result."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No."

"You knew something out there. You said yes."

Her eyes narrowed, slightly. "I recognized that the event did not fit ordinary field transfer."

"That's a very careful sentence."

"It was."

"Do you have a name for it?"

A pause. Not long. Long enough that he could have drawn its outline.

"Not one I'm prepared to give you today."

There it was again. The shape around an answer, the same shape Renn had made in Room Four, the same one she had made herself in an elevator two days ago. He looked at it and put it with the others.

"Go back to Meridian. Eat. Sleep. Tomorrow we discuss training."

"Restricted training."

"Yes."

He slid off the bed, and his knees held. Progress. At the door she said his name.

"You did not win that fight."

"I noticed."

"You learned something anyway." He waited. "So did we."

That was less comforting than she had meant it to be, and he suspected she knew exactly how much.

---

Meridian had turned his forty-seven seconds into a festival by the time he got back. A hand-lettered sign over the elevator said WELCOME BACK, WALL BOY. Somebody had crossed out WALL BOY and written ZERO WALL under it, and somebody else had crossed out both and written NO, in a hand he decided was Mara's.

Eli met him outside eight fourteen. "You look alive."

"Park signed for it."

Eli hugged him, briefly, and then reached past him for the door control as if that had been the plan the whole time, and Kade let him have that.

Inside, Mara sat at Eli's desk with three replay windows open. Sera had his chair and a takeout box. Taren leaned against the wall by the window, the one place in the room from which you could see everyone.

"Did I lose my room while I was gone?"

"Yes," Sera said.

"Democracy," Taren said.

Mara looked at his hands. "Still shaking?"

"No."

"Headache?"

"Two." Her eyes came up, and he answered the question she had not asked. "Yes. Park has all of it. So does my mother."

She nodded, once, and turned one of the windows toward him. Eli took over. "The gap is exactly six frames."

"You mentioned access."

"I temporarily had access."

"How?"

Eli developed an interest in the replay controls. Taren coughed. "House systems are porous."

Kade looked from one to the other. "Those are suspiciously compatible answers."

"The important part," Eli said, "is that the wall takes the load."

Sera pointed a fork at him. "Do not turn his neurological problem into a hobby."

"It isn't a hobby."

"Your hobbies explode."

"Fair."

Kade sat on his bed. The room quieted the way a room does when everyone in it has been waiting for one question.

"What did it feel like?" Mara said.

He looked at the paused image. Her hand on his chest. His body at the line. The wall.

"Like everything was connected."

Taren's face changed. Only slightly. Kade saw it because he watched everyone's face, and filed it, and did not know yet what folder it went in.

"Everything what?" Mara said.

"Priya. Me. Floor. Wall."

"That's ordinary force transfer."

"No." He closed his eyes, and it was there behind them, close enough that his stomach tightened. How did you describe a thing that had not been sight or touch. "It wasn't that they were connected. It was that the divisions felt optional."

Nobody said anything. Eli had stopped moving. Sera put her fork down. Taren looked at the replay again, and looked at it for a beat longer than the replay needed.

Kade opened his eyes. "That sounded worse out loud."

"Yes," Mara said.

"Thank you."

Eli cleared the windows and opened a modeling program, blank. "We can make it worse with diagrams."

Sera looked at the headache monitor Park had paired to Kade's phone. "Ten minutes."

"Park said limited screens."

"Which is why Eli's using the wall and you're sitting over there."

Eli built it from six plain blocks: two bodies, the arena floor, the lattice under it, the barrier wall, the building frame, with lines joining every contact. "Conventional chain. Priya accelerates against the floor. Priya puts momentum into Kade. Kade puts it into the boundary strip, eventually, with several painful steps in between."

"Accurate," Kade said.

Mara took the control and moved Priya's block until it touched Kade's, and entered the impact estimate off the replay. The model threw Kade's block backward across the line. Eli reset it and added the wall's measured flex, and the program refused.

UNASSIGNED LOAD.

Taren leaned in. "Assign him and the wall as one body for the six frames."

Nobody moved.

He kept his eyes on the model. "That's what he described."

Eli drew a boundary around both blocks. The warning went away. Momentum entered through Kade's block and left through the wall, and the model balanced, and Kade's headache sharpened, not from the screen.

The drawn line was wrong. "No."

Eli froze it.

He pressed two fingers above his right eye. "The wall wasn't part of me."

"Then what changed?" Mara said.

He stood, over Sera's expression, crossed to the wall, and took the control out of Eli's hand. The program had given every line an owner and every block a clean edge. Priya ended where he began. He ended where the wall began. The floor stopped at its lattice because the diagram needed separate colors. He erased the boundary around himself and the wall. Then he erased the line between Priya's foot and the floor.

The blocks stayed where they were. The relationships did not.

His hand started to shake. Mara took the control out of it. Sera killed the display, and the glass went dark, and Eli said, "Eight minutes," quietly, and Sera said, "Too long."

He went back to the bed and put both hands flat on his knees until they stopped. Taren had moved away from the wall. Nobody reached for the display. Sera pushed the takeout box across the desk toward him.

"Eat."

Rice, vegetables, chicken. "Is this yours?"

"It was."

"You took Eli's roll last night."

"Systems evolve."

He ate. Nobody asked him to explain anything else, and that helped more than he would have guessed.

At twenty hundred exactly he was on the roof, because the room had too many witnesses, with the bell's stay cable going up into the dark beside him.

She picked up on the first ring. Kitchen table. Folder. Mug. She looked at him the way she had looked at him in Room Four, head, hands, color of the face, and he let her finish.

"Word," she said.

"No structural injury. No conventional discharge. Pupils equal by the third hour. Headache two. Tremor intermittent, resolving. Cleared for housing, restricted from unsupervised field activity until further notice. Park's words. I wrote them on my hand."

She wrote them on the folder. "Who told you the restriction."

"The Director. In person."

"How long."

"Until they understand it. I asked if that meant forever. She said it could."

"Good," Elena said, which was not the word he had expected, and then, "I mean the honesty. Not the forever."

"I know which one you meant."

"I saw it," she said.

He went still. "You saw it."

"Three people from work sent it to me. One of the trauma surgeons asked if you were always like this."

"What did you say?"

"Yes."

"Betrayal."

She laughed, and it did not last long, and then she asked it the way she had asked the other thing two nights ago, with nothing folded up inside. "Do you want me there?"

It landed harder than he had braced for. He looked out over the island: training lights along the southern arenas, students on the bridges, the bell above him black against a blacker sky.

"No." Then fast, "Not because I don't want you. I mean—"

"I know what you mean."

He rubbed the back of his neck, where the thumb had been, where nothing was now. "I lost in forty-seven seconds."

"I saw."

"You said."

"You got up every time," she said.

He looked away from the phone. "That was the assessment."

"That isn't why I said it."

He knew. That made it worse, and he let it be worse, and did not reach for anything to put on top of it.

After the call he stayed. Forty-seven seconds. Officially a loss. Publicly a clip. Medically an unresolved problem with a restriction on it. Somewhere inside them, for an instant with no length, he had seen something real, and he did not know what it was. Sen knew more than she had said, and had said so. Eli had six missing frames and no access. Mara had questions she had not asked. Sera had food. Taren had looked at a model of the arena for a beat longer than the model needed.

He looked at his left hand. It was still. He closed it.

Tomorrow the academy was going to tell him what he was not allowed to do. Tonight, on a roof, under a bell that had not rung in fourteen months and ten days, he let himself have the simpler thing, the one he had spent seventeen years not saying where anyone could hear it.

He wanted to know what he had done.

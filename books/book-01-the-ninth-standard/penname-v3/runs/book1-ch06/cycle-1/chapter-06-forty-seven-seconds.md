# Chapter 6 — Forty-Seven Seconds

*Twelve. Mom says today somebody hits you and it's on the syllabus. I said that was always true at Northline. She didn't laugh. Get up when they put you down. That part's from me.*

The drone was hanging over his face when he woke.

Close enough that the rotor wash moved his hair. It held there, level, patient, the way it had learned to hold over Eli's palm the afternoon before, and when he swatted at it, it rose exactly the height of his hand and settled again.

"Bay One," Eli said from the dark by the door, dressed, tablet under his arm. "Ten minutes. Bring the shoes you've been refusing to open."

"It's not six."

"It's five forty. The assessment floor is grip-coated. Your sneakers will hold you for one step and after that it's a negotiation." The drone drifted toward the door ahead of him like something on a leash. "I'd rather you lost for interesting reasons."

The academy box sat on the shelf exactly as he had put it two days ago, a statement addressed to furniture. He took the lid off. Gray, a blue seam along the sole, and they fit the way things fit when a machine has measured your foot without asking. He laced them and felt like somebody wearing a decision that had been made about him.

---

Bay One was a low padded room under the near tower with a camera in each upper corner. Eli propped the tablet against a water bottle and put the drone in the air between them at shoulder height.

"It can't hurt you. It weighs less than a sandwich. It flies at your left shoulder, you get out of its way, and I tell you what you did."

"I know what I'll do. I'll move."

"Everybody knows what they'll do. That's the problem with them."

The drone came. Kade was back and sideways before it reached him, easily, four times running, and Eli got quieter with each one, which was the wrong direction for Eli to go. On the fifth Eli did not send it. He touched something, and the drone tipped a few degrees toward Kade and went nowhere, and Kade's shoulders were up and his weight was on his back foot before he had decided anything.

"There," Eli said. "That."

He turned the tablet around. Two traces, one from the drone's log and one from the corner camera. "It has to tip before it moves. Two rotors drop, then it travels. It can't skip the tip." A finger on the first trace. "Tip." A finger on the second, earlier. "You. One hundred and sixty milliseconds before it has done anything at all. You're not moving away from the drone. You're moving away from the idea of the drone. If I were the drone, I'd send it wherever you're about to be."

"You're both standing wrong," Sera said from the door.

She had coffee. She set it on the floor, walked to Kade, and moved his back foot out with the toe of her boot until it was under him rather than behind him.

"You stand like the floor owes you a favor."

"It's been fine."

"It's been unopposed."

Mara Vey was in the doorway in training black, on her way somewhere that mattered more than this, and had stopped. She watched the drone tip. She watched Kade hold, because he was ready for it, and lose the readiness on the next pass and rise onto his toes with both shoulders up around his ears.

"Both shoulders," she said. "Before anything has happened. You are announcing where you are going to be."

"I don't announce."

"You did. Twice." She came in and took the drone's place, one open hand up, nothing on her wrist lit. "I touch where you were going. You reset."

She touched where he was going. Three times. On the third he felt himself do it, the small climb of the shoulders, the weight already leaving the front foot, and he made it stop, and stood inside the stopping like a man standing in cold water, and watched her instead.

Her weight moved onto the front foot. It had to. She could not come forward without it, any more than the drone could move without tipping. He waited for that and only that, and when it came he went, not back but past her hand and through the space her weight had just left, and he was behind her with the room's dust turning in the air where he had been.

Mara did not turn around. "Once is weather."

He failed twice more, and on the third try did it again, worse, slower, and still before she had chosen for him. Sera picked up her coffee.

"That's the correction," Sera said. "That's all it is. Don't go looking for a second one before breakfast."

Eli read from a file. "Priya Shah. Six thousand two hundred and four. Standard One, confirmed. Primary strengths, acceleration control and balance disruption. Three years of prep combat before she got here."

Kade looked at Mara.

"Same House," she said. "Different floor. She is better than her number."

"Which foot?"

"Find out. It will stay with you longer." She was already gone.

He stood in shoes he had not bought, breathing harder than a drone deserved, annoyed that there was no time to go again, and a month ago the annoyance would have been the news.

---

They walked him over like a delegation. Anton Bell had watched Priya's last three bouts and narrated them with his hands on the bridge. "She doesn't hit you. She moves the floor, you go looking for it, and then she's where you looked." Nessa Kim said to stay off the center, where the plates were densest. Sera said both of them should stop helping. Eli said nothing about the model he had built overnight, which was how everybody knew it existed, and when Kade asked for the number Sera said, "Four. Percent," and Eli said the sample was emotionally irresponsible.

At the assessment wing the corridor forked, a ramp up to the seats and a door with a yellow line on the floor. Eli stopped at the fork with some last instruction in his mouth and closed it again. Sera had his collar. Mara touched two fingers to her own right shoulder, once, and Kade let his down, and the door shut on them.

The equipment bay was gray lockers, a medical station, and the yellow line running away toward a second pair of doors with noise behind them. The officer at the station read his band, said "Okafor" in the tone of a fact rather than an introduction, ran a scanner over the new shoes, fitted a sensor collar under his jaw, and slid two impact strips inside the suit against his ribs.

"Any pain right now."

"No."

"Headache since this time yesterday."

"No."

"Tremor. Dizziness. Visual disturbance. Field echo. Loss of time."

"That list gets worse as it goes."

Her hand waited above the glass.

"No."

She turned the screen to him. Three lines in gray, the color of a form with nothing in it.

MEASURED SAFE MAXIMUM: UNRESOLVED. ACTIVE FIELD AUTHORIZATION: OBSERVATION ONLY. CONVENTIONAL CONTACT PROTOCOL: NOVICE.

"Plain words. You may not start field action. If the room records an event, Instructor Hale ends the match. Shah is held to twelve percent of her measured ceiling, and the force she is allowed to put on you is keyed to your strips, not her tolerances. Two ambers on your collar and it stops. One red and it stops at once."

"Does she know all that?"

"She is reading it now."

Across the bay Priya Shah stood on a floor plate while a blue line of light climbed her from the ankles up. The file had said small. It had not said that she wore her braid pinned flat so nothing could be caught, or that she stood on the plate the way other people stood in their own kitchens. The officer said a number, and the plate lurched half a centimeter, and Priya did not. Her head stayed at the same height above the floor as if it had been nailed to the air. Six percent, the other direction. Still.

His own screen read PLATE RESPONSE: NONE. SUBJECT STABLE, which was the kindest way he had yet seen a machine say nothing.

"Last box," Okafor said. "This is an assessment, not an obligation to be struck. You may withdraw here, surrender in the arena, or ask Hale to stop at any moment. Withdrawal leaves your combat line unresolved. It does not touch your standing as a student."

The island kept building him doors with the handle on his side and standing back to see if he would use them. Nobody would ever replay a blank line.

"If I go in and lose in five seconds, what does the line say?"

"Whatever you did in five seconds."

"And if I don't go in?"

"Nothing. Until there is evidence."

He put his thumb on the glass. "Then I'd rather it said something I did."

Priya came off her plate and met him at the yellow line. Up close she had the stillness of a person who had been told all her life to settle down and had made it a skill.

"They put you on novice contact."

"Apparently I bruise."

"It means the exchange belongs to me, not the clock. So here are my rules. If you turn away, I break off. If you go down, you get room. If your collar goes red, I stop before Hale says a word. I say this out loud to everyone I'm about to hit, because saying it is how I make myself do it." She looked at the doors. "Half of them up there are going to say I was told to go easy on you. The other half are going to say I couldn't put you away. I'd like to just do my job."

"Then do it."

The doors opened on white floor, and she let him go through first.

---

The arena was a teaching room, not a broadcast one. White composite with a fine grip that his new shoes took hold of at once. Clear walls all around, a red boundary strip a long step inside them, two starting circles twelve meters apart. Above the west wall a booth, and behind its glass Dr. Park with her hands folded, and beside her with nothing in her hands at all, the Director of Asterion.

Of course.

A hundred first-years in tiered seats. Eli in the front row with the model he would not show anyone. Mara beside him, arms crossed. Sera behind them against the barrier, standing, the way she stood in rooms with exits. Three rows up, Taren Holt, assigned to no session Kade knew of, sat sideways in a seat like a man who had come in out of the rain.

Kade took the west circle. Priya took the east, looked at the board and then at him, and he watched her put whatever she thought of the booth somewhere he could not reach.

Instructor Hale stood at the control rail with a slate and the build of a man who had been struck for a living and had come to conclusions. "Controlled contact. No head strikes. No intentional joint injury. Field output capped at twelve percent of measured safe maximum. Match ends on pin, surrender, boundary exit, or my stop."

Kade raised a hand. "What counts as an event?"

"Anything this room can't explain." A beat. "You'll know, because I'll have stopped it."

The barrier sealed with a soft two-note tone. Red. Amber. Priya went up onto the balls of her feet once and came down. Kade did not. He watched her right foot, because it was the part that would have to commit first, and he was watching it when the light went green.

She was not there.

Something struck the center of his chest with a whole person behind it, and his feet left the floor, and then the floor was under his back and moving past him, and he was looking at the ceiling with no air in him and the clock saying three.

The suit had spread the hit; it was the surprise that had emptied him. He turned his head. Across twelve meters of white the circle she had started from had one pale scuff in it, no longer than his thumb. She had not simply run. The floor had given her something and she had taken it.

"Up, Mercer," Hale said.

Priya had stopped a body's length away. "You breathing?"

"Working on it."

She came again and this time he watched the foot. It loaded. He went the correct way, early, past the side she had chosen, exactly as the padded room had taught him, and the floor under his own front foot moved.

Not much. A tilt of a degree, there and gone, and his weight went where the tilt sent it and his stance went with it, and her forearm arrived at his shoulder and turned him, and he was down on one hip at ten seconds with a clear understanding of what Anton had been trying to say with his hands.

She did not move him. She moved the floor and let him move himself.

He got up slower. His eyes had stopped looking for her and started looking down. The composite. The lattice under it, which he could not see but could infer from the way the tilt had traveled. The base of the clear wall, where a row of small sensor lights had flickered when she pulsed the floor, faint as a heartbeat under a shirt. The pulse went somewhere. Everything did.

He set his back foot where Sera had put it, under him, and widened his base until it felt ugly, and waited.

The tilt came first this time, before she did, and he felt it arrive and did not go with it. His weight stayed inside the wide stance and the floor's small opinion passed through him and out. Then she had to actually close, on her own feet, and for the first time he saw her decide, and stepped, and got a fistful of her sleeve.

She turned inside his grip as if the sleeve belonged to someone else, and walked him. Three steps with his own weight doing the work, toward the red strip, and his heel found it and stopped, and he was standing on the last centimeter of the match at nineteen seconds with her hand flat on his sternum.

She took the hand away.

"You had it."

"Yes."

"Then finish it."

"They're not grading what I do." She stepped back to center. "Show them something."

*Nothing* came up as far as his teeth. It was the word he had given Mara at dinner, and Sera had heard what it was carrying, and he swallowed it and breathed.

He had been trying to solve Priya Shah, who was a three-year problem, with twenty-eight seconds and a blank line. Wrong problem. He looked at the room instead, the way he had once looked at a steel sphere in a chamber and refused to believe the chamber was the edge of anything. The floor took her pulses. The lattice carried them. The lattice tied into the footings of the clear wall, or the lights at its base would not have blinked. Her feet, his feet, the strip, the wall, the frame of the building around all of it. Not a list of objects. A chain.

She loaded the foot.

He moved on the load, not the launch, and was inside her reach before she had finished leaving the floor, and for one half of one second his hand was on her elbow and her eyes were wide and the whole room leaned forward in its seats.

Then she dropped her level and he went over her hip and the floor came up at twenty-eight.

The seats laughed again, and this time it was with him. He knew the difference. Priya's face came into the top of his vision.

"That was something."

"I had your elbow."

"You had my elbow." She offered a hand. He did not take it, and she nodded as if that were also correct.

Thirty-three. She came straight, no pulse, and he slipped the first touch and took the second, a controlled forearm into the ribs that bloomed amber on the collar and faded, and he kept his feet. He was learning inside the fight and could feel it, and it was not going to be enough. She had started three years ago. He had started at five forty.

He backed toward the west wall.

Priya slowed. "You're putting yourself in the corner."

"I'd noticed."

He was not going to beat her. That was settled, and settled things did not need his attention. He had perhaps ten seconds and a choice about how to spend them. He could give her one more elbow to take away from him, and he already knew what that taught. Or he could stand in the one place on this floor where she had exactly one move, where her pulse and her weight and the floor and the wall would all be in a line he could see at once, and let her make it, and watch.

It would be the hardest hit of the match. He knew that. He set his heel on the red strip anyway, with the clear wall less than a meter behind him, and felt the part of himself that wanted the clip to end here, and the part that wanted to know, and let the second one have the floor.

Forty-one. She went.

He watched all of it. The foot. The pulse leaving her foot for the floor. The floor giving it to the lattice, the lattice carrying it toward the wall, the wall's footing waiting for it, and her, coming, all her borrowed speed in a line that ended at his chest and, past his chest, the strip, and past the strip, the wall.

The cold thumb pressed at the base of his skull, exact as always, and did not let go. It ran forward. A line of cold went through his head to a place behind his eyes, and for an instant with no width in it the arena stopped being things and became the way things were held to each other. Her foot to the floor. Floor to lattice. Lattice to wall. Her momentum into him. Him toward the line. And somewhere in the chain a place where his body ended and the next thing began, drawn there because a diagram needed separate colors, and not because anything had ever asked.

He did not move a muscle. He moved the line.

Her palm struck his chest.

The wall boomed.

A sound like a door the size of a building being shut. Every light in the room went dark for a blink and came back. Priya stopped as if she had run into something, not him, something that had been standing where he was and had left, and her whole forward weight arrived somewhere behind him with a noise he felt in his teeth. The clear wall flexed outward and held. White stress lines lit through the floor around his feet like frost forming in fast motion, and faded.

He was standing. He had time to know it.

Then his knees stopped being his and he sat down hard on the composite, and the room narrowed to a tube with Priya's face at the far end of it, and Hale's voice said "Stop," once, not loud, and the clock froze at forty-seven.

The silence was the kind a hundred people make when they have all forgotten to breathe at once.

His hands were shaking. He watched them do it as if they were happening to someone else. The cold line behind his eyes had become a hot one without asking. Priya crouched a body's length away, exactly as she had said she would, and looked at him the way people had looked at him in Room Four when the score came up.

"What did you do?"

He looked past her at the wall. The stress lines were gone; the smart material had already spread whatever it had been asked to hold. "I don't know."

That was not quite true. He did not know what he had done. He knew what it had felt like, and what it had felt like was a fingertip on a tablet in a classroom, dragging a line where no line had any business going, and a screen going black. His stomach turned over.

Park came onto the floor at a run. Sen walked. People who frightened rooms did not need to hurry.

Park dropped to one knee with a light already in her hand. "Headache."

"Yes."

"Number."

"Four."

"Nausea."

"Two."

"Hand." He held it up and it answered for him. She looked into one eye and then the other, and her mouth did a small thing he did not like.

Sen crouched by Priya. "Are you hurt?"

Priya opened and closed both hands and rolled the shoulder that had done the pushing. "No. That wasn't me. Whatever that was."

Sen looked at the base of the wall, where the little lights had gone steady again. Then at Kade. Nothing in her face he could have read from a meter away.

Behind the barrier the whispering had started. Eli had both hands on the glass. Mara was on her feet. Taren had straightened in his seat and was not pretending anything about the chair.

Hale consulted his slate. "Match to Shah. Mercer unable to continue."

Kade laughed, which hurt. Priya looked offended on his behalf. "I hit him and the building moved."

"You remained combat capable."

"He did something."

"Yes," Sen said.

One word, and the whispering died as if a hand had closed over it. Kade looked up at her.

"What?"

"Not here."

He tried to stand. Park's hand landed on his shoulder with more weight than she looked like she owned. "Sit. Your pupils are two different sizes."

He sat.

A maintenance panel had lit on the barrier beside his head. STRUCTURAL LOAD EVENT DETECTED. SOURCE: UNRESOLVED.

The board ran the replay on its own, slowed. Forty-one. The foot. The pulse. The palm. And then not a flash, nothing a hundred people could point at, but a hole: the telemetry that traced every field exchange in the room simply stopped, six frames of it, and when it came back the wall was already carrying the load.

Six frames. The seats went from a murmur to a noise.

The replay froze. Red letters replaced it.

DATA FLAGGED — RESTRICTED REVIEW.

Kade closed his eyes. "Is that the kind of flag that comes with a folder?"

"No," Sen said.

"Good."

"It comes with several."

He let his head fall back against the wall that had, it seemed, caught something for him. Priya was laughing quietly behind her hand, and when he looked at her she took the hand away and said, "Sorry."

"For which part?"

"You're about to find out."

---

Medical kept him three hours and Park did not waste one of them.

She imaged his head, then made him walk a taped line, follow a light with his eyes and not his chin, name shapes on the wall as they came up, and answer questions a child could have answered until he missed one.

"Day of the week."

"Tuesday."

"It is Wednesday."

Behind her the last shape was still on the wall, a black thing with seven sides that he had named correctly and had taken far too long to name, and he heard the day he had just said hang in the room as if somebody else had said it.

"That concern you?"

"Yes."

"Good."

It had gotten out before he could dress it as a joke, and Park did not seem to want one. "A neurological test rewards performance. People learn which answers open the door. I am not interested in those. I need the ones that don't."

She stood him on a plate that leaned three degrees and his stomach tried to go on without him. He stepped off too fast. She held out a basin.

"Nausea."

"One."

"It was two on the floor."

"Improving."

"Your color says three."

He held the basin and did not use it and waited for the room to finish moving. She waited with him, which was worse than being argued with.

"Three."

She changed the record, and nothing else changed. No lecture. She lowered the plate to one degree and started the next thing. He had spent years treating pain as information other people used to stop him. Park treated it as a condition of the problem, and it did not make the room less frightening, but it made the truth marginally more useful than the lie.

"My mother," he said, when she reached for the next tray.

"Is on your file. I will call her when I have a sentence."

"Cold goes at twenty hundred. Hurt goes right then. That's the deal I made her." He nodded at the wall clock. "This is right then."

Park considered him exactly as long as it took to decide. "Two minutes. You do not look at the screen."

Elena answered from somewhere with a corridor in it. "Are you hurt."

"Yes." He had never once opened with that word to her, and he heard her stop walking. "Headache, four, now three. Hands shook. Eyes wrong. I'm in medical and the doctor's got a clock on me. I got hit a lot and that part's fine. This is the other part."

A silence with a hospital in it.

"Twenty hundred," she said. "With a doctor's word next to it. I want the word."

"You'll have it."

"Kade. You called."

"You made me."

"I made you promise. You called." And she was gone, and Park took the phone and turned it face down and said nothing about any of it.

By the third hour his pupils matched. The tremor had gone somewhere and might come back. The headache sat at two. No structural injury. No conventional discharge, from him or from anything in the arena. No explanation.

Priya put her head in the door on her way to an afternoon session, in academy blue with the braid down.

"You alive?"

"It's Wednesday. I've been informed."

"I watched it. You didn't move. The hit moved. I've been trying to say it some other way and there isn't one."

"They tell me I had zero output."

She looked at his hands. "You got better in there. First exchange, you should have been done. You gave me forty-four more seconds and I had to work for most of them."

"I lost."

"You did. I'm not sorry about that part." She pushed off the frame. "Lunch tomorrow. I'll explain the floor thing before Anton does it with his hands again."

He decided Priya Shah was going to be very hard to resent, and that this was going to cost him.

Park let him have the phone for ten minutes at half brightness. Three hundred and eighteen notifications. Eli, in a column: ARE YOU ALIVE. DON'T ANSWER, PARK SAID NO SCREENS. I HAVE THE TELEMETRY. I DO NOT HAVE THE TELEMETRY. SOMEBODY TOOK MY ACCESS AWAY, WHICH IS RUDE, AND INTERESTING. Mara, one line: *Tell me when you're cleared.* Sera: *Eat before you sleep.* Taren: *The forum named it. You'll hate the name,* and a link.

THE FORTY-SEVEN SECOND FIGHT. Fifty thousand views on a six-second clip while he had been naming shapes. He read three comments, and one of them was ZERO IS CHEATING, and he closed the forum and put the phone face down himself.

"Contraindicated," Park said.

*Thirteen. I've seen it eleven times. My mom's seen it four. The wall's the best actor in it. Are you okay. Answer that one.*

Sen came at the end of the third hour with a tablet, which he took for a bad sign, and stood at the foot of the bed rather than sitting, which he took for a worse one.

"You are cleared for housing. You are not cleared for field activity of any kind without supervision."

"For how long?"

"Until we understand what happened."

"That could be a long time."

"It could."

His shoulders came up. He made them come down, and she watched him do it, and he found he could hold what he thought of her and what he thought of the honesty in the same hand without either spilling.

"You said yes. On the floor."

"I said what I saw. An event that does not fit ordinary field transfer."

"That's a very careful sentence."

"Careful sentences are the only kind I have today."

"Two days ago you told me you knew more than the wall did and weren't going to say it that day. This is a different day."

"It is." She did not look away, and she did not fill the pause, and he could have drawn the outline of what she was not saying. "There is a word. You are not getting it from me today. When you do, it will come with the rest of the sentence, and not before."

The shape around an answer. Renn had made it in Room Four. She had made it in an elevator. He put this one on the pile.

"Go back to Meridian. Eat. Sleep. Tomorrow we talk about training."

"Restricted training."

"Yes." At the door she stopped. "You did not win that match, Mr. Mercer."

"I was there."

"You learned something anyway." A pause he did not like. "So did we."

The bedsheet was still strung between the two fourth-floor windows, WELCOME BACK, MEDIOCRITY, and under it SPEAK FOR YOURSELF, 612, and under that, new, in fresh paint a meter high: WALL, ONE. MERCER, NOTHING. Beneath it, in a small precise hand and a different color: SHAH, ONE. READ THE BOARD. He stood under it with the bell hanging over all of it and found he did not mind the sheet. He minded, slightly, how much he did not mind.

Eli met him at eight fourteen, said "Park signed for you?" and on Kade's nod put both arms around him for about a second and then reached past him for the door control as if that had been the whole idea, and Kade let him have it.

Inside, his room had been annexed. Mara had Eli's chair turned backward. Sera had his chair and a takeout box. Taren leaned on the wall by the window, the one place from which every face in the room was visible, and said, "Quorum."

Mara's eyes went to his hands first. "Shaking?"

"Not now."

"Headache?"

"Two." Her eyes came up, and he answered the thing she had not asked. "Yes. Park has all of it. So does my mother."

"Fifteen minutes of light," Sera said, "and then I don't care what's on the clock."

Above the far bed the wire drone was gone. Eli had built the arena in the air in pale lines instead: two small figures, the floor, the lattice under it drawn as a grid, the clear wall, the building's frame beyond, a line at every place one thing touched another.

"The gap is six frames," Eli said. "Not five, not seven. I had it for eleven minutes."

"You had it how?"

Eli found something to adjust. Taren coughed. "House systems have gaps in them."

"Those answers arrived together."

"The wall takes the load," Eli said, louder. "She puts it into you and it comes out of the wall, and nothing I have between those two points explains the trip."

Sera pointed her fork at him. "His head is not a project."

"It's a little bit a project."

Mara had not looked away from Kade. "What was it like?"

He looked at the pale lines. The little figure at the strip. The palm. The wall.

"Like it was all one thing."

Taren's face changed. Barely. Kade caught it because he watched faces, and put it in a drawer he did not have a label for yet.

"All what?" Mara said.

"Her. Me. The floor. The wall."

"That is force transfer. It is always all one thing."

"No." He shut his eyes and it was there, close enough to make his stomach tighten. How did you describe a thing that had not been sight or touch or any of the ordinary doors into a person. "It's not that they were joined. It's that the places where they stopped being each other were somebody's decision. And nobody was standing there deciding."

Nobody said anything. Eli had gone still. Sera put her fork down. Taren looked at the model a beat longer than a model needed.

Kade opened his eyes. "Out loud that sounds bad."

"It does," Mara said.

"Thanks."

"Run it," Sera said. "He's over there, the light's over here."

Eli ran the plain chain. Priya's figure pushed off the floor, arrived at Kade's, and Kade's went backward across the strip. "That's the world." He entered the wall's measured flex, and the model thought about it and threw a warning into the air between them.

UNASSIGNED LOAD.

"Draw them as one body," Taren said. "Him and the wall. For the six frames."

Nobody moved. He kept his eyes on the model. "It's what he said."

Eli drew a line around the small figure and the wall together. The warning went out. The force came in through one and left through the other and the model settled, satisfied, and the headache behind Kade's right eye put a finger on him.

"No."

Eli froze it.

"The wall wasn't part of me. Don't give it to me. Take one away instead. The line between her foot and the floor."

"That line's just physics."

"Then take away the physics and see what's left."

Eli erased it. The figures stayed where they were. The grid stayed. The wall stayed. But the model no longer knew who owned anything, and instead of a warning it simply stopped, every pale line holding still in the air, waiting for someone to tell it where one thing ended.

"That," Kade said. "That's what it was like."

His hand had started. He watched it go and could not make it stop. Mara said his name. Sera killed the model, and the room was only a room again, dim, with four people in it who had all stopped talking at once, and Eli said, "Six minutes," very quietly, and Sera said, "Too many."

He sat on the bed with both hands flat on his knees until they agreed to be hands. Taren had come off the wall. Nobody reached for the light. Sera slid the takeout box across the desk with two fingers.

"Eat."

Rice. Vegetables. Chicken. "This was yours."

"It was."

"Last night you took Eli's roll."

"I redistribute."

He ate. Nobody asked him anything else, and that helped more than anything anyone could have asked.

At twenty hundred he was on the roof, because the room had run out of air, with the bell's stay cable going up into the dark beside him and a pencil in his hand.

He had taken it out of the drawer without deciding to. It was the first thing he had ever written with it: Park's words, on the back of a Meridian duty sheet. No structural injury. No conventional discharge. Pupils equal by the third hour. Headache, two. Tremor intermittent, resolving. Restricted from unsupervised field activity until further notice.

She picked up on the first ring. Kitchen table. Folder. The look that went over him head to hands to color and back.

"Word," she said.

He read it to her off the sheet. She wrote it down as he went, the way she read a dosage back.

"How long."

"Until they understand it. I asked if that meant a long time. She said it could."

"Good," Elena said, and then, "I mean that she said so. Not the rest."

"I know which part you meant."

"I saw it. Three people from work sent it before I'd finished my shift. One of the surgeons wanted to know if you were always like that."

"What did you tell him?"

"Yes."

"Betrayal."

She laughed, and it did not last, and then she asked it the way she had asked the other thing two nights ago, with nothing folded up inside. "Do you want me there?"

It landed harder than he had braced for. He looked out past the phone. Training lights down the southern arm. Students crossing bridges. The bell above him, black on a blacker sky.

"No." Then fast, "Not because I don't want you. I mean—"

"I know what you mean."

He rubbed the back of his neck where the thumb had been and where nothing was. "I lost in forty-seven seconds."

"Eleven times, according to Darius. Twice, according to me." A pause with the kitchen in it. "You got up every time."

He looked away from the screen. "That was the assessment."

"That isn't why I said it."

He knew. It made the thing worse, and he let it be worse, and did not reach for anything to put on top of it.

After the call he stayed. Forty-seven seconds. A loss, officially. A clip, publicly. Medically, an unresolved problem with a restriction fastened to it. Sen had a word and had admitted she had it. Eli had six frames of nothing and no way back in. Taren had looked at a wire drawing of a wall one beat longer than a wall needed.

He turned the pencil over. Tooth marks near the tip, from a mouth he did not remember. Seventeen years in a box, and tonight it had written down what a doctor could prove.

Tomorrow the academy would tell him what he was not allowed to do. Tonight, on a roof, under a bell that had not rung in fourteen months and ten days, he let himself have the small true thing he had spent his whole life not saying where anyone could hear.

He wanted to know what he had done.

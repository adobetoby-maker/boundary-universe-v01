# Chapter 3 — Asterion

Darius's first insult arrived at three minutes past three, while Kade Mercer was still trying to work out what held the aircraft up.

*One. Your hair.*

He had asked for forty and told Darius to space them out, and Darius, who had never followed an instruction he could improve on, had apparently decided to start at the airfield gate and pace them by a clock only he could see. Kade put the phone in his pocket without answering. Answering would have been coaching.

The aircraft sat on a restricted pad at the far end of the municipal field, past the crop sprayers and the sheriff's helicopter, past a chain-link gate that had opened for his mother's car only after the flight officer scanned the band on Kade's wrist. It was long and gray and had the general proportions of a jet, and somebody had taken the wings off it. No engines either, none he could find. Four rings sat flush in its underbelly, each about the width of a truck tire, glowing the pale blue of a pilot light, and under each ring the concrete was scrubbed clean in a perfect circle while the rest of the pad wore its normal skin of dust. The dust had opinions about where it was allowed to lie. He found that more unsettling than the missing wings.

Seventeen years of learning how things stayed up. Lift over a wing. Thrust from behind. A load path you could trace with a finger from the weight to the ground. He looked at the gray aircraft and could not find the path, and the part of him that had always been able to find it stood there like a dog at a door that had been bricked over.

"Stop trying to see it," Elena said. "You'll pull something."

She was not in scrubs. She was in jeans and the gray coat, and she had traded a day shift to be here, which meant she would be on the floor at St. Vincent by seven tonight paying it back. She had not said so. He had read the schedule on the fridge.

"It doesn't push on anything," he said.

"So they tell me."

"Everything pushes on something."

"Then it pushes on something you can't see, and you can stand here with your mouth open until they leave without you." She crouched, the way she had crouched two nights ago at the kitchen table, and ran the duffel's slider open and shut, once, twice, listening to the teeth mesh. He had squeezed the slider plate closed with needle-nose pliers and tested it forty times since. She tested it anyway. It was how she loved things.

"It holds," she said, and stood, and did not let go of the strap.

The bag between them was the whole argument from the store, settled. Green canvas from a grocery promotion older than he was, one handle re-sewn in thread that did not match, and inside it three shirts, two pairs of jeans, socks, a charger, and an eleven-dollar collared shirt with the tag still on. Everything else on the academy's list, the formal sets and the training sets and the weather shell, was page eleven's problem. *Property and financial responsibility of the academy.* He had left the whole list unbought on the strength of one sentence in small type, and he was about to find out whether the sentence was load-bearing.

His pocket buzzed. *Two. The way you walk like the floor owes you money.*

"Is that him?"

"He's got thirty-eight to go."

"Tell him I said to eat something that didn't come out of a machine."

"He'll take that as an insult."

"Good." She took Kade's wrist and turned it over and checked the band as if it were an IV line. Then she took out her own phone and held it up. The screen was a photograph of the inside of a silver folder: her square chart print, four conditions, three signatures.

"That's in your messages," she said. "If anybody hands you a slate, anything with a place to sign, you find this first and you read them side by side. If they don't match, you don't sign, you call. I don't care what time it is."

"I'll be asleep at some point."

"Then you'll wake up." She put the phone away. "The rest you know. You call. Not text. I don't want to read you, I want to hear you. Every day this week."

"Every day is a lot of days."

"It's seven. I've counted."

He looked at the aircraft so he would not have to look at her doing this. The rings brightened a shade, and the four clean circles under them got cleaner, dust sliding off their edges the way water slides off wax.

"He used to say—" Elena said.

She stopped.

Kade kept his eyes on the rings. In seventeen years his mother had never once started a sentence with those three words. His father was a photograph, a stopped watch, an industrial accident, a *that part* that had never grown a *this part*. He was not somebody who *used to say*.

"Say what."

"Nothing useful." She had found the duffel strap again and was working the webbing between two fingers. "It's not important. Kade. Look at me."

He looked.

"If you want to come home after the evaluation, you come home. If they tell you the evaluation has turned into something with a different name, you call me before you agree to the name. And if you're safe and it's good there, you're allowed to say it's good. You don't owe anybody a bad review." Her thumb found the staple in his cuff and stayed there. "I'll be at work when you land. Leave a message. I'll hear it on my break and it'll be the best thing that happens all night, because it will be."

"Mercer." The flight officer, from the foot of the stairs, tablet under one arm. "Two minutes."

She hugged him. Not the squeeze she gave him going out the door to a shift. The whole thing, arms and weight, and he stood in it for a second the way you stand on a floor you are not sure about, and then he put his arms around her and stopped testing.

"Go," she said into his shoulder. "Before I say something else that isn't useful."

He picked up the bag. He did not look back from the top of the stairs, because she would have been standing exactly where he left her, and he would have had to carry that on board with everything else.

---

Twelve seats. Four other people. Three wore dark jackets with a small silver crest at the collar, and one of those, a broad young man across the aisle with a nose that had been broken once and set by somebody competent, watched the duffel go under Kade's seat, then looked at Kade's face, then went back to the reader on his lap without any change of expression at all.

Kade had braced for the other look. He knew it from scholarship interviews and the front office at Northline: the quick sort, the file opening behind somebody's eyes. This was not that. This was a man who had seen a bag and a person and had no further use for either. It was the most restful thing anyone had done in his direction since Tuesday.

"Null-lift departure," the flight officer said from the front. "Harness stays on until your seat light clears. Symptoms go to the call panel, not to your neighbor."

*Symptoms.* Kade filed it.

There was no roll. No whine building behind him, no moment where the vehicle gathered itself. The floor simply decided it was somewhere else, and his stomach stayed on the pad for one long second before it caught the flight. His hand was on the armrest without his having sent it there. Through the window the airfield fell away, hangars, the gate, a gray coat with a woman in it not moving, and then the city, flat and brown and stitched with roads, and the whole time nothing pushed. He listened for it. Nothing pushed.

*Three. You're on a flying lunch tray and you're still going to find something to complain about.*

Darius was wrong. He was not going to complain. He was going to find the load path or die trying, and the distance between those two activities was narrower than Darius thought.

He gave up on the airframe after twenty minutes and started on the cabin. The overhead bins had latches. The tray tables folded. The floor tilted, faintly, as the aircraft banked toward the coast, and the drawstring of his hood, which hung down past his collarbone where he had been worrying it, did not swing. The floor went over four degrees, five, he could feel it in the seat, and the string hung straight toward the deck as if the deck were still level.

He watched it the way he had watched the steel ball at Station Seven. The bank ended. The string had never moved.

"Which one of you is lying," he said, to the string and the floor.

"Neither." The broad student had not looked up. "The cabin gets told where down is. Your ears aren't in the cabin. They're in you."

"So it lies to the cabin."

"It decides what the cabin's allowed to feel. Different thing." He turned a page. "Doesn't help if you're going to be sick. Bag's in the seat pocket."

"I'm not going to be sick."

"Good. The bags are worse than the aircraft."

Kade looked at the string again and did something he could not have described. Some inward shift of attention, not toward the string, toward the gap between the string and the floor, the place where a rule was being applied. He was thinking *somebody decided where that goes* and precisely as he thought it a pressure arrived at the base of his skull, small and cold and exact, like a thumb pushed against the bone through a glove. It lasted a breath. Then the aircraft leveled and there was nothing there and there had, plausibly, never been anything there.

He sat very still.

*You tell whoever's closest,* his mother had said, on the airfield, in the kitchen, in every version of every conversation about his head. The closest person was reading. The call panel was above the seat, a plain white button with a picture of a hand on it.

He pushed it.

"Cabin." The flight officer's voice, from a speaker by his ear.

"Mercer. Seat nine. I had a pressure. Back of the skull, during the bank. It's gone now."

A pause. Keys. "Logged, nine. Onset on the bank, resolved on level. Vision effects, nausea, numbness?"

"No."

"If it recurs you press again. Cabin out."

The speaker clicked off. Across the aisle the student had looked up at last, and there was something in his face Kade could not read and decided to call approval.

"You're the first new one I've seen use that," the student said, "without being told twice."

"I was told twice. Not by you."

That got a short exhale, not quite a laugh. "Rian Okafor."

"Kade."

He waited for the surname. He waited for what came after it, the eyes changing, the wrist coming up, *Zero*. Rian nodded once and went back to his reader, and Kade understood that Rian had known who he was since the bag went under the seat, and had made a decision about it, and the decision was that it was Kade's business.

*Four. You're about to make friends with the one person on that plane who doesn't want any.*

He typed *you don't know that*, and deleted it, and put the phone away.

*Five. You deleted something. I can tell from here.*

He ate what the seat console gave him, a sandwich and an apple and a bar in a crested wrapper, because being angry at food had never once made him less hungry. The desert went gray and then went white at its edge, and then the edge was the Pacific, and the aircraft came down.

It did not spiral in from above. It came down early, out over open water, until the sea was two hundred meters under the window and then less, wrinkled steel with light skidding across it, and then it flew straight at a wall.

He saw it before he understood it. A white line on the horizon that thickened into a cliff, twenty meters of pale seawall taking the swell on its face, and the aircraft did not climb until the wall was close enough that he could see the tide stains, and then it lifted and the wall dropped under them and the island was simply there, all at once, the way a room is there when a light comes on.

It curved away from him in both directions, a crescent six kilometers or so from horn to horn. On the northern arm, towers, glass and pale stone with the afternoon on them. Between the towers and the sea, terraces stepped down in green. Along the southern arm, set into the ground like coins somebody had pressed into wet clay and left there, a chain of silver discs with their roofs shut. Arenas. He had watched bouts from inside those discs since he was six, on a cracked wall screen in a district gym, and had never once thought about them from the outside, as objects with a top that sat on land.

And the Spire. Dark glass, a lattice climbing it like ivy drawn by an engineer, and at the top the tower widened instead of tapering, a fist on the end of an arm, the way it did on every broadcast the whole country stopped for.

Below them, close enough to see faces, a student stepped off the roof of a tower and walked down its face. Walked. Boots on the glass, body out level with the ground, hands in her jacket pockets, as if the wall were a road and she had somewhere to be at the bottom of it.

Kade's hand had flattened against the window without permission. He took it back.

Rian was closing his reader. "You'll get the look at the foot of the stairs," he said, not unkindly. "About a week and it stops."

"What look?"

"The one I've been not giving you for an hour."

---

The doors opened on warm air that smelled of salt and cut grass and some flowering thing he had no name for. He came down the stairs onto Asterion Island with a grocery-store duffel on his shoulder and, for a length of time he did not try to measure, was nobody at all.

It did not last. He watched it not last. A girl at the edge of the pad glanced up, glanced at the inside of her wrist, and her face changed; she said something to the boy beside her and his wrist came up; and it went across the pad like streetlights coming on down a road, one and then the next and then the next. Nobody stared. Everybody looked. Rian passed him at the bottom of the stairs, said, "Log it if it comes back," and was gone into the crowd without turning.

The man waiting at the pad's edge wore a gray uniform with blue trim and had close-cut hair, a tablet, and no display lit on his wrist at all. He did not need one.

"Mr. Mercer. Enrollment Officer Cole." He handed Kade a slate. "Your evaluation packet. Medical intake tomorrow at zero six fifteen. Baseline at zero seven hundred. Dinner in the arrival residence from eighteen hundred."

The top line of the slate read MERCER, KADE — PROVISIONAL FIRST-YEAR.

"That's not the word I came with."

"It is the word the statute uses."

"The statute says evaluation."

"A provisional student is a student under evaluation, Mr. Mercer. It is a container, not a verdict. Page three."

He found page three walking, because Cole had started walking, at one speed, without looking to see whether he was followed. CONSENT TO BASELINE EVALUATION. His mother's voice said *find it first*, so he did not read the academy's paragraph. He went to the end of the page. And there they were: four conditions in square chart print, scanned in, three signatures under them, clipped to the top of the consent like the seal on a jar.

"You attached her page."

"Your mother attached her page. It reached the enrollment office nine minutes after the transport request cleared." Cole did not slow. "She then called to confirm that it had been received. Twice."

Kade read the consent against the photograph on his phone, side by side, on a moving walkway between two buildings with the ocean going by on his left. Blood. Imaging. Physical measures. Passive field exposure while standing in an instrumented room. Nothing that asked him to produce anything. At the bottom, in type one size smaller than the rest: *further evaluation stages require separate consent.* It was the first fine print he had ever met that made a thing narrower instead of wider.

He signed for the baseline. Only the baseline. He handed the slate back.

"How many people read the whole page?"

"Fewer than sign it."

The walkway ended in a plaza, and the plaza was where Asterion stopped being a broadcast.

The trees were wrong. Gray-barked, with narrow leaves that showed white on their undersides when the wind came off the water, so that a gust ran through the plaza like a hand through fur. Channels of water were cut into black stone underfoot, fast and silent, and students stepped over them without looking down. A groundskeeper's cart went by on no wheels, a hand's width off the paving, the groundskeeper walking beside it with one hand on the rail as if it were a dog on a lead. Nobody looked at the cart. Kade looked at the cart. He looked at all of it and kept his face where he kept it.

In the center of the plaza stood a fountain that was not one. A column of water rose out of a basin as thick as a tree trunk and three meters tall, and it stood there, it did not fall, and six students in training gray stood in a ring around it with their eyes shut. A chalk line on the stone said DO NOT CROSS. As Kade watched, the column wavered, and one of the six said "Mine, sorry," in a strained voice, and the water sagged a meter and then climbed again, and the instructor beside them made a mark on a tablet without any sign of caring whether it had fallen.

"What is that?"

"Introductory coupled control. The water is the assignment. Six of them are holding one boundary. When one loses it, the others take the load without being told." Cole had not stopped. "You would be assigned to it in the spring. If you are here in the spring."

"Introductory."

"The cart on a track is in the same building. It remains on the syllabus."

Past the fountain a clear panel stood up out of the paving like a sheet of ice, and Kade took it for signage until the names began to move. ASTERION COMBAT LADDER. Thousands of lines scrolling, rank, name, year, House, a column of Roman numerals he had no key for yet. Here and there a number ticked up or down while he watched, and the line above it shivered. At the top of the scroll, unmoving, as if bolted:

1 — CROSS, JULIAN — YEAR 1.

He stopped. Cole, six paces on, stopped too, and came back, and Kade suspected Cole had known which panel he would stop at before the aircraft landed.

"First in the whole school. A first-year."

"The board shows a visitor what it believes the visitor is. It believes you are a first-year, so it shows you first-years." Cole touched his tablet and the header changed. INCOMING FIRST-YEAR COHORT — 10,482 STUDENTS. "This is the ladder you would be measured against. If you were measured."

Ten thousand four hundred eighty-two. Northline had forty-two students, and he had known the name of every one, and their siblings, and which of them had somewhere to sleep.

"Where's the bottom?"

"Scroll."

He put a finger on the glass and pulled. Names blurred. He pulled again, and again, and the numbers went by like mile markers, four thousand, seven, nine, and somewhere in the nine thousands he took his hand off the glass, because every one of those lines was somebody's name and not a number, and he had been the name on enough lists.

"It's public."

"Every line. Every hour of every day."

"Even down there."

"Especially down there, Mr. Mercer. Nobody checks the top. They already know who is on it."

*Six. Bet you're already looking for your name on something.*

---

Cole took him as far as the landing-complex desk, where a woman scanned the band on his wrist and dealt across the counter, like cards, a meal card, a temporary room key, a folded paper map, and a lanyard of blue cloth with PROVISIONAL on it in white. Then Cole stopped walking.

"Arrival Residence C. Eighth floor. Your slate has the map."

Kade waited for the rest. There was no rest.

"You're not taking me."

"I have walked a great many students to that door. Very few could find it again in the morning without me." Cole clasped his hands behind his back, which was apparently what Cole did instead of sitting down. "I will be one pace behind you. I will not speak. Go."

So he went, with the slate open and Cole's footsteps exactly where Cole had said they would be, across a bridge the map called Central, enclosed in glass between two academic blocks with water sixty meters below. The route the map drew was clean. Central Bridge, then straight through a courtyard belonging to something called HOUSE MERIDIAN, the largest residence on the eastern shoulder, and out its far side to a smaller block above the landing pads, marked C.

The Meridian gate was iron and standing open, with a reader beside it, and when the reader saw his band it made a low soft tone, once, the sound a machine makes when it is sorry but not very.

ASSIGNED RESIDENTS ONLY.

Behind him, Cole said nothing, precisely as promised.

He stood at the open gate and looked through it at the courtyard, where a bell hung in a frame and three students sat on a wall eating something out of paper, and he thought about walking through anyway. The gate was open. Nothing physical stopped him. Everything else did. He backed up.

The map, asked again with his band's limits switched on, went gray across Meridian and drew a thinner line around its outside. An exterior stair, metal, bolted to the flank of a long building the map called MERIDIAN DINING, down two flights to a service walk and up again on the far side. Longer. Outdoors. Nobody in academy blue was using it. He passed a kitchen worker on the landing drinking coffee from a paper cup, who nodded at him the way you nod at somebody carrying their own bag.

At the top of the second flight the map went green, and Arrival C stood in front of him, and Cole's footsteps stopped.

"You can talk now," Kade said.

"Note the stair."

"I noted the stair. I'll be noting it every time it rains." He turned around. "And the other direction. If I wanted the pad. The transport desk."

Cole regarded him without any change at all.

"Your band opens the transit desk," he said. "The desk does not require my presence, or anyone's. It requires your band and a departure time. The clinic reassigns the slot."

"That's it."

"That is the procedure."

"You're not going to tell me what I'd be throwing away."

"I am not paid to hold opinions about what you would be throwing away, Mr. Mercer. I am paid to have you at a clinic door at six fifteen if you are on this island. Whether you are on this island is the one line on tomorrow's schedule that belongs to you and not to me." He inclined his head, a centimeter. "Eighth floor."

---

Arrival Residence C had a lobby the size of Northline. Not Northline's lobby. Northline.

There was a rail. That was the first thing he understood about the place, because it was the thing that came for his bag. A polished track ran along the lobby wall at hip height, and luggage rode it: hard-shell cases the color of expensive cars, garment bags, a cello, a trunk with brass corners and its own attendant walking beside it with one hand on the lid. A boy his age stood at the intake point with four matched pieces, watching a handler print a tag for each, and beyond him a girl in academy blue was explaining to her mother, with real distress, that the ocean was on the wrong side of her room, while two people in gray moved her boxes onto a pallet that floated.

The handler held out her hand for the duffel.

He gave it to her. She set it on a plate in the counter, and the plate said eight point one kilograms, and she looked at the number and then, briefly, at him, and printed a tag and looped it through the handle with the mismatched thread, and set the duffel on the rail between the brass-cornered trunk and a garment bag longer than he was.

"Is there a second piece?"

"That's the piece."

She nodded and touched the rail, and the duffel went, green canvas gone gray at the corners, riding away between the trunk and the garment bag toward a door in the wall, and for about five seconds he was ashamed of it. He felt it happen. A hot, specific thing up the back of the neck. The bag had not changed since the airfield. He had not changed. The only thing that had changed was what it was riding next to, and he stood in the lobby and hated the five seconds, every one of them, and did not get them back.

*Seven. You packed the shirt with the tag on. Don't tell me you didn't.*

Eighth floor. Two beds, two desks, a glass wall with the Pacific behind it, and on the near bed, stacked in clear packaging, two formal sets, three training sets, a pair of shoes, a weather shell, and a thing folded small enough to be an approved field underlayer. Page eleven had kept its word. He stood and looked at the stack for a while, and then a hatch he had not noticed opened in the wall and the duffel slid out of it onto the floor by the door, tag fluttering, and lay there.

The second bed was bare. The room had been built for two, and he was the only thing in it that had not come in packaging.

He did not unpack.

He thought about it. He looked at the drawers, deep and lined, which would have held everything he owned in the top one with room to spare, and he understood that he could put three shirts in a drawer built for thirty and the room would look exactly the same, except that the bag would be empty, and an empty bag was a bag that had arrived. He left it by the door, zipped.

His phone was at eleven percent. Darius had been busy. He crouched and opened the side pocket for the charger and found, on top of the charger, a photograph he had not packed. Him and his mother on the front step two summers ago, both squinting, her hand on the back of his neck. Behind it, a square of paper folded once, in the print she used for charts.

*Call me. Eat food. Ask questions. You are allowed to leave. You are also allowed to stay. — Mom*

He read it standing up. He read the last two lines again and found that the second of them was the one that was hard to look at, which he had not expected. Then he put the note in the desk drawer, face down, under the paper map, so that anyone who ever opened the drawer would find a map and not his mother, and he plugged in the phone and zipped the pocket and left the bag where it lay.

*Eight. You found the note. She told me she was putting a note. Your face right now.*

He typed *stop*, and sent it, which was a mistake.

*Nine. He answered. Thirty-one to go and he answered.*

---

He went to dinner alone, because it was one of the things he was good at.

The arrival residence dining hall held two hundred seats and eight stations and more languages moving through the air than he had heard in one room in his life. A strip of display ran the length of the wall above the stations, showing hours and menus. He took a tray, and food, and a table by a pillar with his back to it, and had eaten a third of a meal better than any Northline had served in his time there when a girl with a blue lanyard sat down across from him without asking.

"The site has you as a clerical error at three to one," she said. "I've decided to believe the site. It makes you restful."

She was short, with a great deal of curly hair tied up off her neck, and she had brought her own tray, and she was already eating.

"Imani Bell." She said it, and then said the next thing the way other people told you their town. "Heart surgery at fourteen. There's a device in my chest that keeps the rhythm honest, and nobody knows whether it argues with field coupling, because nobody's ever had one and tried. Tomorrow at seven they find out. Medical Coupling Review, Clinic Two. You?"

"They haven't told me."

"They haven't told any of us. That's what the blue means." She pointed her fork at his lanyard, then her own. "White is enrolled. Blue is *we'll see.* There's a dozen of us in this building, give or take. We eat together because nobody else knows what to say to us, and we don't know what to say to each other, so it's efficient."

"And you sat here because."

"Because you're blue, and you're eating alone by a pillar with your back to it like somebody's coming for the tray." She chewed. "Also the site. If you're a clerical error I want to have met you before they find the clerk."

He had nothing for that. He ate.

Two more blue lanyards arrived and sat without ceremony. A tall boy in a knitted hat put down two full trays and started on the second without a word, and a girl with a stylus behind one ear had her slate open before her tray touched the table. Imani did not introduce them. They did not introduce themselves. Kade understood that this was the arrangement.

"He's fasting," Imani said, of the boy with two trays.

"He's eating."

"Blood draw at six fifteen. Nothing after midnight. He's banking." The boy raised his fork in confirmation and went on. "You're six fifteen too. It was on the door list."

Kade set his sandwich down. He had read *zero six fifteen* on the slate and not the line under it, because nobody had ever written him a fasting instruction in his life, and his eyes had not known to look.

"Then you should eat that," the boy said, the first thing he had said, "instead of putting it down."

He picked the sandwich back up.

The girl with the stylus turned her slate around without being asked. Two columns of figures. "Regional certification. Seven control bands. They said they can't convert my scores to their scale without watching me do it again, so I converted them myself in the lift." She tapped the right-hand column. "I'm not in the top thousand. I'd like to be told that by a person and not a lift."

"Would it help?"

"No." She turned the slate back. "It'd be mine, though."

He felt that land somewhere in himself and did not look at where.

Above the stations, the strip flickered and changed. PROVISIONAL BASELINE GROUPS, and under it names and places and times, and the table went quiet the way any table goes quiet when the sheet comes round. Six forty, academic control. Seven ten, south arena four. Imani's name under MEDICAL COUPLING REVIEW — CLINIC TWO, and she looked at it for a long second and went back to her food.

Then his own name, on a line by itself, with nobody under it.

MERCER, KADE — CONTROLLED BASELINE — SUBLEVEL ACCESS.

The hall did not go silent. It went thin. He felt it more than heard it, the conversation at the nearest tables losing its middle, and a wrist coming up here and there like a hand at the back of a classroom. The boy in the hat looked at the strip, and then at Kade, and then went back to his second tray, and Kade would remember later that this was the kindest thing anyone did for him that night, and that it had cost the boy nothing, and that it had been done anyway.

The two who came over from the next table wore white lanyards. The boy walked like somebody who had been good at things in public for years. The girl with him had a display lit on the inside of her wrist that she did not bother to hide.

"Sublevel," the boy said, pleasantly. "That's where they put the ones who break the instruments."

"I'll tell them you said so."

"Look. I'll be straight with you." He sat on the end of the bench, uninvited, one leg out into the aisle. "There's a lot of money on you. Not mine, mostly. And everybody's going to be guessing until seven tomorrow. I can save you the night." He laid one hand flat on the table between the trays, and the silver plate at his wrist caught the light. "I put a small field through this. Low. Nothing you'd feel through a shoe. You sit there and tell me if it feels like anything. If it does, you're something. If it doesn't, you're not. Either way you know before they do."

There it was. He had not expected it to be offered. He had expected to be asked to *do* something, to make a fork turn, and to have nothing to turn it with. This was the reverse, and it was worse, because it was the exact shape of the thing he wanted. An answer that would be his. Tonight, at a table, from a person and not from a lift, before anyone behind a glass wall could have it first.

He felt himself lean in, an inch. He felt the boy see it.

"Not through this table," Imani said.

She said it to her tray, not loudly. The boy glanced at her.

"It's low. You won't feel it."

"There's a device in my chest," Imani said, "that nobody has ever put a field through. That's a sentence I've said to four doctors this week, and I'm going to say it to a cardiologist at seven, and I'd like her to be the first one to try, and I'd like her to have a screen." She looked up, and her voice did not move at all. "So. Not through this table."

The boy's hand stayed on the table. Kade looked at it, and at the field he could neither see nor feel that would come out of it, and at the far wall, beyond which he could not see a sealed room under the ground with instruments in it either, and could picture it.

"No," he said.

"You don't want to know?"

"I want to know so badly I nearly let you." That was the truth, and it cost him something to put it on the table where they could all see it. "But you'd know first. You'd have it on your wrist before I had it in my head, and then it'd be yours, and I'd be reading about myself again." He nodded at the girl's lit display. "That's recording."

"That's a clock."

"It's been the same time for a minute and a half," said the girl with the stylus, not looking up from her columns.

The boy in the hat stood. He was very tall, standing, and he did nothing with it except stand, and lift his empty first tray, and set it down again on the end of the bench where the enrolled boy's leg had been, so that the leg had to move.

"Bench is full," the boy in the hat said, and sat down.

The enrolled boy took his hand off the table. His face did one brief honest thing before he put the public one back over it.

"Seven o'clock, then. Everybody finds out."

"Not everybody," Kade said. "I signed for a room with a door."

They went. Around the table the hall found its noise and came back to its middle, and the girl with the stylus wrote something in her columns, and Imani took a bite of her bread and chewed it and swallowed and said, "You'd have let him."

"Yes."

"Because of the money?"

"Because he'd have told me." He looked at his sandwich. "Nobody ever tells me. I'm the one line on that board I can't scroll to."

Imani thought about that with her mouth full. "Then tomorrow, when they tell you, it's yours," she said. "Not his. That's the whole difference. It isn't a small one."

"You didn't have to say that about your chest."

"I say it every day. It's cheaper than being polite." She set her fork down, and it was, he saw, slightly bent, and she looked at it and took another from the middle of the table and did not comment. "I'd still hate going home."

"You don't have to."

"No." She picked up the new fork. "But I might."

They stayed through dessert, because dessert was there. The girl with the stylus rejected the route the map gave her to academic control on the grounds that it used two moving walkways and she was not going to arrive at a control assessment with her balance argued with, and drew another one, up two flights of stairs, on the back of a napkin. The boy in the hat finished his second tray and looked at Kade's protein bar until Kade slid it across. Imani told him he had the face of somebody who was going to ask the meal desk who paid for the meal, and he said he had already read the card, and she said she knew, she had watched him read it, she just wanted to hear him admit he had looked.

He had looked. PROVISIONAL SERVICES — NO FAMILY LIABILITY, the card said, and under it the line he had read three times: *evaluation meals, lodging, and medical intake are covered whether or not the student enrolls.* He did not entirely believe a card. But between the stations and the tables he had watched a boy in a jacket that fit as if it had been sewn on him and a girl with a repaired sleeve carry the same food away from the same scanner, and that proved nothing, and it made the card something he could check. He would be checking. He had a week of dinners to do it in.

At a quarter to eight the others left to find clinics and arenas and one walkway to avoid on principle, and he took his water to the end of the table by the glass and let the room happen around him. A cup went off the edge of a table two over and stopped a hand's width from the floor, and rose, and went back where it had been, and the boy who had knocked it did not stop talking. Kade laughed into his water, and the laugh surprised him, coming out of him in this room.

*Ten. That was a real laugh. I felt it from here. Thirty left. Going to sleep. Eat something.*

---

He called her at eight. The leave-a-message idea had lasted exactly as long as it took him to picture her hearing it.

She picked up on the first ring, which meant she had been carrying the phone in her scrub pocket against policy. The sound behind her was the sound he had grown up with, monitors and the intercom and somebody's cart.

"Are you hurt."

"No. I'm on an island. It has fruit."

He heard her breathe out. Then he heard her put the phone against her shoulder and say something to somebody about a bed in four, and come back. "I've got two minutes. Talk fast."

So he talked fast. He gave her the seawall coming up out of the water and the girl walking down the tower and the fountain that stood up. A man named Cole who would not tell him what he would be throwing away. A girl with a bent fork and a device in her chest, who had told an enrolled student *not through this table* in a voice that did not move. He gave her the pressure at the back of his skull on the bank, and that he had pushed the button and it was logged and it had not come back, because she would have found out, and because he had promised, and because it was easier to say into a phone than to her face. He did not give her the five seconds in the lobby. Those were his.

"Logged," she said. "Good. That's good, Kade." And then, with the intercom going behind her, "You're talking fast."

"You said to."

"Not that fast."

He looked out through the glass. Across the courtyard the ladder scrolled on its panel, ten thousand four hundred eighty-two lines of somebody, every hour of every day.

"It's a school," he said. "It's just a school. It's got a bottom and a top and a strip on the wall that tells you which one you are. And I've been here five hours and I've already—"

"Kade, hold on."

Something on her end. A voice, and her voice answering it, and the sound of her walking. When she came back she was moving.

"I have to go. Tomorrow night, twenty hundred, I'm off. You call at twenty hundred. Exactly. I'll be sitting by it."

"Exactly."

"And whatever you've already, you can tell me then." Her voice did the thing, the one that meant the joking part had used its time. "I heard the start of it. It's allowed."

The line went. He stood with the phone at his ear a second longer than the call, and put it in his pocket.

---

The room was dark when he got back to it, and the glass wall was full of the island lit at night. The Spire was a column of dim gold with the lattice black across it, and far down the southern arm one of the arenas had its roof open, light coming out of it like a lid off a pot. He did not turn the room lights on.

He set an alarm for five forty on a phone at sixty percent. He took the top training set off the stack on the bed and peeled the packaging off and hung it on the back of the chair by the door, gray with a blue seam, and it was the first thing in the room with the academy's crest on it that he had touched with the intention of putting it on. Then he moved the duffel from where the hatch had dropped it to a place beside the chair, on the floor, zipped, the handler's tag still on the handle, the eleven-dollar shirt inside it with its tag on too.

He stood and looked at the two of them. The chair with the academy on it. The bag on the floor with his mother's stitching on the handle and everything he had come with still inside.

Both true. He could carry both true as far as six fifteen. He had carried worse pairs than that for years without a chair to hang them on.

After six fifteen there was a sealed room under the ground with instruments in it, and people behind glass who were going to find out what he was. And for once in his life, when somebody told Kade Mercer what he was, he intended to be standing in the room when they said it.

# Chapter 9 — Meridian Night

*Twenty-three. Mom says there's a party on a roof and you've agreed to go. She said it the way she says a lab result. Don't stand near the edge doing math.*

The number had changed overnight, and Eli had it up before Kade had finished sitting down.

"Eight thousand one hundred fifty-nine," Eli said, and turned the tablet so the corner of the model, in the color it used for facts, faced him across the toast. "Eight hundred and twelve places. In a week. I'd like it on the record that I did not build this thing to do that to me at breakfast."

Kade looked at it. The number was flat and black and the same height as every other number in the column, and something under his sternum went up a step anyway. Seven ranked fights since the review. Five wins, none of them pretty. Two losses that had each taught him something on purpose. The ribs had stopped talking except on the tower stair. It had been the kind of week that felt like nothing while you were inside it and then, at the end, put a comma in your life.

"Say the eight thousand part," Eli said. "It's important for your development."

"Eight thousand one hundred fifty-nine."

"See, you said the whole thing. A month ago you'd have said the last three digits and hoped."

At seven fifty-five Taren Holt looked up through the Commons glass at the bell and said, "Fifteen months, five days," to nobody, and half the room groaned on schedule. Then he stood up on his chair, which was new.

"By order of nobody with any authority, tonight is mandatory."

"That word doesn't mean what you think it means," a second-year called from the landing.

Taren pointed at him. "You'll be there."

"Probably."

"See?"

Sera took the chair out from under him on her way past with a bin of cutlery, and he came down on his feet, which was the only part of the exchange anyone would have bet on.

Meridian Night, Eli explained, happened whenever the House moved up in the inter-House standings or won anything worth pretending mattered. The official reasons tonight were a second-place engineering relay, three strong combat placements on the week's ladder, and a fourth-year team passing an emergency-response practical on the first attempt. The unofficial reason was that it was Friday.

By lunch the thing had grown committees. Nessa had food. Anton had heaters. Two second-years had the music, because Eli had been near the sound cabinet once in the first week and the fire panel still had opinions about it.

"That happened once," Eli said.

"The alarm happened twice," Sera said, without looking up.

Kade had been assigned nothing.

He found that he minded. At Northline, not being asked had been the whole of freedom. You were left alone, and being left alone was the reward for making yourself difficult to ask. Here the same silence read differently. Everybody with a job belonged to the roof. Everybody without one was a guest, and a guest, at some point in the evening, was going to be handed a plate by somebody who had paid for it.

So at four o'clock he went up without telling anyone why, and found Anton Bell on his back beside a heater with the access panel open and both hands inside it.

---

"Blue case," Anton said, without looking.

Three tool cases sat against the parapet. Kade brought the blue one.

"Other blue."

"That one's green."

"It was blue before the roof got to it." Salt had bleached the lid to something between the two. Kade traded and knelt on the far side of the heater, where the wind came off the water and went through his jacket without slowing down.

Inside, one connector had furred white around the contacts like frost on a fence wire.

"That looks bad."

"Advanced technical assessment." Anton fitted a driver into the housing. "Hold the frame. If this screw strips, Nessa gets the last working indoor unit and everybody on the north side develops character."

Kade braced the metal. Anton leaned into the driver and the screw did not move.

"Heat the housing," Kade said.

"And expand the screw with it."

"Freeze the screw, then."

"With what?"

Kade looked past him to the greenhouse, where a climate pipe ran along the inside of the glass with white vapor curling the length of it. Anton followed his eyes and knew what he was looking at before he had finished looking.

"You're not dismantling agriculture for a party."

"I was evaluating options."

"Evaluate holding still." Anton changed the angle of the driver, tapped the handle twice with the heel of his hand, and the screw broke free with a sound like a knuckle. He held out a replacement lead without looking. Kade matched it to the empty socket, and it was the wrong shape, and Anton traded it for another without a word.

The second one fit.

"You could label those."

"I did." Anton turned the first lead over. A strip of tape on its underside read NOT THIS ONE.

"That's hostile."

"It works." He closed the circuit. The heater came up with a low hum and the air over the grille went from nothing to something Kade could put his hands in. Anton had made the repair. Kade had kept the frame from tipping while it happened. It was a smaller job than he had come up here wanting, and it had still needed two people, and he found he did not have anything to say about that.

The roof door banged open and Nessa Kim backed through it with a sealed food crate held against her chest.

"Clear route."

Kade stood and reached for it.

She turned a shoulder to him. "I have this."

"I was helping."

"Help with the other one."

The other one was twice as wide, on a wheeled dolly just inside the door, with a printed label warning that it contained frozen fruit bars and was not to be tipped.

"You gave me the easy one."

"I gave you the one I don't already have." She hitched hers higher against her shoulder. "Do you want to help, or do you want to be recognized helping?"

Anton became very interested in the heater controls.

Kade took the dolly handle. "People here are aggressive about social interpretation."

"House living," Nessa and Anton said, in the same breath, without having planned it.

At the threshold the front wheel caught the raised strip. He tipped the load back the way you would with any cart, and the top crate started to slide, and Nessa stopped it with her heel without setting down the one in her arms.

"You need a spotter."

"For frozen fruit?"

"For the threshold." She crossed first, put her crate down, and guided his front wheel over the lip with the toe of her boot. It had not been a strength problem. It had needed somebody standing on the other side, and he decided not to say that it sounded designed.

Sera met them at the folding tables with a checklist on a clipboard, because of course there was a clipboard.

"He volunteered," Anton said.

"I can see that."

"You told me I was assigned nothing," Kade said.

"You were."

"Now I'm moving food."

"Yes."

He waited for the rest. Sera checked off frozen storage.

"An invitation isn't a debt, Mercer." She said it the way she said heels. "If you want a job, take a job. Don't confuse that with the price of being let upstairs."

She handed him the clipboard and went to intercept Eli, who had appeared from nowhere and was standing near the sound cabinet with the expression of a man not touching it.

Kade looked at the list. Beside HEATERS, Anton had written *probably*. Beside FOOD, Nessa had written *unless Taren arrives early*. He put a check beside TABLES, for the four he had carried, and looked at the mark longer than a check mark deserved. Not a payment. A thing he had done, on a list of things other people had done, in the same handwriting as theirs.

---

By dark the roof had become somewhere else. Light ribbons ran from the antenna mast on the mechanical housing out to the greenhouse frame and back, the two heaters had become six, and somebody had carried three couches up the tower stair and set them facing the water as if the water were a screen. The folding tables held enough food to supply a small evacuation. The music was loud enough to fill open air and quiet enough that people who wanted to talk could. Kade liked that less than he had expected to, which he had learned meant he liked it more.

The first twenty minutes were harder than the simulation hall. He knew what to do when somebody wanted to hit him. He had almost no practice at standing near people who expected nothing from him but that he stand there.

Around him the House argued about House Trial strategy, and a professor who supposedly graded lab reports on handwriting despite everything being digital, and whether the north residence tower was haunted or only badly wired. Nobody asked him what he could do. It went on for nearly six minutes. It was a record.

A third-year named Mina found him at the end of it with a marker and told him to sign the underside of the food table, because everyone who survived their first month did.

He got down on one knee and looked. Hundreds of names covered the frame, some dated years back, many with a rank beside them that had been struck through and replaced by a better one, and a few by a worse one and then a better one again. Near the center brace, in a confident hand, somebody called J. Ramos had written nine thousand eight hundred eighty-two, drawn a line through it, written one hundred fourteen beside it, and added STILL BAD AT LAUNDRY.

"Do I have to put the rank?"

"Only if you want to enjoy crossing it out later."

He wrote it. KADE MERCER — 8,159. It looked different on a table than it did on a board. On the board it was a fact about him. Here it was a thing he had put somewhere with his own hand, and he found he wanted to add something so the number would not be alone.

WASHING MACHINE 1 — MERCER 0

Mina laughed. "Room."

A broad-shouldered fourth-year dropped to the roof beside him and slid under the table with his own marker while two friends crouched to watch. He reached past the brace to a name almost hidden behind it. DEV PATEL — 6,440. He struck one line through the number and wrote 1,998 beside it, and his friends made enough noise that people at the heaters turned around.

"You waited until tonight?" Mina said.

"Meridian Night rules."

"There are rules?" Kade said.

"Many," Mina said. "We invent them when enforcement becomes necessary."

Dev capped the marker and ran a thumb over the old number. "My first ladder instructor told me to leave it until I crossed two thousand."

"Why?"

"He was nineteen hundred and something."

"Educational integrity."

"He also said I'd want proof the first number happened." Dev looked at Kade's fresh line. "Don't let anybody talk you into erasing yours. Crossing out is different."

His friends hauled him upright by the wrist and the three of them left for the food line, arguing about whether one thousand nine hundred ninety-eight made him responsible for buying anything. Kade stayed under the table one more breath. His own number had not improved because somebody else's had. It looked less final anyway.

Near the greenhouse door somebody had dressed an old brass history board in paper streamers. FOUNDING STANDARD EIGHTS, in letters worn soft, with eleven small portrait medallions around the words, the faces too old and too small to read in ribbon light. Below the eleventh, where the tape crossed, the brass dipped.

He lifted the tape with a fingernail. A twelfth circular recess, the same size as the others, empty, with an old mounting screw still seated at its center.

"Do not dismantle House history on your first Meridian Night," Mina said behind him.

"There are twelve mounts."

"There are eleven portraits."

"I can count."

"Congratulations. Asterion admission validated."

"What was in the twelfth?"

She glanced at it the way you glance at a light switch that has never worked. "No idea. That thing predates the House charter. Probably a donor plaque." She pressed the streamer back over the recess with her palm. "Food."

He let himself be turned toward the food. One spare screw in an old board was not a mystery. Asterion simply contained a great many things nobody could explain without getting annoyed, and he had been adding them to a list for weeks, and the list did not need to come to a party.

---

Sera found him by the ventilation stack ten minutes later. "You look alarmed."

"I'm evaluating exits."

"Normal behavior at a party." She handed him a fizzy fruit drink in a paper cup.

He tasted it. "What is this?"

"Legal."

"Low bar."

"Meridian standard."

Across the roof Taren stood in the middle of a group telling a story with his whole body. Mara sat on the low greenhouse wall listening to somebody, wearing the expression of a person who could leave at any moment and was letting you know it. Eli had drifted toward a table where somebody had taken a speaker apart, which was the one place on the roof Sera had told him not to go.

Kade was considering the stair when a voice beside him said, "You're Mercer."

He turned. Tall, neat, in an academy jacket that fit too well to be standard issue. Kade knew the face from the plaza glass. Cal Reid. One hundred eighty-three. He had scrolled past the number weeks ago, in the part of the ladder that had nothing to do with him.

"Last I checked."

Cal smiled without any heat in it. "You climbed fast."

"Gravity reversed."

"I watched Shah."

Kade's shoulders came up a quarter inch, and he felt them do it, and left them there because putting them down would have been worse. "Lots of people did."

"Not the whole thing. The end." Cal moved half a step in, to the distance where people stood to talk quietly or to make you take a step back. "Whatever that was, enjoy it before Sen takes it away."

Kade's face put on the automatic smile, the one that closed a door from the inside. "Thanks for the concern."

Cal looked down at the paper cup. "Meridian really will adopt anything."

Heat came up his neck. He knew the next version of himself, could feel it stepping forward. A sentence sharp enough to make Cal's next sentence sharper, and then the one after, until there was something on the roof that he could later say somebody else had started. At Northline that boy had been the whole personality. It had never been a decision. It was only where his feet went.

His feet stayed. It was not virtue. He was still deciding, and the deciding was taking one beat longer than it used to.

"Reid," said a voice behind them.

Cal's face changed before Kade had turned. Julian Cross stood three steps away with a plate.

Rank one was disappointing in person. Same age as everyone else. Dark hair. A plain black training shirt with a fray at the collar. Nobody standing with him, no dramatic stillness suggesting an orchestra had begun playing somewhere behind him. He looked like somebody who had come up for the food.

"Cross," Cal said, straighter.

Julian's eyes went once between them and did not stay on either. "You're blocking the food."

Cal looked behind himself. He was, barely. "Right." He moved, and kept moving, toward people who owed him laughter.

Julian stepped past, took two dumplings, and looked at Kade. "You're Mercer."

"That's becoming a theme."

"Sorry. Julian."

"I know."

"Of course." No arrogance in it. No performance of not being arrogant either, which would have been worse. It made Kade suspicious, and he let it.

Julian nodded after Cal's back. "He does that to everyone he thinks might eventually pass him."

"I'm eight thousand places away."

"Cal plans long-term."

Kade laughed before he had decided to, which he had not done in front of a stranger in some time. Julian bit into a dumpling and considered it with the attention of a man examining a sample.

"These are excellent."

"You interrupted a status confrontation for dumplings?"

"I was hungry."

"You're a disappointing rank-one stereotype."

"I get that a lot." Julian's smile sharpened by a degree, the first thing Kade had seen him do that matched the thirty-eight seconds. "Do you know why he wanted to do that up here?"

"Because it's cheaper than doing it on a floor."

"Because on a floor it's on the record."

"Do you know everybody's rank?"

"Mostly."

"That seems unhealthy."

"It's useful."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

"No," Julian said, and seemed to enjoy that. "They aren't."

A student passing behind him said, "Cross. Your father called the House office again," and went on without waiting to see what it did.

It did something. Not fear. A tightening, all at once and everywhere, and then control laid over it so fast that a person who had not spent his whole life reading adults' faces for the temperature of a room would have missed it. Kade missed nothing, and pretended to, and Julian saw the pretending and let him have it.

"Thanks," Julian said, to the space where the student had been.

"Family," he said to Kade.

"Dangerous institution."

"Sometimes."

The one word had enough weight in it to make Kade revise something. Rank one had a father who called the House office, and it was not the first time, and the House office knew to tell him.

Somebody called Julian's name from the heaters. He lifted the plate a few centimeters, which was apparently how he said goodbye. "Good luck with the climb, Mercer."

"Planning long-term?"

"I plan everything long-term." He went.

Sera arrived beside Kade with the timing of somebody who had heard all of it from behind the ventilation stack. "You look betrayed."

"He was nice."

"Tragic."

"I had a whole personality ready for him."

"You'll get to use it on somebody." She nodded across the roof. "Watch."

He watched. Julian joined a group by the heaters, and inside a minute three people had turned toward him without being asked, and he was following every conversation at once with attention that never drifted, the same attention that had bought three pieces of information for a price inside a white circle. Nice was not the same as harmless. That helped more than it should have.

The other thing helped too, and he turned it over after Sera had gone. On a floor it was on the record. Cal had wanted the thing said where no board would write it down, and Julian had put a plate in the way. That was a kind of decision Kade had not watched anyone make before, and he filed it with the rest.

---

The party did what parties did. A group by the couches started a game called First Impression, guessing strangers' ranks from posture and clothes, and it lasted six minutes before somebody put a Standard Five upper-year at four thousand and the upper-year became personally invested in disproving the method. Then Taren climbed onto the greenhouse wall with a stack of empty paper cups under one arm.

"Relay."

Sera looked up from the first-aid kit she had laid out on the chess table bolted to the parapet. "No fields near the food."

"No fields anywhere. Pure athletic excellence."

"You brought cups."

"Accessible athletic excellence."

He laid out the course while objections were still forming. Start at the west heater. Carry one cup around the antenna mast, under the ribbon line, up the three steps to the greenhouse terrace, and back down to the line. Each runner handed the cup to the next. Crush it and your team was out.

"Why?" Julian asked, from beside the dumplings.

Taren held up a cup. "Because Meridian owns four relay batons and Engineering currently has all of them."

That satisfied more people than it should have. Teams formed by proximity, accusation, and Taren changing a rule whenever one side looked too strong, and Kade found himself in a line with Mara, Eli, and Mina, across from Anton, Nessa, Cal Reid, and a fourth-year he did not know.

Cal looked down his line and then across. "This is balanced?"

"Mara's on theirs," Taren said.

"Exactly."

"Their anchor is Eli."

Eli lifted one finger. "I reject the premise."

"Balance restored."

Thirty seconds to set an order. "Mina," Mara said, "then me, then Kade, then Eli."

Kade had been reading the course. The mast turn was tight. The ribbon hung low enough to catch anybody who went under it upright. The three steps were the only place one runner could pass another, and the first runner on the other side would be Nessa, who had carried crates through that door six times today and knew exactly where the lip was.

"Put me second. If their first runner's slow I can pass on the steps."

"And meet Mara coming off the handoff," Mina said.

"Not if she moves."

Mara held out her hand for the cup. "You say that before every competition you aren't prepared for."

"That can't be true."

"You've been here four weeks."

"Small sample."

"Order stays."

Across the way Cal arranged his team by rank until Nessa moved herself ahead of him. "I've been through that door six times. You asked which roof this was."

Cal stepped back without an argument, which Kade noted.

Taren raised both hands. "No fields. No furniture. No appeals to rank, faculty policy, family influence, or the spiritual authority of Director Sen."

"That happened once," somebody called.

"It worked."

"Start it," Sera said.

His hands dropped and Mina and Nessa went. Mina reached the ribbon first and ducked a stride early, which cost her the stride. Nessa stayed tall until the last step, folded nearly double, and came out ahead. They hit the greenhouse steps together, and Nessa took the handrail and swung herself around the turn on it.

"That's equipment," Mina shouted after her.

"The building's available to everyone."

Nessa crossed first and passed to Anton. Mina came in half a second behind, and Mara took the cup without looking down at it.

Anton had the longer legs. Mara took the tighter line. They reached the mast together and Anton went wide around the concrete base, and Mara put one hand flat on the concrete and turned on it without losing anything, and Kade leaned forward before he knew he had. He knew that foot. Heel uncommitted until the turn had chosen itself. She had been putting her shoe beside his and saying choose, then step, for a week, and here it was, at speed, on a roof, for a paper cup.

She cleared the ribbon, took the steps two at a time, and reached him a pace ahead of Anton. The cup hit his palm.

"Don't plan the opening," she said.

Then he was moving, and Cal was moving across from him, and it was one hundred eighty-three against eight thousand one hundred fifty-nine with no field on the roof and nothing between them but a paper cup, and for once the line over his gate did not matter, because there was no gate.

Cal went hard off the line and had the mast first. Kade let him. Cal took the turn on the inside, tight to the concrete, and his own shoulder made him shorten one step to keep from touching it, and the outside opened for exactly as long as it takes a person to shorten a step. Kade went through it. They reached the ribbon together and Cal ducked, and Kade went sideways instead, and the streamer touched his hair, and the greenhouse steps were in front of him.

The first two he took fast. On the third he held. Rear heel down, weight not yet spent, the decision still open, and he could feel Cal arriving on his left and wanting him to turn that way, and he let Cal's approach choose the angle, and then he chose the other one. The foot landed clean. Not because Mara had told him where to put it. Because she had shown him when to decide.

He reached the line half a step ahead and held the cup out to Eli.

Eli was not there.

He was three meters to the left, at the top of the greenhouse service stairs, waving. "Efficiency shortcut."

"The course goes around the mast."

"The rules said up the greenhouse steps. These are greenhouse steps."

Taren, on the wall, looked delighted. Sera did not. Kade threw the cup. Eli caught it against his chest, turned, and took the narrow stairs, and the other team's fourth-year followed the marked course and came off the terrace two body lengths behind.

"We're winning," Mina said.

"He's going to fall," Mara said.

Eli did not fall. The stairwell door opened outward, and a second-year came through it carrying a tray, and Eli stopped. The cup did not. It left his hand, struck the door frame, and on the way down took two clean cups off the stack Taren had left on the step, and all three landed in different places.

The fourth-year crossed the line.

Anton cheered. Nessa accepted the result as if she had expected nothing else. Cal looked at Kade across the course, no warmer than before, and for the moment without a useful thing to say.

Eli came down holding one flattened cup. "The route was clear when I assessed it."

"People move," Mara said.

"That's a design flaw."

Taren held the surviving cup over his head. "Official result."

Kade could have argued. The service stairs were not on the course. The door was outside interference. His team had been ahead when the plan changed. He could feel each of those lined up and ready, the way sentences had lined up for Cal, and he looked at the three crushed cups on the roof instead.

"Our equipment failed."

Mara's mouth moved at one corner. Eli looked wounded. "Our equipment made an independent strategic choice."

Sera took the rest of the stack away from Taren. "This House is structurally unfit for recreation."

"Rematch next month."

"No."

Half the roof began proposing replacement equipment, and for twenty minutes nobody cared about his score or his rank or Zero or the last six seconds against Priya Shah, and he did not notice the absence until somebody in another conversation mentioned the national exam and he realized he had forgotten himself for a while. That was new. He did not know yet what to do with it, so he ate.

---

Somebody turned the music down. Taren was still on the wall.

"First-month stories."

Half the roof cheered. The other half complained in the specific key of people who intended to participate.

Kade looked at Sera. "What is this?"

"Public humiliation with historical value."

"Meridian has excellent traditions."

Taren pointed at Mina, who groaned and stood. "First month, I challenged six hundred because I thought losing to somebody good would make me look ambitious."

"What happened?" somebody called.

"I woke up in medical." She raised a finger over the laugh. "And dropped eighty places for unsafe decision-making."

Anton had gotten lost in the Spire and sat through half a graduate seminar on orbital structural dynamics before admitting he was in the wrong room. Nessa had cried in her first Perception lab because everyone around her was describing colors in the field and she could not see one; three weeks later she had learned that hers came as pressure, not color, and had stopped apologizing for it. A third-year had started at nine thousand seven hundred and spent six months convinced the number meant admissions had made a mistake. A second-year had failed the same Conduit Theory exam twice.

Sera refused until the roof started chanting her name. She stood as if it cost her something.

"First month, I left campus twice."

The noise dropped. Kade looked at her.

"Why?" somebody asked.

"Because I'd decided everybody here was unbearable."

"That remains true," Eli called.

Sera pointed at him. "Case study." People laughed. She let them finish. "Second time, an instructor told me if I walked out again I could keep walking."

Kade's stomach tightened around nothing.

"What happened?" Mina said.

"I stayed. Because I wanted to prove he wasn't the one who got to decide."

She sat. No lesson. No inspirational ending. The story went in with the others and the roof let it.

Taren pointed at Kade. "New person."

"No."

"Tradition."

"I don't respect tradition."

"Liar. You signed the table."

Everyone looked at him. There were three clean routes off the roof and he had checked all of them before the relay. He stayed on the couch.

"Fine."

The roof quieted. He looked at all of them, forty faces in ribbon light, and not one of them was waiting for him to be good at this.

"My first month isn't over."

"That's technically true," Eli said.

"I came in last. In the whole year."

"We know," several people said.

"Helpful."

He had a dozen. The foam blocks. The notation that came out as a drawing. Mae Larson putting him on the strip in front of forty people who had come for the number. The gray shirts. Any of them would have done, and gotten the laugh, and cost him nothing.

He said, "I thought being bad at things here would feel like proof I shouldn't be here."

It came out more honest than he had loaded it. Nobody laughed. He felt the joke rise, the way it always did, bright and ready and useful.

Mina said, "And?"

He looked at the roof. "And it mostly feels like homework."

That got the laugh, and he let it, and the honest part stayed under it where he had put it. His mother was counting the times he told the thing. That made three.

Taren lifted a cup from the wall. "Meridian."

"Meridian," the roof said back.

He did not know if the toast was old or something Taren had made up last spring. It did not matter. For a few minutes everybody's rank had been less interesting than the thing they had once been terrible at. He had never been in a school that kept its failures on purpose. He liked it. That concerned him.

*Twenty-four. Mom says you used the word committees about a party and sounded like somebody who lives somewhere. I told her not to get used to it. She said too late.*

---

Later, when the music had gone quiet on its own and the roof had come apart into smaller groups, he found Mara alone at the east rail.

He considered leaving her there. She said, without turning, "If you're going to stand there deciding whether to talk to me, decide faster."

He walked over. "Do you sense indecision?"

"No. You reflect in the greenhouse glass."

He looked. There he was in the black panel, directly behind her.

"Less impressive."

"Usually."

They stood. Below them the island went down in lit terraces to the water, the training towers marking the skyline, an arena's lights sweeping slowly across the swell, and out past the breakwater the running lights of cargo ships moving along the horizon like patient stars.

He nodded at a cluster of buildings beyond the upper residences. "What's that?"

"Research."

"Restricted?"

"Some."

"Do you actually read every orientation document?"

"Yes."

"That feels like cheating."

"It's called reading."

"Strong branding."

She glanced at him. "You climbed again."

"Eight one fifty-nine."

"You were eight nine seventy-one last week."

He looked at her. "You checked."

Nothing in her face moved. "I track anomalies."

"That's almost flattering."

"It wasn't."

He smiled. The quiet came back, and this one was less awkward than the first, which bothered him more than awkwardness would have.

She looked at the water. "Why Northline?"

It landed without warning. His smile stayed where it was. "Building availability."

"You know what I mean."

"Why do you care?"

"I don't know yet."

That stopped him. Not pity. Not curiosity dressed as concern. Only accuracy, offered at whatever it cost her, which was not nothing.

He leaned on the rail. "I got good at making school optional."

"How?"

"Didn't turn things in. Argued past where I should've stopped. Left class. Made teachers choose between ignoring me and fighting me, and most people would rather ignore you."

"Why?"

He looked at her. "Do you always ask the easy ones?"

"No."

The joke was forming, bright and useful, and he could feel exactly how it would go and how she would let it. He thought about his mother on the bench outside the ambulance bay, counting.

"I liked being the reason," he said.

She waited.

"If I failed because I didn't care, I never had to find out what happened if I did."

It was too exposed the second it existed. He reached for the joke.

Mara said, "That sounds exhausting."

He blinked. Then he laughed, not because it was funny but because it was exactly right, the way a foot is right when it lands where the turn wanted it.

"Yeah," he said. "It was."

The corner of her mouth moved. She drank from her cup. "My father would call that inefficient risk management."

"Your father continues to sound delightful."

"He likes systems."

"So do I."

"No."

He frowned.

"You like finding where systems are wrong."

That was different enough that he went quiet. "Maybe."

"My father likes systems because he thinks they can be made correct."

"And you?"

She looked back at the water. "I like knowing where I stand."

He looked through the roof door at the Commons panel below, cycled to the ladder, the top of it readable from here. "Seven."

"That isn't what I meant."

He wanted to ask what she had meant. Before he could, a shout went up from the far side of the roof. Eli had turned the dismantled speaker into something that made smoke. Sera's voice: "Navarro!" Eli's: "It's technically functioning!" A cloud of harmless white vapor rolled across the food tables, and Julian Cross stood beside the speaker holding two disconnected wires, looking genuinely offended by the machine.

Kade laughed, and Mara laughed, at the same instant. He noticed. He wished he had not. She noticed him noticing, and her face reset in the time it took the vapor to reach the rail.

"Good night, Mercer."

She went. He stayed on the rail.

Taren arrived beside him with timing that should not have been possible. "No."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

"I was going to ask if you wanted another drink."

Taren grinned. "Also, Mara Vey."

Kade groaned. Taren laughed. "You're terrible at subtle."

"I wasn't doing anything."

"Exactly. You usually do three things at once to avoid doing one honest thing."

Kade looked at him. Taren's grin stayed easy. It was a precise observation, more precise than a person had any business making about somebody he had known four weeks, and Kade put it in the file beside the note about nine thousand one hundred twelve wanting his audience.

"Do you study people professionally?"

Taren lifted his cup. "Everybody at Asterion does. Some of us admit it."

His wrist lit. He glanced down, and the display was gone before Kade's eyes reached it.

"Expecting somebody?"

"No."

Smooth. Too smooth to mean anything yet, and Kade let it go the way you let go of a thing you intend to come back for. Taren wandered toward another group, already talking.

Across the roof Julian was helping Eli disconnect the speaker while Sera issued instructions neither of them followed fast enough. Mara had joined the cleanup without being asked. Somebody started the music again. Below the parapet the bell hung dark in its frame above the courtyard, fifteen months and five days, and nobody up here was counting.

The roof was loud and messy and full of people, and every one of them was a possible attachment to something that could be taken away. His first instinct was to go down the stair before that became a problem. He knew the instinct now. Knowing it did not make it leave. It only gave him a choice, which was more than he had owned a month ago.

He walked toward Eli. "Do not touch that wire."

Eli looked offended. "I know exactly what I'm doing."

The speaker sparked. Julian stepped back. Sera closed her eyes. Mara said, "That was predictable."

Kade laughed.

At midnight the House lights dimmed on their own schedule, and nobody left. The party only got quieter. He helped carry two tables down and found his own name under one of them on the landing, and touched the ink with his thumb, still there, still fresh enough to smear, and did not smear it. A stupid thing. A small one. He was starting to understand that those were the ones that got you.

Then he went back up, and stayed until the roof was nearly empty.

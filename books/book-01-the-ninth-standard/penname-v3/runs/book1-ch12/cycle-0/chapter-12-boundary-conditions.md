# Chapter 12 — Boundary Conditions

*Thirty. Mom says the floor has a name now and it's yours. I said that's what happens when you break something first. She said that's exactly what she's afraid of.*

Park had run the morning check at seven, shapes and tones on a tablet in a room with no window, and had told him the score would be delivered at nine-thirty in a room with the Director in it. He had reported the headache as one and been corrected to one and a half. Nine hours of sleep and the last two fingers on his right hand still had opinions.

He did not count the bays on the way through the Hall of Standards. He knew the number. He kept his hands in his jacket, one of them around the printed restriction card, and went up the administration stairs because the elevator had a mirror in it.

Sen's office was smaller than the position. One window, looking down across the arena roofs to where a crane stood over Arena Four with a section of white composite hanging from it like a pulled tooth. One desk, its whole surface a display, currently dark. Two chairs, the same height. A glass of water sat on the desk near her hand, half full, on a wooden coaster that was the only wooden thing in the room.

She did not offer him the glass. She drew a box around it with one finger, and the desk surface woke and made the box a thin white line.

"What is the system?"

He looked at the box. Then at her. "Is this a trick?"

"Probably."

"The glass. The water."

She drew a larger box, around the coaster too. "I have put a line around the coaster. Whether the coaster is in the system is a different question." She sat. "Most Resonant instruction begins after someone has already answered that question for the student. Here is the mass, here is the transfer, here is the edge of what you are responsible for. The boundary is handed to you on the first day and nobody mentions it again, because it is stable enough to be furniture."

"And I don't keep the furniture where I'm told."

"You may not. That is a hypothesis, not a compliment."

The desk changed. A training sphere crossed a simulated arena, and a Redirection operator beside its path turned it aside at the last meter. It struck the padded barrier at an angle. Colored lines ran from the impact into the barrier frame, then the floor, then branches too fine for the display to bother labeling.

"Did the operator redirect the sphere?"

"Yes."

"Did she redirect the barrier?"

"No."

"Then why did its bolts flex?"

"Because the sphere still hit it. She changed the angle. She didn't change the fact."

"So she changed a path without owning every consequence of the change." Sen drew a box around the instrumented arena. "The academy model stops here. Not because the world stops here. Because past this line the contribution becomes too small to matter to the exercise and too expensive to measure."

"So the system is whatever the sensors can afford."

"Often."

The image changed. Same sphere, same barrier, same operator, and now a second student a step inside the diverted path, close enough that the sphere crossed the front of her uniform before it hit the frame. The student flinched.

"Same system?"

"No."

"The equations have not changed."

"The reason for solving them did."

Sen let that sit. Then she drew a new box. It did not follow the arena walls. It cut across them and took in the sphere, the barrier, and the student, and nothing else.

"Every model contains a judgment about what matters. Most instruction hides the judgment by giving you the boundary in advance. When you draw one yourself, you have made the judgment, whether or not you noticed making it." She looked at the crooked white shape. "You produced nothing on that floor. Force that already existed resolved where our models did not expect it to. I told you last night that the rest was a longer conversation. This is the conversation. It is about where you drew the line, and what stood inside it."

"Pike stood inside it."

"Pike. The floor. The west damper. Two technicians in a service trench, if the trench had not been checked." She did not raise her voice for the trench. She did not need to.

The crane outside the window let its section of floor down out of sight.

"How many times have you done something consistent with that interpretation? I have the wall and I have the floor. You are about to tell me a different number, because you have been sitting on it since you came in."

He had been. "Eli has a model. Small things. A block that pushed somebody farther than the numbers said. A hit that came through me lighter than the strip measured. Nothing I chose. Nothing big enough for a sensor to argue with."

"Did Navarro design tests around the pattern?"

"No."

"Did you ask him not to?"

"Yes. Weeks ago. He kept the box and stopped adding to it."

Her posture changed by a fraction, and if it was approval she did not spend a word on it. "I want a copy."

"I'll send it tonight."

"No. You will ask him whether he wants to share it."

"He will."

"Then ask anyway."

He heard the difference, and he heard that she wanted him to hear it.

"Why does that matter to you?"

"Because power makes shortcuts around consent very easy to justify, and the first shortcut is always someone else's data."

It did not sound like a sentence she had built for him. It sounded like something she had said before, in a room that was not this one, and kept.

---

She brought up the floor. The footage froze on Pike's strike, the frame where the fracture had not happened yet, and she laid a ledger beside it. The engineer's totals, and under them Park's traces, three lines on a time axis with the morning of the match at the left edge.

"What we know. Pike generated nearly all of the kinetic energy in the event. Your body and stance contributed a small amount. The building contributed no unexplained energy."

"So I didn't amplify."

"You did not. The load registered in the arena structure along a path our standard telemetry did not detect, because your conventional output was beneath measurable threshold."

"Conventional."

"An important adjective. Learn it. It is the one that lets you say true sentences to your mother." She touched the fracture. "The damage is consistent with Pike's transfer resolving into structural members that should not have been the primary load path. You changed where it counted. Possibly."

"Possibly is doing a lot of work."

"Science is a small number of sentences and possibly is most of them."

He looked at Park's traces instead. Pupil response. Motor tremor. A third line he did not recognize, which fell hard after the match, climbed through the evening, and dropped again this morning at seven.

"What's that."

"Reaction. Shapes and tones."

"I did fine on that."

"You missed four tones out of nineteen." Park's number, in Sen's mouth. "Yesterday evening you missed one. You reported the headache as a negotiation."

He remembered sitting back from the tablet with the feeling of having done well on something built for a ten-year-old.

"I thought I was fine."

"That is why what you think is not clearance."

It went in and it stayed. He put his right hand flat on the arm of the chair and it was mostly still, and mostly was not a number Park would write.

"What's the rule?"

"Observation only. No deliberate attempt to reproduce the effect unless Park is present, a safety team has been briefed, and the structural load path has been mapped in advance."

"That could take weeks."

"Yes."

"What if it happens in a match?"

"You stop."

"What if stopping means I lose?"

She did not answer, and her face did not help him, and after a second he heard the question the way she had.

"Right. Stupid question."

"No. An important question with an easy answer while nobody is hitting you. The work is making the answer stay easy when someone is."

That was worse than a lecture, because it was a description of the second on the floor when his hand had stopped at his hip.

Sen cleared the desk and wrote three words on it, spaced apart. SOURCE. PATH. SINK. She connected them with a line.

"Boundary architecture."

The words in daylight, on a desk, with the crane working outside. They did not sound smaller.

"Provisional term. Conventional training asks what can move from source to sink and how an operator changes the path. Boundary architecture asks which parts are allowed to count as source, path, or sink in the first place." She slid the line sideways with one finger so that it cut through PATH. Half of PATH became SOURCE. "That is all it is. Relabeling."

"That's it?"

"Usually. A model can be wrong in ways reality ignores. The danger is assuming reality always ignores them."

She opened a drawer and set three disks on the desk in a row, the way a person lays out cutlery. Copper. Black composite. Steel.

"Power cell." Copper. "Damaged conduit." Black. "Containment shell." Steel. "The cell is discharging through the conduit and heating the shell. What is the system?"

He pointed at all three. "Too easy."

She moved the steel disk a hand's width away. "The shell is the casing of a medical transport with a patient inside."

"Patient too."

"The patient has an implanted field regulator drawing from the same cell."

He opened his mouth and the answer stopped working before it reached it.

If the regulator belonged to the failing circuit, cutting it out protected the shell and switched off the patient. If it belonged to the patient, the discharge path was not waste heat. It was the thing keeping someone alive. Same three disks. A different obligation, depending on a connection he could not see from the outside.

"You can't choose until you know what each connection does."

"Good."

"And if you don't know?"

"Then you do not pretend the cleanest diagram is the true one. Perceiving a boundary is not the same as owning what stands inside it." She took the copper disk out of his hand, where he had not noticed it arriving, and put it back in the row. "Pike, your body, the lattice. We know those participated. We do not know what definition made that arrangement available to you."

"Available."

"Not obedient."

He looked at the three words on the desk. The line through the middle one. He thought of the examination, months ago, and a selection box that had stopped snapping to objects, and a word on a screen he had not put there.

"You had this before the floor."

Sen did not move.

"You said it last night the way you'd say a street you grew up on. Not the way you'd say something you'd made up for me."

"I said it last night because you needed a word before you invented a worse one."

"Has anyone done this before?"

"No."

Clean. Flat. Nothing in it to hold.

"Last night you said tomorrow."

"Last night I had not slept and you had missed one tone. Today I have Park's chart and you have missed four." She did not look away. "No."

"Never."

"Not in any verified modern training program."

He heard it land in a different place than the first answer. He waited for her face to notice that it had, and it did not.

"Modern."

Nothing.

"Training program."

Nothing at all. She was very good, and being very good at it was itself a fact.

"You're answering the exact question you wish I'd asked."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth and was gone before it committed to anything.

"Zero Protocol was built years ago." He said it slowly, because he was assembling it while he said it. "You don't build a route for something that's never happened. You build it because it happened once and nobody knew what to do."

Sen touched the desk once, and the disks were sitting on nothing but a dark table.

"This session is over."

He laughed, once. "That's a yes."

"That is a Director ending a session."

"Director."

"Kade." Her voice sharpened one notch, the first time he had heard it do that, and then she took a breath and set it back. "Curiosity is useful. So is survival. I am going to teach you carefully, without assuming that novelty makes you safe or that significance makes you permanent."

"I never assumed either."

"Good." Her eyes went to his right hand on the chair arm. "Because whatever happened to that floor happened through you. Not to it. Through you."

The one and a half became a two, quietly, the way a room gets darker when a cloud crosses.

He stood. He got as far as the door.

The question had been sitting behind every other question since Room Four, since a photograph on a kitchen table and a box on a closet shelf that his mother had stood in front of without reaching up. He did not know why it chose now. Maybe because Sen had just lied to him with a straight face and he wanted to see what the face did with a different one.

"Did you know my father?"

For the first time since he had met her, Sen lost control of her face.

Almost. A stillness around the eyes. A breath that started and did not finish the way breaths finish. Then the Director came back into the room and sat down behind her expression.

"No."

Aaron Mercer had been dead for most of Kade's life. A watch that had stopped. A pencil. There should have been no reason for the question to matter, and she had just made it matter, and they both stood there knowing that she had.

"Okay," Kade said, and went out.

---

The first person he saw outside was Mara, which was becoming a statistical problem.

She sat on the stone ledge across the courtyard with a book open on her knee. A physical book, the kind Asterion kept in rooms with their own climate, with a library band around the spine that meant she had signed for it.

He sat down beside her. Students crossed the courtyard around them.

"Well?"

"She lied."

"About what?"

"At least two things."

He told her. Not all of it. The parts that were his. Existing force. Unexpected load path. Conventional output. Source, path, sink, and a line that could be slid. Verified modern training program.

Mara stopped him there. "Say that again."

"Not in any verified modern training program."

"That is lawyer language."

"Director language."

"Same family." She closed the book on her finger. "And the second thing."

"I asked if she knew my father."

Whatever had been about to happen at the corner of her mouth went away.

"And?"

"She said no."

"How sure?"

He thought about the breath that had not finished. "Completely."

Mara looked across the courtyard at the administration building, at the fourth-floor window, as though she could measure the distance to it.

"Then your father matters to whatever she is protecting."

"Or she met him once at a conference."

"You do not believe that."

"No."

She opened the book to the back and ran a finger down a column. Boundary feedback. Boundary-layer interference. Boundary-state estimation. Her finger stopped where a word should have been and was not.

"I searched for the term after you told me last night. It is not in this edition. It is not in the academy catalog. It is not in the public standards literature back to the founding."

"Between last night and now."

"I searched one phrase."

"At length."

She did not dignify that. "Absence proves very little. A provisional term may never have been published. Classified work would not be in the catalog. She may have coined it herself."

"Or she learned it from somebody."

"That is possible. It is not what we know." She turned the book so he could see a marked passage about recursive coupling as a problem of observation. Where an analyst drew the system edge decided which feedback looked like a cause and which looked like an answer. "What do we know?"

He recognized the maneuver. She was doing it with his father now, and he disliked it for the usual reason. It worked.

"She had the term before she explained it. She restricted her denial to modern training programs. She ended the session when I connected it to Zero Protocol. Her face did something when I said his name."

"Good."

"None of that tells me how she knew him."

"Correct."

"You enjoy this."

"Precision?"

"Being right near me."

"It is not rare enough to stay enjoyable." Her attention went back to the page, and the corner of her mouth did not.

They sat. He had expected anger to come and fill the space. What came instead was worse, because it had no direction. He had owned one story about Aaron Mercer his whole life. Young. An accident at work. Sad enough to explain the photograph, final enough that nobody had to keep asking. Sen had made even that unstable, in one syllable, and then closed a door on it.

"Do not go back up there today."

"That was an instruction."

"It was. You are angry, and she is better at this than you are, and you would give her something for nothing."

He looked at the cover of the book. FIELD STABILITY UNDER RECURSIVE COUPLING.

"You brought a textbook to a stakeout."

"I had reason to expect you would leave through this courtyard."

"That's what waiting is."

She went back to reading. He sat beside her a while longer, and found that his hand had stopped, and did not mention it, and she did not either.

---

He asked Eli that night. Not in passing, and not at the door on the way out. He sat down at his own desk, turned the chair to face the room, and asked.

"Sen wants the observations. Not your notes unless you want to include them."

Eli stopped spinning the stylus. "She said that?"

"She said I had to ask you."

"Huh." He looked at the file on his display, a grid of dates and numbers that had been quietly growing since the Rusk week and had not grown since Kade told him to stop. "What does she want it for?"

"I don't know."

"Who gets access?"

"I don't know."

Eli frowned at the grid.

Kade waited, and waiting was harder than he had expected. The reasons lined up in his mouth. The data might help. Sen had most of it already from the arena sensors. They needed to know. All true, and none of them his to say, because the file had Eli's name on the header and Eli's hours in it.

"Observations only," Eli said finally. "No private notes. No raw dorm sensor logs. And if anything I collected turns out to break a restriction I didn't know about, she tells me, so I can delete it instead of finding out at a hearing."

"I'll tell her."

"That's it? You're not going to argue me up?"

"No."

Eli looked almost disappointed. "Growth is upsetting."

"So I'm told."

The clean copy took forty minutes, mostly because observations only turned out to need a definition. The file held match timestamps, which Eli approved. It held motion readings from their own room on the night Kade's hands had shaken, which he did not. It held three guesses about Kade's sleep, a note about Sera checking the door before she sat down anywhere, and a chart plotting Taren's lateness against dessert.

"Why is that in my file?"

"The model needs environmental variables."

"Pudding isn't an environment."

"Taren disagrees."

They stripped everything that belonged to a person who had not been asked. What remained was smaller and better. Match, time, measured impact, predicted path, observed path, confidence. Eli added one more column, labeled it ALTERNATE EXPLANATIONS, and filled it in, and the whole file looked less certain when he was done, which he said was the point. Beside one anomaly, a note read: THIS MAKES NO SENSE UNLESS THE FLOOR IS LYING.

"That goes."

"That's analysis."

"That's slander against a building."

He removed it. Kade attached the conditions in Eli's own words and sent the file, and they sat looking at the display for six minutes, until it answered.

*Accepted. Thank him. Conditions honored.*

Eli read it twice. "Huh."

"What?"

"She could have pulled all of that through academy systems. My display's on the House network. Technically she didn't need either of us."

The word sat there. Technically was one of Kade's favorite places to hide, and Sen had walked past it without going in.

It did not make her honest. It made her complicated, and he was finding that complicated adults were harder to do anything with than cruel ones. Cruel ones you could just leave.

---

Park's schedule for the next morning called it a non-provocative boundary observation session, and he read the title three times.

"That sounds provocative."

"It means you are not permitted to do anything."

"Ah. Science."

The observation room hung over one of the small assessment floors, a reinforced window at chest height, a ledge, one chair. Below, three stations had been laid out under separate lights. A rolling mass on a rail. A padded impact rig facing a damping block the size of a suitcase. A suspended platform, hip height, with load weights clipped around its edge and two students standing at opposite corners.

He wore no active Conduit. The band on his wrist was a medical band and nothing else. Park set a hand control on the ledge in front of him. One red button. No label.

"Comforting."

"Press it if you develop a headache, tunnel vision, tremor, missing time, sudden nausea, or the sense that you can influence anything on that floor."

"What does it do?"

"Stops all three stations and calls the safety team."

"Dramatic."

"So was the floor."

She took his baseline before the technicians moved. Follow the light. Thumb to each finger. Six numbers backward. Describe the taste of the electrolyte drink without insulting it.

"Artificial orange."

"That was not an insult."

"It was accurate enough to become one."

She marked it. "Every five minutes you report symptoms before observations. If there are none, you say none." She set the timer beside the button. "Understand what this is, Mercer. It is not an endurance test. Nobody is grading how long you stay in this chair. The successful result is that you leave it with an accurate record and no injury. Pressing that button first may be the most correct thing you do all week."

It bothered him. Every test he had ever taken rewarded staying in the chair. Rank. Approval. Proof. Here the button was on the scoresheet, and pressing it could be the right answer, and that was a shape his hands did not know yet.

He rested two fingers beside it. "Ready."

The rolling mass went first. A Redirection student put a palm out and the mass leaned off its line into the rail, and the rail sang, and he said so, and Park reminded him that he could not see the field. He could see the rail.

The impact rig struck the damping block. A Storage student took it, held it a visible second, and let it go into the block. The display above the station said one number.

"Second one's storing less than that says."

Park looked at the telemetry. "Why?"

"The block's heating early. Watch the seam on the left. It's already gone dull."

The technician checked, and did not look pleased. Kade was right.

"Five minutes. Symptoms."

"None."

The platform came last. Two Coupling students, one at each corner, and the technicians began sliding weights around its edge without warning. The platform tipped. The students caught it. It tipped the other way, less, and they caught that, and then it began a slow oscillation that neither of them was causing and both of them were feeding.

He leaned toward the glass.

"What?"

"They're correcting each other."

"That is the point."

"No. They're correcting the correction. Every time she fixes it, his fix means something different, and he's still doing the old one."

Park watched the platform. Kade watched the two people, and saw Mara's bracket with two limits inside it, and a stretcher going through a door in the Commons. Two operators. One active system that was not simply the sum of them.

He waited for the cold line.

Nothing. No pressure behind the eyes. No thumb at the base of the skull. Just the understanding, arriving on its own, the ordinary way.

He breathed out, and heard himself do it.

"Relieved?"

"Yes."

"Good. You are allowed to learn something without injuring yourself. I would like you to have that experience more than once."

She opened the room channel. "Platform. Repeat the sequence. At ten seconds, swap primary and support."

The students exchanged corners. The platform rose. Ten seconds level, and at the signal they traded roles, and the whole thing tipped left as if somebody had kicked it.

"They switched the labels."

"They switched tasks."

"No. Look at her. She's still expecting her correction to be the main one. He's waiting to support something that isn't there anymore. They're both acting like the first arrangement still exists."

The left edge dropped again.

"What happens next?"

He ran it. She would push up. He would come in late. The lateness would make her back off early. "Front right corner comes up, then they overcorrect backward."

The corner lifted. The platform rolled back. The two students found their own inputs and brought the thing level between them.

Park wrote something. "Again. Announce ownership before every correction."

"Vertical load," the first student called.

"Rotation," said the second.

Each named the change before making it. It looked slower than the first run and settled in half the time.

"Five minutes. Symptoms."

"None."

"Observations."

"They made the line between their jobs explicit. And then they kept moving it on purpose instead of pretending the first one was still true."

Park nodded once. From her that was a ceremony.

"Is that boundary architecture?"

"It is two Coupling students communicating properly."

"That was almost an answer."

"Do not become greedy."

The session ended with no anomaly, no button, and one accurate record. Park called it a successful result. Two days ago he would have called it nothing happening. He carried the difference back across the bridge and found it had weight.

*Thirty-one. Mom says you sat in a chair for an hour and were told you did well. I said that's not a school, that's a waiting room. She said it's the first test you've ever passed by not doing anything and she's going to frame it.*

---

Elena called while he was on the bridge, with the wind coming through the rail and the water going black underneath.

He nearly did not tell her about Sen. The thought lasted about as long as the walk to the first tower, because he knew how that story went when adults made the same choice for him, and he had spent his whole life on the other end of it.

So he told her. Not the classified parts. The parts that were his. Sen had known the term before the floor. Sen had said no when he asked about Aaron, and her face had said something else.

Elena stopped moving on the screen.

"Kade. Do not go looking for old records alone."

He slowed. "That was specific."

"I am serious."

"Why?"

"Because I don't know enough to give you the answer you want, and I'm not going to invent one."

"That's becoming a family tradition."

Her face tightened. He regretted it before the sentence had fully left.

"Sorry."

She let it go, which cost her something visible. "Your father worked around people connected to early Resonance research. That is what I know for certain."

He stopped walking. "You said industrial systems."

"He did both."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because after he died I was told parts of his work were classified, and I didn't know which parts, and I had a two-year-old and a night shift." A cart went past behind her. "I didn't know what mattered and what didn't. I still don't."

"Who told you it was classified?"

She looked away from the camera, at something in the corridor. "I don't remember everyone who called."

Not a lie. Not the whole answer either. He had spent seventeen years learning to hear that gap, and he heard it now, and he heard something else too, that she knew he could.

"Did Sen?"

Her eyes came back. "I don't know what she was to him."

He believed that. It was worse than not believing it.

"What did he actually do?" he said. "Not the classified version. What did you think he did?"

She leaned against the wall. "He was hired through an industrial controls contractor. Ports. Power routing. Automated safety systems. That was on the pay records, and some of it was real. He'd come home complaining about load-balancing software and draw diagrams on whatever paper was nearest for half of dinner."

"What kind of diagrams?"

"Circuits, mostly. Lists of ways things fail. Once, a very detailed argument with the refrigerator."

"Who won?"

"The refrigerator. He took it apart at two in the morning and had it back together by five, and we kept the milk in a cooler for three days."

He tried to put that beside the photograph, the one where his father was smiling at somebody outside the frame. There was no refrigerator in it.

"Was he bad at fixing things?"

"He was bad at accepting that some things should be replaced."

The sentence had weight in it, and he nearly pulled at it, and she saw him nearly pull.

"Do not turn every ordinary memory into evidence."

"I didn't say anything."

"You didn't have to."

It stopped him, because it was not a defense of Sen. It was a defense of Aaron's right to have been a person before he became a clue.

"He was patient with machines," Elena said. "Less patient with people. He thought reading the instructions after something broke counted as planning."

Kade looked over the rail. "That sounds like me."

"It sounds like him at twenty-six. He also left wet towels on the floor and believed every shelf could hold one more book. Don't turn resemblance into destiny because he's dead."

"I wasn't."

"You were considering it."

He had been.

"Did he ever say Zero Protocol? Boundary architecture?"

"No. Neither."

"Anyone I could ask?"

"Not one I trust. The contractor changed its name. Supervisors changed. After the accident, everyone who called already knew what they were allowed to tell me." She rubbed the mark her mask had left across the bridge of her nose. "We had a baby, I worked nights, he traveled. Most of what we talked about was whether there was milk and whose turn it was to sleep. I'm sorry I can't turn that into the thing you need."

He had never once pictured Aaron Mercer tired at a kitchen table. The photograph had made him permanent. Young, smiling, already gone. This version left towels on the floor.

"If you find something," Elena said, "you tell me before you confront anyone."

"That sounds like permission to look."

"It's recognition that telling you not to look would waste both our evenings."

"You do know me."

"Unfortunately." The line should have been light. Her voice made it tired instead, and he let it be tired. "And you won't search alone."

He thought of Mara with the passage already marked. Eli asking who got access before he asked anything else. Sera, who would find every unsafe assumption in a plan before he reached the first locked door.

"I won't be alone."

"That isn't what I said."

"It's what I can promise."

She considered him through the screen for a while. "Then promise it."

"I promise."

The call ended with neither of them satisfied, which he was beginning to understand was what a real conversation with her cost, and he stood on the bridge another minute before he went home.

---

The argument about the bell had started without him.

"Four hundred and forty-eight days," Taren said, from the long table, to the room.

"It's fourteen months," said Sera.

"Days are more upsetting. I'm going for upsetting."

A napkin hit him in the shoulder. He retrieved it without looking and folded it. "I stand by the count. The question is whether moral time differs from calendar time. Mara. Explain observer effects to her."

Mara, at the end of the table with the coupling book open beside her plate, turned a page. "I would rather eat the book."

Kade's seat was open. A covered plate sat on it. Rice, roasted vegetables, a piece of fish approaching structural failure. Eli pointed at the fish as Kade sat.

"One observation. It's dry."

"Did you obtain consent?"

"I asked the fish. No response."

Sera looked at his untouched water. He drank before she could say anything.

"Stretches after dinner."

"That was suspiciously easy."

"Park educated me."

"With threats," Eli said.

"The best kind."

Mara closed the book. "Did you call your mother?"

The room went on around the question. Cutlery. An upper-year losing an argument about tomorrow's training rotation. Taren opening a minority report on moral time to a first-year who had not asked.

He could have said later. He could have kept the bridge on the bridge, separate from the people at the table, the way he had kept everything separate for seventeen years.

"Yes."

Mara waited.

"Aaron worked in early Resonance research through an industrial contractor. She doesn't know what Sen was to him. She told me not to look alone."

Eli's next joke did not arrive. Sera pulled out the chair opposite and sat in it. Nobody asked for the rest, and that made it possible to give them some.

"She remembers him losing an argument with a refrigerator," Kade said. "That's what she's got. Nothing she knows how to connect to Sen."

Taren lowered himself into the last open chair. His eyes went to Kade's right hand first, which had not shaken since the courtyard, and then to the book by Mara's plate, and then to nothing. "The refrigerator knows too much."

Mara pushed the bread toward Kade. "Eat before you form a theory."

"Is that a scientific rule?"

"It is now."

He took the bread. The fish was dry. Eli had been right about that too, and Kade ate it anyway while Sera timed his stretches from her chair and Taren appealed the bell ruling to an upper-year who refused jurisdiction. Mara reopened the book only after half his plate was gone.

He had believed, for as long as he could remember, that serious things had to be carried alone or they were not serious. The table kept proving otherwise without making a point of it.

Later, in the room, with Eli asleep and the display dark, he took the duty sheet out of the desk drawer and turned it to the back, where a line already stood in a hand that had not been quite steady. *Forty-seven. Wall held. I want to know what I did.*

He took the pencil out from under it. Tooth marks near the tip. Seventeen years in a box, and now three weeks in a drawer at Asterion.

He wrote under the first line, steadier than the first line, and did not cross it out.

*Floor. Somebody's done it before. She said no.*

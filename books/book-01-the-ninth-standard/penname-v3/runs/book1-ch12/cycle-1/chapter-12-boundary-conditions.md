# Chapter 12 — Boundary Conditions

*Thirty. Mom says you get the name today. I said a name is what you give a thing so you can stop looking at it. She said that's the smartest thing I've said all year and also to shut up.*

Park's clinic opened at seven and he was at the door at ten to, because the alternative was lying in the dark listening to two fingers.

She did not take his headache number first. She put a tablet in front of him with a blank field on it, put a stylus in his right hand, and said, "Your name. Normal speed."

He wrote it.

"Fast."

He wrote it.

"As slowly as you can without stopping."

The last one was the hard one. Somewhere in the middle of the second E the stylus went its own way for a length he could see, and he watched it go, and finished the R anyway.

Park laid the three signatures over the one from his first morning on the island, the intake day, when the only thing wrong with him had been a number. She looked at them for a while without saying which one bothered her.

"Headache."

"One."

"Sleep."

"Some."

"Those are two words that mean nothing, and I have written both of them down." She took the stylus back. "The Director has the rest. You will hear it from her, because she asked to be the one who says it, and because if I say it you will bargain."

"I don't bargain."

"You gave me one and a half yesterday as an opening offer."

His band said where. TEACHING ARENA. 08:30. Under it, in the way that told him it was Sen and not a form: *The card.*

---

The teaching arena had not changed. Same white composite, same clear wall on the east side that had flexed under Priya Shah's weight and held, same red strip a long step in from it. What had changed was the center circle. A roll of blue marking tape lay there, and beside it a roll of yellow, and Sen stood over both with her hands in her coat pockets as if she had brought them and then decided they were not hers.

"Card."

He held it up without letting go of it. She read the four triggers from where she stood.

"Keep it out. You will want it in your hand for this." She moved the blue roll an inch with her shoe. "The morning you stood in this room with Shah, you told Dr. Park afterward that the room had become one shape. Tape it. From memory. Where the shape stopped."

"It didn't stop. That was the point."

"It stopped somewhere or the totals would not have closed. Tape it."

He picked up the blue roll. The end had been started for him. He walked the room with the tape running off his fingers and laid a line where the shape had ended: not at the wall but past it, into the mounts, around the plate under his own feet, and then in a long straight run back toward the place where Priya had stood, a body's length from him, the morning the building moved. He stopped short of her circle with the tape held taut and looked at the gap.

"You are hesitating."

"She was inside it." He heard himself say it before he had decided to. "The shape. She was in it. I've been thinking of it as me and the wall."

"Close it."

He closed it. The blue went around the place where Priya Shah had been standing, and it was not a big room, and it took less tape than he wanted it to.

"Yellow. Arena Four."

That one went faster, because he had drawn it once already with a stylus on a rolling table. West damper. Ribs. Footings. The seam under his own front foot. And Pike, who had been closer to him than Priya, with everything he had loaded into his lead leg and the lead leg inside the line. He walked the yellow around the place where Pike had stood and did not let himself slow down.

Sen stepped over the yellow and stood on the mark he had made for Pike, and looked at the tape around her shoes.

"A first-year asked me a question yesterday," Kade said. "At the Commons door. Whether I could do that to a person."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I didn't know."

"Look at your feet."

He looked. The yellow ran a hand's width from the toe of her shoe.

"I do not know either," Sen said. "That is the true answer and I want you to keep it. But I will tell you what the tape says. You did not choose the floor. You chose a place, and the floor was what happened to be standing at the place. Everything inside the line was available. A damper. A rib. A service trench that somebody had checked. A knee." She stepped back over the line the way a person steps over a live cable. "That is the lesson, and it is not physics. Whatever is inside the line when it falls is what pays. You do not get to learn afterward who that was."

The edge of the card had gone damp in his hand.

"Now the vocabulary. You have earned that much, and if I do not give it to you, Navarro will invent one." She said the two words the way she had said them over the crack, and in daylight they were not smaller. "Boundary architecture. Sit."

There was nowhere to sit. He sat on the composite inside the blue, and she stayed on her feet, which he thought was deliberate.

"Every Standard on the wall in the Hall is an operation. Perceive a transfer. Redirect it. Amplify it. Partition it. Store it, convert it, sustain it, couple it. Every one of those verbs is done to a system, and no student is ever asked what the system is, because the answer is supplied. Contact supplies it. Your hand is on the post; the post is the system. The Conduit supplies it. The room supplies it, with sensors that watch operators, because operators are the only thing that has ever needed watching." She turned one hand over. "Nobody in this building has had to decide where a thing ends. It was decided for them. The Standards are eight ways of acting on a decision."

"And the decision is the architecture."

"The decision is the architecture. What counts as one thing. Where it stops. Which parts of the room are permitted to answer when force asks a question. For every student I have taught, that layer does not move. For you, twice, under load, with no command, it moved." She looked at the yellow. "It was named architecture, a long time ago, because it is not force. It is structure. You added nothing to that floor. You redrew the plan of the building for one second, and the building did what the plan said."

"A long time ago."

"Yes."

She did not go on, and he heard that door close, and left it, because she had brought a tablet out of her coat and he wanted what was on it more.

The engineer's ledger first. He had seen it: input, output, heat, strain, totals that met. Under it now, Park's three lines. He knew the pupil line and the tremor line. The third was new, a saw-tooth that dropped hard at the match, climbed all night, and dropped again at seven this morning, when he had been writing his name.

"Reaction. Dr. Park's tone battery. Last evening you missed one tone in twenty. This morning you missed six."

"I felt better this morning."

"You wrote your name three times this morning and one of them is not your name." She took the tablet back. "Which is why you do not clear yourself. Here is the rule. I want you to have it in your body before you have it in your mouth. You do not attempt this. Not in a bout, not in a bay, not on a roof with Navarro holding a sensor. When we attempt it, if we attempt it, the load path under you is mapped first, a team is briefed, and Dr. Park is standing where I am standing. Until then, you watch. You may watch anything you like."

"And when it doesn't ask first."

"Then you take your hand off it. You got the hand to your hip yesterday. I am told that is a first." Nothing in her voice softened. "You will be told that stopping is losing. The people who tell you will not be standing inside the tape."

He turned the card over in his fingers.

"Has anyone done this before?"

He had meant to hold it until the end. It came out into the empty room, and there was nothing in the room to catch it but her.

"No."

It arrived clean. Nothing in it moved. Last night, over the crack, she had said tomorrow, and it had sounded like yes. This morning it was no, and the two could not both be hers.

"Last night you said tomorrow."

"Last night you had not written your name three times."

"That isn't why."

She let that stand.

So he did what he had been doing since a folder came open in a classroom built for fourteen-year-olds. He counted what she had left out.

"Zero Protocol," he said. "A section of the national examination nobody could reach, kept in the code for thirty-one years because taking it out would have meant explaining it. The last time a candidate got through, the program that took him was classified. So was he. You don't write a failsafe for a thing that never happened. You write it the morning after."

She looked at the blue tape for a long moment, and at the place inside it where Priya Shah had been.

"Nobody has learned this at Asterion," she said.

He heard every word of it separately. Learned. At Asterion.

"That's smaller than no."

"It is what I have." She turned toward the door. "The session is over. Dr. Park has you tomorrow."

"Director."

"Kade." One notch sharper. Then a breath, and it was set back. "Whatever happened on that floor happened through you. Not to it. I am going to teach you carefully, and I am not going to assume that being new makes you safe. I have seen what that assumption costs."

She was at the door when he asked the other thing. Not because he had planned it. Because a woman who had just said no to him with nothing in her face was walking away, and he wanted to see the face do it twice, and he had only one other question.

"Did you know my father?"

"No."

It came before he had finished. Before *father* had all the way left his mouth. She had had it ready, and you keep an answer ready for a question you are expecting, and you expect a question when you know why it would be asked. Then she seemed to hear the speed of it herself, and stood in the doorway half a second longer than she needed to, as if checking that the sentence had been correctly assembled.

"Okay," Kade said.

She went. He sat on the floor inside a blue line with a card in his hand, and a stopped watch in a box in Northline, and found that a man who had been dead since before he had memories had just been made present by one syllable said too soon.

---

He called Elena from the covered way outside, because she had said after Sen and she had said a call, and because if he waited until he had decided what to tell her he would decide to tell her less.

She answered from the driver's seat of her car in the hospital's parking structure, with a container of something on the dashboard and a fork in her hand and the badge still clipped to her scrubs. Shift change. She ate in the car because it was the one place at St. Vincent nobody could find her.

"After Sen," she said. "Good. Face."

He turned the phone.

"Hands."

He held up the right one. The two fingers did their thing for her.

"Okay." She put the fork down. "Give me the sentence she gave you for me. She'll have given you one."

She had. "I produced nothing. Force that already existed resolved somewhere their models didn't expect. Everything else is where, and why."

"That's two sentences and a clause." But she nodded slowly, the way she nodded at a discharge summary that was better than she had feared. "And the why?"

"She's got a word for it. Not a Standard. A layer under the Standards. She says it's structure, not force. I didn't add anything. I moved where the building thought it ended."

"For one second."

"For one second."

"And it cost you six tones out of twenty and a signature that isn't yours."

He had not told her about the signature. "Park sends you things."

"Park sends me what I'm allowed to have. You're seventeen and I read fast." She picked the fork up again and did not use it. "What else."

He could have stopped there. It would have counted as a call. She would have hung up, and eaten, and gone back in.

"I asked her if anyone had done it before. She said no."

Elena said nothing.

"Last night she said tomorrow, like it was yes. This morning it was no, and when I pushed, it was nobody has learned it at Asterion. Which isn't no. It's a fence around a no."

"Kade."

"And then I asked her if she knew Dad."

The fork went down. Elena did not look at the phone. She looked through the windshield at the concrete wall of the parking structure, where somebody had painted a number and a letter, and stayed there a while.

"Why did you ask her that."

Not *what did she say.* He heard the difference and put it on the pile.

"Because she'd just lied to me and I wanted to watch her do it again. And she said no before I'd finished the word."

"Do not," Elena said, "go into old records on your own."

He waited.

"That's a very specific thing not to do."

"I'm a specific person."

"Mom."

She turned back to the phone then, and he watched her decide something, the way she decided things at the kitchen table with the bills fanned out, quickly and with her whole face.

"Before you left, I told you the contractor part was true. It was. It's on his pay stubs. Ports, power routing, automatic shutoffs. He was good at it and bored by it. The last few months he was sent somewhere that wasn't a port. He called it the site. He said the people there were doing something with Resonance that wasn't in any book yet, and he was excited, and he wasn't allowed to tell me what, and he was bad at not telling me things, so I got pieces." She stopped. "After, I got a call. Then a visit. Parts of his work were classified, they said. They were sorry. They took some papers out of the apartment and left me a box."

"Who."

"People with badges I didn't keep. I had a baby and a night shift and a box."

"Was one of them her."

"I don't know what she was to him." Fast and flat, the way she had said the contractor part, and he knew the shape of it. Not a lie. A door with something behind it that she had decided not to open in front of him. "I'm not going to guess in front of you. You'd build on it."

That was Renn's word, from a classroom. *Don't build on that.* He had built on it anyway. He always built on it.

"Then tell me something that isn't evidence," he said. "One thing. Not off the list you keep."

She looked at the fork.

"He counted stairs," she said. "Out loud. Every flight, every time, under his breath, and if you spoke to him on the stairs he'd lose the number and have to go back to the top, and he'd be annoyed with you and never say so." Her mouth did something. "Nine to the apartment. Fourteen at my mother's. He knew them all. I used to hate it."

Kade counted stairs. He had counted the twelve bays in the Hall yesterday without deciding to. He had counted the doors off a courtyard before he looked at a bell.

"That sounds like me."

"It sounds like a man who counted stairs. Maybe you got it from him. Maybe you got it from being a person who counts. I don't know which, and neither do you, and I'm not letting you decide it today because he's dead and can't say it was nothing." She picked up the container, looked at it, put it back. "Two things."

"You're going to say them anyway."

"You don't open a door on your own. Any door. Records, rooms, her. And whoever you're going to stand in front of with what you find, I hear it first. Not after. Not three words."

"That's a lot of doors."

"It's an island. It's mostly doors."

He thought about Mara at the glass in Arena Four, watching his hand stop at his hip. Eli asking who got access before he asked anything else. Sera, who found the unsafe step in a plan before he had finished describing the plan.

"I won't be on my own," he said.

"That's the first one. Say the second."

"You hear it first."

"From you."

"From me."

She held the phone a moment longer. Whatever else she had was bigger than the car. "Eat. Call Darius, he's unbearable. I love you."

"I love you."

Somebody knocked on her window. She raised one finger at them without turning her head, and the screen went dark, and he stood on the covered way with a phone and a card and a piece of his father that was smaller than a watch and weighed more.

Mara was in Bay One at the hour she had said, which was not a surprise, with the mat marked in tape at four-step intervals, which was.

"Shoes."

"I'm restricted."

"From combat. This is walking." She had a tablet on the rail and was not looking at it. "Four steps and a turn. Rear foot later. Talk while you do it. If you stop walking, I stop listening."

He walked. He told her. The blue around Priya and the yellow around Pike. The layer under the verbs. Twenty tones and six missed. Watch, don't attempt. No, and then tomorrow, and then *nobody has learned this at Asterion.* He got to the doorway and the answer that had come too soon, and found it was easier to say with his feet moving, and understood why she had done it this way.

"Stop."

He stopped. He had lost the count somewhere around the doorway.

"Two piles," Mara said. "Things you saw. Things you decided. Say each one and tell me which."

"She said no." He did not have to think. "Saw."

"She said tomorrow last night."

"Saw. Four people heard it."

"It isn't published."

He caught himself. "I don't know that. I haven't looked."

"Then it's not in either pile yet. Go on."

"She said a long time ago. About the word. That it was named a long time ago." He walked a step without meaning to and stopped. "Saw."

"Nobody has learned this at Asterion."

"Saw. And it's a built sentence."

"That second part is a decision."

"The words are a fact. What they're for is a decision. Keep them apart." She had not written anything down. "The father."

"She said no before I'd finished asking."

"Saw."

"She'd been waiting for it."

"Decided." Mara put a hand up before he could argue. "Probably right. Still decided. A person can answer fast because they're tired of a question. My father asks me the same thing every Sunday and I've answered it before he reaches the verb."

He had not known that about her. He put it somewhere careful.

"So what are the piles for."

"So that when you go back in there, you go in with the first one and leave the second one here." She picked up the tablet. "And you're not going back in there today."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to at about four o'clock, once you'd made yourself angry enough. I watched you do it with Navarro's model. She's better at this than you are, and she's had thirty years to get better at it, and she'd have you handing her the second pile inside a minute."

She was right, which he disliked, and she had said it without any pleasure in it, which he did not.

"Then what."

"Then somebody finds out whether it's published. Not you. Anyone in the House can run a catalog, and a catalog doesn't know it's being asked about you." She looked at the tape on the mat. "And you write down what would make you wrong. If she knew your father, what would we expect to find. If she didn't, what would we expect. Two lists. You bring me the second one first."

"Why the second."

"Because you'll write the first one without trying." She turned to the rail. "Feet. Again. You crossed on the third turn and I let it go because you were talking about the doorway."

He walked. His feet did what she had taught them without asking him. On the fourth turn he noticed that the last two fingers of his right hand had been still for the whole of the second circuit, and did not say so, and she did not either, and each of them knew the other had counted.

He asked Eli at the long table in the Commons at eleven, under the ladder wall, where the red bar across his own line made a kind of weather. MERCER, KADE. 8,159. RESTRICTED. Not upstairs, where the display was, because he wanted Eli to be able to walk away from the question without walking away from his own desk.

"Sen wants the small ones. The observations. She wants them from you, not me. She said if I sent them without asking it'd be the first shortcut, and she'd know what kind I was from it."

Eli had a spoon in a cup of something that had once been hot. He stopped turning it.

"She said that. The kind you were."

"Close enough."

"Huh." He looked up at the wall, at the bar. "Did she say what it's for?"

"No."

"Who else sees it?"

"I don't know."

"Does it get a name on it? Mine or yours?"

"I don't know that either."

Kade let the three answers sit there and did not add to them. It was the hardest thing he had done that day, harder than the tape. Every good reason was lined up behind his teeth. She had the arena feeds already. It would help. They needed the pattern. All true, and all his to think, and none of it his to say, because the file had Eli's small capitals in the header and Eli's nights inside it.

Eli turned the spoon one full circle.

"Bouts only," he said. "Nothing from the room. There's stuff in there from the room and it isn't going. Nothing about anybody who didn't know I was watching, which is most of Meridian, because I'm not a good person. A version number on it, so if it ever comes back to me changed I know it got changed. And if she finds something in it that breaks a rule I didn't know was a rule, I hear it from her before I hear it from a hearing."

"Yes."

"That's it? You're not going to make a case?"

"No."

Eli regarded him with real suspicion. "Who are you and what did the floor do with my roommate."

Cleaning it took an hour, because *bouts only* turned out to need arguing. The match rows stayed. The night his hands had shaken, logged off the room's light sensor as a series of very small disturbances, went. There was a column called CONTEXT that was, when Kade read down it, his own mood in Eli's shorthand. ANNOYED (RUSK). ANNOYED (MARA, BUT DIFFERENT). SLEPT.

"Why do you have my mood."

"It's a variable. Everything's a variable until you can prove it isn't."

"It's not going."

"It correlates."

"With what?"

Eli deleted the column with enormous dignity. "I'll tell you when it's published."

What was left was smaller and stronger. Match, time, measured impact, predicted resolution, observed resolution, confidence. Eli went down the confidence column and lowered every number in it by one step, then went down it again and lowered two of them a second time.

"Why those."

"Because I liked those two the most." He typed a version number into the header and his initials after it. "Send it. Your words for what she gets. My words for the terms. Don't improve mine."

Kade sent it. They watched the tablet, and the red bar above it, for seven minutes.

*Terms accepted. Tell Navarro the version is his. Thank him.*

Eli read it twice and set the tablet face down.

"You know she had it," he said. "Right? Arena feeds. House network. My display's on the House network. She's had it since the day I started."

"Probably."

"So what she asked for was the one thing that isn't on the network." He picked up the cold cup. "Me saying yes."

It did not make her honest. It made her a person who had wanted a yes she could have taken instead, and that was harder to leave than cruelty. He had left cruelty before. He knew where its door was.

*Thirty-one. Mom says you asked the Director about Dad and then told Mom you'd asked. I said that's two things you've never done. She said three. She said you called.*

Park's schedule called it a non-provocative observation session, and it gave him a job.

Assessment Floor Two had no stands and one platform: a square of ribbed metal at hip height, hung on four cables from a frame, with weights clipped around its rim in a pattern the technicians could change. A Coupling student stood at each of two opposite corners. Behind a painted line, between the technicians and the platform, with a clipboard and a stopwatch and no active Conduit, was where Park put him.

"You call the loads. The technicians move them on your call. Those two hold the platform level. You write down what happens. This is the observation duty you did for Instructor Hale in your second week, and nobody has ever died of it."

"That's the job."

"That is the job. Here is the rest." She stood in front of him so that he had to look at her and not the platform. "There is a word. The word is *bench.* It means you are off the floor. If you say it, every station stops, the platform locks, and I look at your eyes. Headache above two. Narrowing. Tremor. Anything that feels like the line. You say it."

"And if I say it for nothing?"

"Then the record says you stopped for nothing, and I write *complete* beside it." She held out her hand, palm up. "Say it now."

"Now?"

"For nothing. So that the first time you say it in this room is not the time it matters. People do not say a word they have never said."

He felt stupid. He said it. "Bench."

The technicians stopped. The two Coupling students, who had been settling their feet, looked toward the line and then at each other. The platform, which had not been doing anything, went on not doing anything. Park wrote on her tablet and turned it so he could read. STOP CALLED. NO EVENT. RESUME.

"Again, at any time. Begin."

He began. Front left, two kilos. The technician unclipped it and moved it, the platform tipped, the two students caught it between them, and he wrote down how long. Back right, four. Longer. They over-held and it rocked. He wrote that too. By the sixth move he had noticed something, and by the eighth he was sure of it: when he said the corner a beat before the technician's hand arrived, the two of them had the platform before it tipped. When he called it as the weight moved, they were late. When Park, on the channel, had a technician move one with no call at all, the platform rolled almost to the cable stops before they found it.

"What do you see," Park said.

"They hold better when they know what's coming. Not stronger. They've got the same picture." He watched the far student's feet reset. "Try something."

"Tell me first."

"I call the wrong corner. On purpose. See if they're still faster than no call."

Park considered it, put it to the floor, and the two students agreed with the expression of people who have been asked to be lied to for science. He called front right and the technician moved back left. The platform went. They caught it. Not as fast as a true call. Faster than silence.

He sat back on his heels behind the line. Two people, one wrong picture, and still better than two people with none. Mara's bracket, with two limits inside it. And something under the bracket, that let two people share a picture without either of them being the one holding it.

He waited for the cold line, because that was the shape of a thought that had cost him before. Pressure behind the eyes. A thumb at the base of the skull.

Nothing. It had come the ordinary way, off a clipboard, and it stayed.

He breathed out, and Park, who missed nothing, wrote the breath down.

"That is what learning is supposed to feel like," she said. "I would like you to become bored with it."

They ran it heavier. On the fourth series, calling back left with six kilos, he saw the pen in his hand write the six with a small extra loop in it that he had not put there, and the light over the platform had acquired a faint edge, and the headache had gone from one to something he would have called one and a half yesterday and today was not going to call anything.

"Bench."

The floor stopped. The platform locked at a tilt. Park did not look at it. She looked at his pupils, then his hands, then set a ninety-second timer and said nothing until it went.

"Number."

"Two."

"Cold."

"No. The pen went, and the light did a thing, and I said it."

She wrote, and turned the tablet. STOP CALLED BY SUBJECT AT THRESHOLD. NO EVENT. SESSION COMPLETE.

"Complete," he said. "Not stopped early."

"There is no early. There is the record and there is the injury, and you have one and not the other." She took the clipboard out of his hand and looked at the loop in the six. "Yesterday the Director told you that you would be told stopping is losing. Today nobody told you anything. You had a pen and a light and nobody hitting you, and you said the word. That is not nothing. It is a rehearsal, and you have now had exactly two."

He carried that back across the bridge with the wind in his collar, and it did not feel like a pass. It felt like a floor that had held.

The roof had both heaters going and the bell's stay cable coming up out of the dark to its anchor, and the chess table with nobody at it, and Sera on the parapet with her boots on the bench and a paper bag beside her.

"Sit. Eat. Stretches after. I'm counting."

He sat. It was real food, from the kitchen, still warm through the paper.

"Park said the word *rehearsal* to me in the corridor," Sera said, "on purpose, so I'd say it to you. She said you found out you can stop in a room where nobody's hitting you, and she wants you to keep finding it out until it's boring." She looked at him sideways. "She likes you. It's very well hidden."

Eli came up with the tablet and no coat. Mara came up with a coat and a list. Taren came up last, the way he always did, with a coffee he had not needed to make.

"Catalog," Mara said, and sat. "Two words. Academy catalog, public standards literature, the founding index that goes back to the first year. Nessa ran it from the perception lab, because a query from there looks like coursework. Zero. Not once. Not as a chapter, not as a footnote, not misspelled."

"Which means either it's new," Eli said, "or it's somewhere a catalog can't see."

"Or it's a word one person used and never wrote down." Mara did not look up. "Second list. What we'd expect if she never met him. She'd have said no at the speed she says everything. She wouldn't have had the word before the floor. There wouldn't be a thirty-one-year-old failsafe in the national examination." She folded the tablet closed. "I wrote the first list too. It's longer. That's the problem with it."

Kade ate. Then, because the four of them were waiting and pretending not to, he told them what Elena had said. The site that wasn't a port. Badges she hadn't kept. *I don't know what she was to him.* Stairs.

Nobody made a joke. Eli, who made jokes the way other people breathed, looked at the cable going up into the dark.

"Stairs," Sera said finally. "Out loud."

"Every flight."

"You do that."

"I know."

"You did it on the Meridian stairs your first night. I heard you through the wall. I thought you were praying."

Taren had been quiet, which was not his usual condition. He set the coffee on the chess table.

"Pike pulled the open," he said. "Off the wall an hour ago. He posted a note under it. Rematch when there's a floor. I only mention it so nobody hears it from Kepler first and thinks it means something it doesn't."

"How do you know he pulled it an hour ago," Mara said.

"It's on the wall. Everyone can see the wall."

"Ours updated ten minutes ago," Eli said. "I was looking at it."

"Kepler's, then. Before ours. They post faster." Taren picked the coffee back up. "You're all looking at me like I said something."

"You did," Sera said, but she let it go, and Kade put it in the file behind the roommate and the Sol thing and the Kepler feed, and the file had a shape now that he did not like and could not yet name.

They stayed until the heaters clicked. Mara made him say the second list out loud once more so she could hear whether he changed it. Sera counted his stretches with her eyes closed and got the number right. Eli fell asleep against the parapet and had to be steered to the stairs. Nobody asked what he was going to do, and that was the only reason he was able to say it.

"I'm going to find out where somebody learned it," he said. "Not alone. Mom's got that in writing."

"Good," Mara said. "Bring the second list."

Later, in the room, with Eli breathing on the other side of it, he took the card out of his jacket. Four triggers in Park's hand and Hale's, and his thumb under them. He turned it over. The back was blank. Printed for nothing, meant for nothing.

He took the pencil out of the drawer. Tooth marks near the tip. A man who counted stairs had chewed it, and had been sent to a site that was not a port, and had been excited, and had not been allowed to say why.

He wrote on the back of the card, in a hand steadier than it had been on the duty sheet, and drew a line under the last two words.

*Nobody has learned this at Asterion.*

*Then where.*

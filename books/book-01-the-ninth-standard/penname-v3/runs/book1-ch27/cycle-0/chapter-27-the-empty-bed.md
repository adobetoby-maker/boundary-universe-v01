# Chapter 27 — The Empty Bed

Kade went into Room 814 a little after six in the morning, because a second-year needed the stairs and he was sitting on them.

Eli's half of the room was too neat. That was wrong in a way it took him a moment to place. Eli tidied when he was nervous, and he had been nervous about Friday all week, so the cables were coiled and the bench mat was square to the desk edge. The drone rotor he had promised to fix three weeks ago sat in the exact middle of the mat, still unfixed, as if it had an appointment.

Above the workstation the fellowship application was still open, pale letters hanging in the air where he had left them.

LOW-COST FIELD STABILIZATION FOR CIVILIAN RESPONSE.

Under the title, in the margin notes, Eli had written to himself: *Version seven still too expensive. Stop pretending rare materials are free just because Asterion buys them.*

Kade sat down on his own bed and looked at the other one. The blanket had a dent in it the shape of a tablet.

People began arriving at seven. Most of them did not come in. Food appeared on the shelf by the door, covered, with notes. A card came from the first-year engineering cohort, signed so thickly that the names ran up the sides. Kade answered everyone with the smallest words he owned. He had a talent for making himself unavailable, and it came back as easily as a language he had grown up speaking. He could close the doors and make the room smaller, and need less from everyone. It had always worked before.

It did not work in here, because Eli had spread too far into the room. There was a scorch mark over the desk, and a dent in the ceiling from an incident with the drone that the whole floor had officially agreed never happened. Twenty-three cables hung on hooks, each one labeled in handwriting only Eli could read. The protein powder stood on the windowsill beside the soldering iron. He had been willed one of those and forbidden the other, at lunch, yesterday.

On the desk was the mug. WORLD'S MOST ADEQUATE ENGINEER. Kade had bought it as a joke after the House Trial, and Eli had drunk out of it every day since. Kade hated that buying it now felt like one of the important things he had done that year.

At nine a professor from Systems Lab knocked on the open door and waited to be asked. She was carrying a dented steel tool case with a strip of orange tape wound round the handle.

She set the case under the desk and pressed the broken latch twice with her thumb, as though it might have changed its mind.

"He repaired this latch on everybody's case but his own," she said.

"That sounds like him."

She held out a slate with a form on it. TEMPORARY CUSTODY OF STUDENT PROJECT MATERIALS. Under Eli's name there was a line for a responsible recipient.

"Administration needs somebody to acknowledge the transfer," she said. "It doesn't assign ownership."

Kade kept his hands where they were. "Why me?"

"Because Navarro listed you as his emergency room contact."

It was not an emergency contact, which would have been his mother. It was an emergency *room* contact, because in Eli's view a room could have an emergency too, and somebody ought to be on file for it. Kade nearly smiled, and it hurt.

"What happens if I sign?"

"The case stays here until the investigation releases it. If nobody takes custody, academy storage collects it this afternoon."

He thought of Eli's tools turning into inventory on a numbered shelf while the blanket still had a dent in it. He took the slate.

"There's no inventory on this," Kade said.

The professor looked at him more carefully. "No, there isn't."

"So I'm taking custody of an undefined set."

"That is exactly what you'd be doing."

"He'd hate this form."

"He filed three complaints about it last term. I countersigned the second one."

So Eli was not a ghost in the building. He was a procedural irritation, still lodged in it.

She opened the case. Every tool sat in its own fitted slot, except for one empty space the shape of a narrow wrench. Inside the lid was a panel, and when she woke it a list came up, forty-two entries long, each with a serial number and a note on its condition.

"He made his own," she said.

Of course he had. At the bottom, under the last entry, Eli had added a paragraph.

*If this case is being returned because I am dead, missing, expelled, or finally arrested for unauthorized fabrication, check Lab Five for the ten-millimeter torque key. It is not lost. It is being useful somewhere else.*

Kade read it through twice. He knew where the key was. It was in an orange tool roll in Lab Five, and he had handed Eli that roll himself.

"Can I sign his list instead of yours?"

She turned the panel toward him. "I'll attach it to the form as the controlling inventory."

He signed under Eli's paragraph. His hand went unsteady halfway through his surname, and the professor became very interested in the broken latch until he was done. She closed the lid and left the orange handle turned outward, where a person could grab it.

"The fellowship committee has suspended his review," she said at the door. "Suspended is not rejected."

Kade's throat closed. "Do you think that distinction helps?"

"I don't think anything helps today." She settled her bag on her shoulder. "But it's his, so I'm keeping it accurate."

That helped more than comfort would have.

---

At eleven a housing coordinator stopped at the threshold with a tablet against her chest, and did not step over the sill. She would need a House representative later, she said, because Meridian could place a hold before housing made any disposition. For now she needed the roommate to confirm dependent devices, hazardous materials, living organisms and perishables.

"Does protein powder count as hazardous?" said Kade.

"Not under housing policy."

"It should."

It came out on its own, and for about one second the room sounded the way it had on Tuesday. The coordinator waited. He told her about the discharged battery stack under the desk and the drone cells in the wall locker. Then he opened the little cold drawer between the desks. It held half a box of noodles, two nutrient drinks, and a piece of fruit that had crossed over from food into research. Every item had a strip of tape on it. MINE. ALSO MINE. And on the noodles: KADE, THIS DOES NOT BECOME YOURS WHEN I AM IN CLASS.

He shut the drawer. "There are some perishables. I don't want them cleared today."

She marked it. "A hold doesn't oblige you to preserve spoiled noodles. Most people need that said to them twice. Nothing in here moves before I come back, unless it's unsafe."

Sera came at noon with a strip of blue cloth, and tied it round the post at the foot of Eli's bed without asking.

"What is that?"

"Seven-day hold."

"On the room?"

"On the person." She worked the knot tight. "When somebody in this House goes missing, or dies, or gets evacuated or arrested or transferred overnight, nobody clears their room for seven days."

"That's a strange list."

"Meridian has history."

"Why seven?"

"So administration can't turn a person into available capacity before their friends have found out what happened."

"And after the seven?"

Sera looked at the bed. "Depends who comes back."

She had said *who comes back*. She had not said *what gets taken away*. Kade heard the difference and looked somewhere else, before hope could sharpen into a thing that cut.

She was holding the loose end out to him. "Pull."

"You've already tied it."

"The first knot marks the bed. The second one means somebody's accepted the hold."

"Accepted what, exactly?"

"Seven days of answering to housing. Stopping people from turning this room into a shrine, which he would hate. And leaving it yourself, at least twice a day."

"You invented the last one."

"It's the oldest part of the rule."

He took the cloth. It had been cut from something bigger a long time ago, and handled so often since that its edges had gone soft.

"Whose was it?"

"No idea. There's a roll of it in the bell cabinet. You don't get a fresh strip just because your grief is special."

It sounded cruel, and then he understood it. Other hands had pulled this same cloth tight. Meridian had not solved any of those absences. It had kept a roll of cloth, so the next person would know the House had some idea what to do for a week.

Kade pulled. Sera finished the second knot, and it sat crooked on the post.

"Now what?"

She showed him the House schedule on her tablet. Every hour from now until the memorial had a name against it. There was a name for meals, one for faculty contact, one for housing, and one for door duty during visiting hours. One line only said SIT HERE IF HE ASKS. Six people had signed up for it before the slot filled.

Something in his chest hurt. "Who made this?"

"Julian started it. Priya threatened him until he handed it over and let Meridian run its own room."

"He isn't even in this House."

"That was Priya's point, and she made it loudly."

At the bottom of the schedule there were two controls, DOOR OPEN and DOOR CLOSED.

"That one's yours," said Sera. "Change it whenever you like."

Kade chose CLOSED. His thumb was already moving away when he made himself go back and set it to open again at two o'clock. He knew what he did with closed doors. Sera watched him do it, and did not remark on it, and that was the reason he left it set.

---

The door opened at two, and at ten past Mara came in. She saw that he was awake. She sat down on the floor with her back against the side of Eli's bed and her knees drawn up, and said nothing. She did not ask him anything, and she did not put on a careful face for him.

They stayed like that for twenty minutes. She looked once at the application floating over the desk, and left it alone.

His tablet buzzed four times in a row. One request for comment came from the academy bulletin and two came from mainland news services. The fourth offered payment for an exclusive account of what it called the Lab Six tragedy. Kade turned the tablet face down on the blanket.

"I could sort those," said Mara. "People who need an answer, people who believe they're owed one, and people who should be introduced to Sera."

He passed her the tablet. She held it without waking it.

"Tell me what I'm allowed to open."

*Everything* was in his mouth, because deciding took effort and he had very little to spend. But that would have been exhaustion answering for him, and she would have known it, and she would have asked again.

"Today's messages only. Not my mother's, and not Darius's. Don't reply to anybody. Just flag them."

She went to work without narrating it. The media went behind a filter and stopped buzzing. She slowed at a note from the training office about his evening slot.

"Cancel it," said Kade.

"Today's, or all of them?"

The question irritated him, and that told him it mattered. "Today's. Just today."

She cancelled one session and left the rest of the term alone.

Near the end a new message came in from Darius, and she turned the screen so that he could read the preview without opening the thread. *No answer needed. Your mom ate. I checked.*

Kade swallowed. "Flag that."

"Which pile?"

"People who need an answer."

She marked it. Then she laid the tablet on the floor between them, locked, rather than keeping hold of it.

"You don't have to stay," Kade said after a while.

"I know I don't."

That was why he could let her.

Sera came back toward evening with a notebook, the paper kind, fat with use and held shut by a rubber band.

"It was on a bench in Lab Five. The responders brought it up to staging with everything else they cleared."

Kade looked at it in her hands. "Did you read it?"

"It isn't mine to read."

He took the notebook and held it on his knee. Sera sat down on his desk chair. For once she did not steal any of the food on the shelf, or tell him what he was doing wrong.

"How did it go this morning?" he said. "The assessment office. Did you walk in?"

She blinked. "Why do you care about that today?"

"Because you were good last night. I watched you."

She looked at the window. "They've offered me accelerated assessment for the Response Corps."

"Sera. That's enormous."

"It probably is."

"You are terrible at being happy."

Her jaw set. "Eli is dead."

The sentence filled the room. Kade looked at the dent in the blanket.

"Presumed," he said.

She shut her eyes. "Kade."

"I know what it means. I'm only keeping it accurate."

She breathed out slowly. "Part of me was glad. When the commander said it to me, in that hall, with the glass still white. Part of me was glad, and it hasn't stopped. What kind of person does that make me?"

Kade thought about the week after the House Trial. He had liked the applause, and been ashamed of liking it, at the same meals.

"I don't know," he said. "But I don't think one feeling cancels the other. I think they just both sit there, and you have to carry them around together."

Sera's face eased a little. "Look at you, growing."

"Please stop."

She very nearly smiled. At the door she put two fingers on the blue knot, quickly, as someone might touch a friend's shoulder in passing, and then she was gone.

---

Asterion went back to class the next morning. The east engineering wing stayed sealed, but breakfast was at seven, and Vector Analysis was at eight, and the ranking boards would update at noon as they did every day. Kade hated the academy for carrying on. He also knew that ten thousand students could not hold still for one missing boy most of them had never met. It felt like a betrayal anyway.

Taren was on the eighth-floor landing, standing well out from the wall with his hands in plain view.

"I put my name down for the door," he said. "Priya took it off. She was right to. I wanted you to hear it from me."

Kade had a dozen answers, and none of them were true yet. "I don't know what to do with you. I'm not going to work it out this week."

"I know," said Taren, and stepped back to let him have the stairs.

Professor Amadi did not mention Eli when Kade came in by himself. She looked at the empty seat beside his and said, "Navarro's assignments are suspended," and began the lecture. It was the same word the professor from Systems Lab had chosen.

Halfway through the hour she set a three-person coupling problem. Kade turned to his right, already starting to speak, and found the empty chair there. His mind stopped.

Across the aisle, Owen Park had looked at the chair as well. Owen had once argued with Kade for a whole period that a field solved symmetrically even when the bodies in it did not. He slid his tablet over to make room.

"You can work with us."

The refusal was ready before Kade had chosen it. *Don't replace him. Don't make the empty place any less empty.* He looked at the thought properly, and it was stupid. Sitting next to Owen for forty minutes would not rub anybody out.

"All right," he said, and moved his chair.

The rig had three linked field plates and a load that travelled across them. One operator held the center plate. One moved power between the two outer plates. The third watched the load and called each transfer before the load reached a seam. Kade took the center, because that was where he always stood. Owen's partner, a quiet girl from Sol, took the outer plates, and Owen opened the instrument channel.

Amadi started them. Kade steadied the center plate and waited for the low steady stream of numbers from his right. None came. The load reached the first seam, and the left plate sagged. Kade pulled power across after the transfer had already begun, and all three plates went red.

DISCONTINUITY: 0.31 SECONDS.

Amadi reset the rig and said nothing.

"You didn't call the transfer," said Owen.

"Instruments call it."

"Not in this exercise. Read the sheet."

Owen was right. Eli had always called anyway. He had never believed the edge of his own job was a reason to let a system fail quietly.

"Again," Kade said.

On the second run he tracked the load himself, called the left transfer and shifted the center to cover. Owen read him the seam value after the load had crossed.

"I need it earlier than that."

"The sensor only confirms once it's over."

"Eli could predict it off the acceleration curve."

He had said it before he could stop it. Owen's hands stayed where they were on the instrument controls.

"I'm not Eli."

Kade's face went hot. "I know that."

"Then stop handing me his job."

The load reached the second seam while they were looking at each other. The right plate dropped, and the girl caught it with a rough overcorrection that kept the system alive and wrecked their efficiency. The run closed amber.

Owen pointed at the acceleration display. "I can widen the sensor window. You'd see the curve before the seam. It brings in a lot of noise, though."

"How much noise?"

"Enough that you can't trust the raw figure."

Eli would have cleaned that noise in his head, talking the whole time. Eli was not there.

"Then don't give me a figure," Kade said. "Give me which way it's going, and how sure you are."

Owen changed the display. The fine trace collapsed into three bands, rising, steady and falling, each with a confidence beside it.

"That's less information," said the girl.

"It's information we can use in time."

Third run. "Rising. High confidence," said Owen.

"Left in two," Kade called. The girl readied her plate. "One. Transfer."

The load went over the seam. The center gave up power at the instant the left plate took it, and nothing flashed. At the second seam Owen called medium confidence. Kade held half a count longer, and the girl adjusted her timing to match without waiting to be told.

The load ran off the end. Their score was not elegant, but the run had stayed stable from one end to the other.

Owen saved the configuration. "Navarro would hate those bands."

"He'd build five better ones by dinner, and then insist he'd agreed with you from the start."

The girl laughed, briefly. Kade's hands tightened on the desk edge, and after a moment they let go. Somebody could be nothing like Eli and still help him build something that worked.

They placed third in the section. He did not care about placing third, and not caring worried him.

Amadi stopped him at the door. "Mercer. Grief does not excuse missing every assignment."

The anger arrived hot and welcome, and she went on before he could use it.

"It does justify asking for help before you miss them." His tablet chimed with a revised workload. "Two extensions and one office hour. No penalty."

The anger had nowhere left to go. "Why did you say the first part like that?"

"Because you would have heard pity in anything softer."

He disliked how right she was. "Thank you."

"Do the work," said Amadi, and went back to her desk.

Outside, the Spire elevators carried students up and down as if nothing had happened. He would have to go on living in the place that was left, and nobody had mentioned that part of belonging beforehand.

---

The investigators came that afternoon, two days after the blast. Eli's tablet had gone into the spine with him, so what they wanted was the desk dock, along with the workstation core under it. A systems officer in a gray incident jacket unplugged them both, carefully, and wrote the cable labels down without trying to read them.

"What are you looking for?" Kade asked.

"Network activity before the incident."

"He used a dead maintenance channel that night. Null seven. He said there was an open ticket for it from March."

The officer looked up, and then wrote that down too. "Anything else?"

"He found the lab's status traffic being mirrored off campus."

The officer hesitated. "We have that. Some status packets were duplicated to an unauthorized endpoint. That much I can tell you."

"Was it the Directorate?"

"We haven't attributed it publicly."

"That isn't what I asked you."

"I know it isn't. It's the answer I've got."

Kade nearly pushed. Then the officer, winding a cable, added one more thing. "There's also an unresolved device identifier from the lower lab."

Hope was moving before he could stop it. "What does that mean?"

"It means a device stopped reporting before the main coolant failure, and it never reappeared on the recovery network."

"Was it his tablet?"

"It was different hardware."

"What sort?"

"We don't know. It doesn't match anything in the registry." The officer's face stayed professional, and was not unkind. "Don't turn an incomplete log into a story, Mercer."

"What if the story's already in there?"

"Then the evidence for it will still be in there tomorrow."

When the officer had gone, Kade sat beside the bare patch of desk where the dock had been. One device identifier could be a sensor, or a maintenance controller, or a piece of somebody's broken drone. He would not let it be Eli. He had worked out at a gray door what that kind of wanting was for. Hope without evidence was just another way of getting out of the room.

He opened the notebook. He kept to the project section, which had a tab marked PROJECT. The pages behind the other tabs he left shut.

One page had a heading printed large enough for anybody.

WHY CIVILIAN STABILIZATION MATTERS.

And under it, in ordinary handwriting: *Because people should not need to be rich, military, famous or lucky to survive the weird future we are building.*

Kade read it three times. He had known what Eli wanted. He had not known how much he wanted it. The application hanging over the desk stopped looking like paperwork to him. It was a future that had not happened yet.

Farther on there was a line in a different pen. *If version eight works, ask Sera to break it. She will enjoy that too much.*

The last written page was dated the night of the blast, and the ink was smeared, because it had been copied off the back of a hand. *GO / NO-GO. One trip equals what? Set at placement. Must be wrong in the right direction. Send to S before Friday. She will mark where it kills people. (Enthusiasm.)*

Kade laughed once, out loud, in the empty room. Then he put both hands over his face. The laugh hurt more than crying would have. He cried as well, and did not try to stop it.

---

At dinner Meridian left Eli's chair empty. Nobody had arranged it, and nobody sat there. His plate stayed in the cupboard, not at his place, because the House apparently had a rule about that too. Kade sat down next to the gap.

For ten minutes the long table was quiet. Then Priya began complaining about a conditioning instructor, and Anton Bell told her she was wrong, in detail. Nessa pushed her hood back and told a story about a second-year who had field-locked himself inside a storage room and tried to pass it off as meditation. Somebody laughed, and the mood of the table changed.

Kade felt anger go through him. Then he remembered Eli laughing in every room where anything had ever gone wrong, and the anger came apart in his hands. He let himself laugh at the second-year. The chair beside him stayed empty while he did it.

---

Julian came by after dinner. By then Kade had worked out who was making problems disappear. A room rotation had been quietly covered, and a missed training block explained to an instructor who never asked about it. Julian announced none of it. Things that should have landed on Kade simply failed to arrive.

"You gave Priya the schedule," Kade said. "You're still doing all the rest. Why?"

Julian leaned on the edge of Kade's desk. "My father says leadership is what people call control when the outcome is good."

"That sounds healthy."

"It's not." He looked at his hands. "I told you his rule, over the washing up. I've spent two days discovering how much of it is still in me. Every list I write makes me feel better, and I know perfectly well that it's the wrong sort of better."

"You don't believe him."

"I'm trying not to. It turns out that's a separate job." Julian looked across at the empty bed. "I can organize meals, and I can't fix this. I keep needing to say that out loud."

"Welcome to the group," said Kade.

Julian gave him a tired smile. On his way out he tipped his head at the blue strip. "My House does three days."

"Meridian does seven."

Julian studied the crooked knot a little longer. "Seven is better," he said, and went.

Every House on the island, it seemed, kept a custom for people who did not come back on schedule. Kade did not like what that said about Asterion.

---

The old instinct came back after midnight.

*If caring can do this, care less. If belonging makes a room hurt this much, get a smaller room.*

He sat on the floor with his back against his own bed. Mara had gone at eleven, because he had asked her to sleep. Sera had response training at six. Julian had left three messages he had not opened. Darius had sent three terrible jokes and then one line that said only, *I'm here.* His mother had sent a photograph of the Northline vending machine, because Darius had apparently convinced her it would help.

None of it made the hole any smaller. It did not make the world any smaller either, and the world was what he had been trying to shrink.

At six minutes past midnight he called Darius.

Darius picked up before the first ring finished. The picture shook, showed a ceiling tile, and settled on half a face behind the Northline security desk.

"Hey," said Darius.

Kade had not prepared a second line, and Darius did not supply one. Behind him the Room Four vending machine hummed, as it had through every disaster Kade ever carried into that building.

"Why are you at Northline at midnight?"

"Night guard called out. And your mom was reorganizing a kitchen drawer by frequency of use, so I left before she gave me a category."

"She does that when she's scared."

"I know she does."

"Is she okay?"

"She isn't," said Darius. "She's scared and she's trying to be useful."

It should have been too blunt. It was a relief instead. Nothing at Northline had become less ugly while he was away.

"She ate. She called the academy twice, and they told her what they told you."

"Tell me one thing about him," Darius said. "I know he's an engineer. I know he sent me a six-page correction to a joke, because my battery math was inaccurate."

"It was inaccurate."

"See, that's one. Give me one I haven't got."

Kade nearly refused. He had spent two days turning down strangers who wanted Eli made into a story. But Darius was asking for a piece small enough to carry.

"He labels his food like it's contested territory."

"That's reasonable, with you in the room."

"He talks in his sleep before deadlines. Once he recited a load table for eight minutes. I recorded it, because I thought he was awake and being strange."

"Tell me you kept that."

"He found it and deleted it."

"Tragic," said Darius, lightly, and they both heard the word land heavier than he had thrown it. Kade looked up at the pale letters over the desk.

"He wanted to build rescue gear that ordinary people could afford. I told you that."

"You told me."

"I didn't understand it. I thought it was a project. I thought he liked it because he likes fixing things. But he had all of it worked out, Darius. He knew which county services couldn't buy academy materials, and what their purchasing limits were. He was designing for one nurse on a night shift, and he'd never met her."

"Sounds annoying."

"Extremely."

"Good."

Kade looked back at him. "Good?"

"Now I've got something specific to miss."

That went straight through him. Kade pressed the heel of his hand against his mouth, and Darius stayed on the screen. He did not promise that Eli would be found, and he did not warn that he might not be. He kept the call open.

When Kade could talk again he said, "I don't know what to say to my mom."

"Tell her you're alive."

"She knows I'm alive."

"Parents are inefficient. They require updates."

Kade opened his mother's thread and typed. *Still here. Ate dinner. Memorial tomorrow. I'll call you after.* He held it up to the camera. "Too much?"

"For your mother? That's barely proof of life."

He added *I love you* and sent it before he could tidy it. Darius nodded once.

"Want me to keep talking? I could explain why the vending machine is no longer permitted to accept credit."

Kade let his head rest against the bed frame. "That sounds like it was your fault."

"It's a story with multiple valid interpretations."

Darius started at the least relevant point he could find. The story was terrible. The call ran twenty-seven minutes, and Kade did not once look at the clock to see when it would be over.

Sera found him still on the floor at twenty to one. She had a bowl.

She put it on his desk, and he did not touch it, and she sat down beside him on the floor with her boots out in front of her.

"He mattered," she said.

Kade shut his eyes. "I know he did."

"Then let it hurt."

He looked across at the bed and the crooked blue knot, and asked her the question he had been circling for two days.

"What if this is the part I was right about?"

"Which part?"

"That needing people hands them a way to hurt you."

Sera was quiet for some time.

"Of course it does," she said.

He turned his head. She shrugged.

"What did you think the other choice was? Some trick where you get to have people and there's no risk attached?"

"That would be nice."

"It doesn't exist. I looked for it for years, in worse places than this one."

He hated the answer. He could not find anything wrong with it.

She got up. "Memorial's tomorrow."

"I can't do it."

"You can."

"I don't want to."

"That's a different thing, and you know it." She was at the door when he said her name, and she looked back.

"The unresolved device. The one that dropped off before the coolant went. Do you think it means anything?"

Her face stayed very still. "I think the investigators have incomplete data."

"That was careful."

"It was meant to be." She looked at Eli's bed and then at him. "Don't use hope to skip grief, Kade. If something's there, it'll still be there when you've done this properly."

He nodded. She left, and pulled the door most of the way shut behind her, though she did not close it.

He opened his messages. The unsent line was still sitting there. *Spine C. They came in by spine C, like you said.* He did not send it, and he did not delete it. He would not let it turn into a prayer, and he would not let it turn into an ending.

He stayed awake until the window went gray. For the first time in years he did not tell himself that none of it mattered. He did not make a miracle out of a missing device number. He did not take the empty bed as proof that caring had been the mistake. The room was too large, and he left it that way, with the blue strip tied to the post.

When it was light he ate the cold bowl, because somebody had carried it up eight floors. Then he got dressed for the memorial.

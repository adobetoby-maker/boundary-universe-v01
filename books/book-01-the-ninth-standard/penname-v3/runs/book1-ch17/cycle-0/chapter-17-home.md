# Chapter 17 — Home

*Forty-seven. Mom says Thursday. I said you don't go back to places, you told me that in ninth grade with your whole chest. She said you're not going back, you're visiting, which is a different thing. Tessa says bring the chips. That part's from Tessa. The rest is from me.*

Northline was smaller than he had left it, and that was the first thing he held against it.

Nothing had moved. The tire warehouse still leaned its stacks against the shared fence. The self-storage sign still had the bottom corner gone out of its S, so that from the curb it read like a word somebody had started and thought better of. The blue on the south wall had faded another shade toward bandage. Through the front glass he could see the vending machine in its alcove and the district loop running silent above it, the woman at the overpass with her hand up, the seawall, the arena. Thirty-one years of Resonance. Your turn.

He had stood in front of that screen forty-three mornings and never once looked up.

"You've been staring for three minutes," Elena said.

"I'm checking the building's still load-bearing."

"It held up a school before you and it's holding one up now. Go." She had come off nights at seven and driven to the terminal on coffee, and she had the look she got at the end of a shift, everything about her tired except the part that watched. She turned off the engine, and the car did the thing it did now, the shudder that ran up through the seat after the key was out, that Kade had started hearing as a number on page two of a contract.

He did not move.

"Mom."

"What."

"Thanks for driving."

"I wanted to see Alvarez," she said. "You're incidental." But she waited for him to get out first, and she brought the bag of chips from the back seat without being asked, because she had been at the store when he bought them and had said nothing, which was its own kind of remark.

The metal detector was working. That stopped him in the doorway harder than the building had. Somebody had come out and fixed it, the gray arch that had stood dead since March with its cord coiled on the floor behind it, and now a green light sat on top of it and it chimed at Elena's keys, one clean note, official.

Mr. Boyd looked up from a bicycle wheel he had clamped to the edge of the desk, looked at the arch, looked at Elena, and waved her through.

"District came out the week of the vans," he said. "They wanted the front to look like a school on camera. It's been beeping at me ever since." He set the spoke wrench down. "Mercer."

"Boyd."

Boyd took him in, the whole length of him, the way he checked a bicycle somebody swore was fine. "You taller?"

"No."

"Shame. Card."

Kade held up the band with the visitor pass lit on it, and Boyd looked at that for a while with no expression, and then at the roll of electrical tape on his desk, and back.

"Tessa told a man with hair you weren't allowed on county property."

"I heard."

"I wrote it down." He nodded at the arch. "Go on. I'm not going to make you walk through it twice. Good to have you home."

"It's good to be home," Kade said, and then heard it, and there was nothing to do about it. The word was out in the front hall with the chili smell and the floor cleaner, where anyone could pick it up.

Elena did not look at him. She had the decency, and also the patience, and he knew the difference, and he knew that patience was the one that came back later.

---

The hall was lower than he remembered. The poster beside the fire extinguisher still said CHOOSING YOUR FUTURE and still hung a quarter-turn off level, and he had spent four months hating that poster and now it looked like a friend who had not gotten the joke either.

Room Four heard him before it saw him. Darius stood so fast that his chair went into the wall, and said, at a volume the building had not been built for, "Zero!"

Every head came up.

"I took a boat and a car for that."

Darius crossed the room and put both arms around him and lifted, briefly, and set him down, and stepped back. "That didn't happen."

"Agreed."

Tessa had her hand out before he had finished being put down. He gave her the chips, and she read the bag, and read the flavor, and nodded once, the nod of a debt formally closed, and Miguel, at the back, in a coat with one sleeve shorter than the other, gave a small silent thumbs-up, as if the chips had been on his behalf too.

Ms. Alvarez was at her desk with the coffee at half, which was the hour, and her arms folded, which was the weather.

"Mercer."

"Alvarez."

"You look less irritating."

"It's the distance."

Her mouth moved at one corner. Then she came around the desk and hugged him, once, hard, no speech attached, and let go and looked at Elena over his shoulder.

"Still alive?"

"Barely."

"Him, or the emergency department?"

"Yes."

He decided he could not hear them and went to stand by his old desk, and the room came at him.

They wanted the island. They wanted to know if it floated. They wanted Julian Cross, whether he was that fast in person, and whether it was true that Kade had fought him, and whether the fight had been real.

"He put me on the floor eleven times in a morning," Kade said. "It was real. He was doing me a favor."

"That's a weird favor," Tessa said.

"He's a weird person."

"Is Mara Vey actually that scary?"

He did not answer that, which was a mistake, and Darius said, "Oh," slowly, with a whole story in it, and Kade considered the shuttle schedule back to the island.

"I didn't say anything," Darius said.

"You were about to."

"I've got all day."

The floating question came back around, and he told them the truth, which was that an island was in water and those were usually the rules, and that the outer platforms moved and some of the bridges retracted, and half the room groaned like he had let the air out of something.

"You go to school next to a tire warehouse," he said.

"I maintain standards," Darius said.

Then a girl by the window, who had not laughed at anything, said, "Your van was in the loading zone two days."

The room turned a little toward her.

"It wasn't my van."

"It had your name in it. Miguel couldn't get his bike around it. Boyd had to go out and move a cone." She said it flat, not a complaint, an entry in a ledger. "The man with the hair asked me if you were always special. I said I'd been here five weeks and you were a clip."

"That's fair."

"I know it's fair. I'm telling you what it was like."

He looked at her and let it sit, because it deserved to sit. Five weeks. Boyd had known all forty-two by name when Kade left, and there were faces in here he did not have. A boy near the heater, thirteen or fourteen, with a tablet he held like it might be taken from him, who had been watching Kade the whole time without saying anything and now did.

"What does nineteen hundred mean?"

Ms. Alvarez said, "Marcus," in the voice that meant the question was allowed.

The room came down a degree. Kade had answered that one for Student Media, with weightings and opponent quality and language that would survive an edit, and none of it was going to help him here.

"It means there are one thousand nine hundred and three people the school puts above me."

"At fighting?"

"At a list of things the school turns into one number."

"Are they all better than you?"

He thought of Mara reading a plan off him three decisions before he had made it. Of Julian's hand coming down onto a mat eleven times. Of Eli building a working sensor bridge from a drawer of parts while eating noodles.

"At something. Probably every one of them."

"But not everything."

"Nobody's better at everything."

Darius pointed at himself, and Kade said, "Naming them would weaken the claim," and the laugh came back, and Marcus did not laugh. He waited for it to be over.

"What does Zero mean?"

That one went through the room like the heater kicking on. The word had gone further than any explanation attached to it, and he could see, in the way the room held still, that it had gotten here first and been living in Room Four without him.

He put a hand on the back of his old chair. It still leaned left.

"It means the first test they gave me couldn't measure what I did."

"So you broke it," Tessa said.

"That's one way to say it."

"What's the other?"

"It asked the wrong question." He caught Alvarez lifting her coffee to cover the beginning of something, and kept going before the sentence could set into a slogan. "Then I spent four months finding out that being right about one question didn't make me good at anything. I came last. Out of everybody. That part they cut."

"Cowards," Darius said.

"For time."

"Show us," Tessa said. "The Conduit. Show us."

He turned his right wrist over. The plate sat along the bone where the technician had seated it, a curve of dull silver with the pale strip beside it, and the band above it with the number on the inside face. The room leaned. He let them look, and then turned it back.

"Now do a thing," Marcus said.

"No."

He did not make it a joke, and the room heard that it was not one.

"I don't use it without a doctor in the building. That's not modesty. There's a card with my name on it and three lines on the card, and if I put a number on any of them in Room Four I'm not getting back on the island." He looked at the water-stained tile over the heater. "Also, I like this building."

"You've never once liked this building," Darius said.

"I'm trying it."

"Alvarez would kill you before the physics did."

"Correct," said Ms. Alvarez.

That got the laugh, and under it Marcus went back to his tablet and traced its edge with one finger, and Kade watched him do it and thought: there's a kid working out the difference between a broken test and a broken student. Good. Let him.

He sat in the leaning chair. He had not meant to. His feet found the two places on the floor that stopped the desk from rocking without asking his permission, the way his hand found the collar of the training set at Asterion, and Tessa opened a geometry module and Darius opened one and did not do it, and the fluorescent tube that buzzed was still the one over the door.

At ten seventeen by the bad clock Darius stood up and said, "Bathroom."

Ms. Alvarez looked at Darius. Kade looked at the clock. Darius looked at both of them, and sat down, slowly, like a man lowering himself into cold water.

"Personal growth," he said.

Kade laughed so hard that Tessa threw an eraser at him, and it hit, and he did not check where it had come from.

---

After the break Alvarez taped three sheets of butcher paper across two tables pushed together and drew Northline on them from memory, six rooms, two halls, the front doors, the dock, the staff room where the chili was, and a red marker line across the east hall where, according to the card, a fire had started above the ceiling. Bottle caps for students. Paper clips for staff.

"Power's out. Emergency lights only. Get everyone off the paper. Eleven minutes to plan, then we run it."

She did not say for whom. She did not have to. Kade had come to sit in on an afternoon block because she asked, and he had nearly said no out of a reflex four years deep, and then remembered that he no longer attended Northline and could not use school as a reason to avoid school.

"How many?" he said.

Tessa read the card. "Forty-three students. Seven staff."

"Forty-nine," Darius said.

"That's fifty."

"I was testing you."

"You were losing to addition."

Marcus wanted the front doors, because they were nearest. Darius wanted the loading dock, because it was big. Tessa said the dock gate failed closed when the power went, and Darius said since when, and Tessa said since always, and Kade kept his hands flat on the table and watched the arrows go down.

They all went to the front. Every group. The paper filled until the front doors looked less like an exit than a drain.

His hands wanted the marker. He could see the whole of it, two narrow halls and a staff room set exactly where a bottleneck would want to be, and at Asterion he would have picked up the pen and solved it before the clock reached ten. Assign a lead per room. Put a clip at the north door. Draw the second route. It would have been correct. It would have been his, and he had spent a Sunday learning what a plan was worth when it needed him on the floor.

"Test it," he said.

Marcus frowned. "It's a map."

"Then it should survive people."

They borrowed the other block and lined everyone up in the hall. Marcus took the stopwatch to the front doors. Darius stayed in Room Four to count. Lacey, who had said nothing all morning and had a way of standing that took up no room, went where Alvarez pointed, and Alvarez cut the lights.

The emergency fixtures came on three seconds later. One of them flickered. Nobody found that surprising.

"Scenario begins."

The line moved, and stopped. Two kids reached the front doors together and each held one side and looked at the other. Somebody behind them asked which side. Tessa came out of the staff corridor and walked gently into the back of the group, and Alvarez called, "Fire in the east hall, you cannot cross the red line," and three people turned around in a hallway that had no room to turn around in, and the whole thing locked like a wheel with a stick in it.

"Fourteen still in Room Four," Darius called. "Fifteen. One's come back."

Marcus looked at the stopwatch as if it had lied to him.

"Time," Alvarez said, at two minutes.

Back at the paper, Marcus crossed out half his arrows without being told. "Nearest is bad."

"Nearest is good," Kade said. "Until everybody knows it's nearest."

Tessa moved nine caps down the back corridor. "Dock."

"Gate fails closed."

"The gate does. The person door next to it doesn't. It's a push bar."

Darius looked personally injured by the building. "Since when?"

"You've been through it."

"I don't accept remembered information."

Marcus was already cutting the rooms into zones with the pen. Front two rooms out the front. Back rooms through the person door. Staff go with their rooms. He moved the clips, and he was fast, and he was nearly right, and Kade was reaching for the one thing the map had left out when Lacey said it first.

"Mr. Omondi can't do either of those if the lift's out."

The table stopped.

Mr. Omondi taught numeracy in Room Six and used a powered chair, and there were two steps down to the dock and a threshold at the front that the chair took at a run on a good day.

Darius put a paper clip at the north door. "Level there."

"And a manual bar," Lacey said. "It's the only door that doesn't need power to open or a step to get out of."

Marcus drew a third route.

Kade looked at Alvarez, and she gave him nothing at all, which was correct. It was not his problem. It was theirs.

"Who checks Room Six?" Marcus said.

"Mr. Omondi checks Room Six," Lacey said. "We ask him what he needs before we make him a job."

Kade looked at her.

"My brother's in a chair," she said, and shrugged, and it was not an explanation. It was a credential.

Different competence. Different piece of the system. He had been one second from drawing a route that treated a man as cargo, and it was nearly the same second Darius had been about to leave Room Four four months ago, and the difference between then and now was that he had kept his hands on the table long enough for somebody to know something he did not.

He picked up a bottle cap and set it back inside Room Four. "You've still got one."

Darius counted. "Forty-three."

Tessa counted. "Forty-two."

They went over the paper. Marcus found it. The restroom at the end of the west hall, and nobody had checked it.

The second run used three routes and sweep zones and a meeting point at the tire warehouse fence. Lacey stood where she could see the north door. Darius carried a paper attendance sheet, because when Alvarez killed the room's router the class tablets lost the building and went to a spinning wheel, and Darius had said, without looking at anyone, "Paper doesn't need a network," and the last kid crossed the marked threshold in seventy-eight seconds.

"Seventy-six," Darius said.

Marcus held up the stopwatch.

"My count includes morale."

"Your count includes lying."

Kade leaned on the wall and let them fight about it. The first plan had been his kind of problem, visible, bounded, waiting for the fastest person in the room. The second had survived because Lacey knew what the map left out, and Tessa knew the building, and Marcus was willing to throw away his own drawing, and Darius, against every precedent, had remembered to count. Asterion had taught him the same lesson with more expensive walls.

"You got weird," Darius said, at his shoulder.

"I've always been weird."

"No. You let him find it. You had it, I watched you have it, and you sat on your hands."

"That's called teaching."

"Exactly."

"Take it back."

Darius did not.

---

Lunch was the same room with the same chairs, and the chairs were worse than memory had kept them. Tessa dropped down beside them with her aunt's latest, which was that Kade was now government property, and Kade asked which aunt, and she said the moon one, and for eight or nine minutes it was exactly what it had been.

Then Tessa went to trade half a sandwich for someone's pudding, and Darius turned Kade's milk carton on the table until the label faced away, which was a thing he did with his hands when he was about to use his mouth for something.

"Forty-seven," he said.

"I know."

"You've answered, what. A handful. One of them was the word stop."

"That was the fifth one."

"I framed it." He did not smile. "You sent a face at Boyd asleep. You sent a number and the word bell. Then you were on every screen in the county going backward over a rail, and Alvarez knew your rank before I did."

There was no volume in it. That was what made it hard. Darius could shout, and shouting would have been a door Kade knew how to walk out of.

"She follows the school reports."

"I follow you."

The refrigerator under the staff counter kicked on. Somebody at the next table was arguing that applesauce was a drink.

"I should have called," Kade said.

Darius blinked, once. He had come armed for something else, and Kade knew the look because he had worn it in every office in the district, the look of a person who has prepared a defense and been handed an agreement instead.

"Yeah," Darius said.

"I kept thinking I'd call when I had something that made sense. First I hated it. Then I didn't, and I didn't have a sentence for that, and I wasn't going to send you a face about it."

"You go to a combat school on a floating island."

"It doesn't float."

"Nothing was going to make sense. That wasn't the deal."

Kade turned the milk back so the label faced him. "I spent four years telling everybody I didn't need a school to want me. Then one did. It put it on a screen."

"Northline wanted you."

"Alvarez wanted me. You did. The district wanted me to stop turning into paperwork."

"Fair."

"So I didn't call, because I was angry, and it wasn't at you, and I couldn't work out where to send it."

Darius looked at Kade's hand on the carton. Not his face. His hand. The last two fingers on the right had started their small separate thing a minute ago, the way they did when he had been sitting upright a while, and Kade had stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing weather, and Darius had not.

"Does that hurt."

"No. It's just a thing they do."

"Since when."

"Since the floor."

"You could have said that too."

Kade looked at him. Darius's face had changed, and it was not curiosity. It was worry held carefully enough that neither of them had to be embarrassed by it.

"I'm okay."

"Wasn't the question."

Kade rubbed his thumb along the seam of the carton. "The hard stuff gives me headaches. There's a doctor who makes me put a number on it every time and stops me before I'd stop myself. The fingers go on their own when I'm tired. The eleven times Julian put me down, I got up eleven times. Nothing on the head. Mom counted it off a sheet."

"She told me. She said it like she was proud of a bruise."

"She was."

"I hate that school."

"You've never seen it."

"I'm building a position from the evidence." Darius pushed the carton back across. "You'll call before you get nationally confusing. Not after. Before."

It was not a promise to get good at staying in touch. He was never going to be good at that. It was a promise to stop using confusion as a reason to disappear, and that one he could make.

"Before," Kade said.

He did not get to sit with it, because Vice Principal Russell came through the lunchroom door with two adults behind him and a smile that had been put on in the hall.

Kade's back went tight before his head had caught up. Old reactions had better memory than people did.

Mr. Hensley had taught sophomore physics at Canyon Ridge. He had once told Kade that being technically correct while refusing the assignment was still failing, and Kade had hated him for the whole of a spring. Ms. Rook had taught English there and had been one of the few adults in that building who stopped arguing when Kade said he needed to leave a room before he said something worse. She stood a half step behind Hensley now, arms folded, and did not look like a person who had asked to come.

"Kade," Russell said. "Some of your old teachers wanted to say hello."

Hensley had his hand out. Kade shook it, because Elena was at the far end of the room with Alvarez and had turned around.

"We always knew," Hensley said. "Extraordinary potential. I said it in a meeting. There's a record."

Alvarez's face, across the room, went entirely still.

"Everyone did," Russell said.

Heat came up under Kade's collar. Not because it was false. Because it was nearly true in the most dishonest way a thing could be true. Russell had told him he was smart. Hensley had told him being smart made every failure less excusable. Every meeting had been a negotiation over how little trouble he could cost the building, and now the story had been rewritten with the ending in place, and everybody in it had always known.

He opened his mouth.

"Kade," said Ms. Alvarez, from across the room, not loud.

One word. She was not telling him to be quiet. She was telling him to choose, which was different, and he had learned the difference in this building.

"You thought I was smart," he said to Hensley.

Hensley's smile eased. "Exactly."

"You also thought smart meant I ought to do well inside a thing that wasn't working, and that if I didn't, it was proof of something about me."

The smile stayed and stopped meaning anything.

"I made it worse. A lot worse." He heard Darius go still beside him. "I didn't turn things in because sometimes I wanted the zero more than I wanted somebody else deciding whether I was good enough. I walked out of rooms. I argued when I knew I was only arguing. I made your job harder and I knew I was doing it. That part's mine, and I'm not handing it to anybody."

Russell nodded, carefully, the way a man nods at a road he is not sure of.

"Northline helped," Kade said, "because the people here stopped treating doing what you're told as the same thing as learning something. She helped." He did not point. Alvarez became extremely interested in her coffee. "You don't have to pretend you saw the island coming. Nobody did. I didn't."

The room had gone quiet, the kind of quiet a lunchroom only manages when something is happening to an adult.

Russell cleared his throat. "I'm only saying we're proud of you."

Kade breathed. "That part I'll take."

Something in Russell's face let go. "Fair enough."

Hensley looked at the floor and then at the door.

Then Ms. Rook said, "I owe you one too."

He turned.

"I knew some of your work was better than the grades. I got tired of arguing about it with people who had the forms." She did not unfold her arms. "I told myself you didn't care enough for it to matter. That was easier than it should have been."

It landed differently because he had liked her.

"I made that easy to believe," he said.

"You did."

"Still."

"Still sorry." She nodded once, and that was all she was going to give it, and it was exactly the right amount.

"Okay," Kade said.

No forgiveness, no speech. A true sentence put down where a false one had been, and it held.

They stayed six minutes and asked about the island and left. Darius looked at him.

"What."

"That was very mature."

"Take it back."

"No. It's gone. Whatever you had here, it's gone."

"Ruined."

"By growth. Tragic."

---

The vending machine took his two credits, and turned its coil, and gave him one bag of pretzels, and then another, and then a third.

He stood and looked at them.

"He was right," he said, when Alvarez came down the hall. "It respects people."

"It respects a bent coil." She leaned on the wall beside the machine. "You handled Russell."

"I had a good teacher."

"Careful."

"That was dangerous?"

"Very." She took the bag he held out and opened it. They stood with the district loop going overhead, silent, the seawall, the arena. "I've got one too. Before your mother comes back with whatever she's carrying."

"One what."

"I saw you were bored. Angry. Defensive. I understood more of it than some people did."

"You did."

"And some of the time I still thought that if you'd just fit better, it'd get easier for you. Sand the corners. Hand it in." She ate a pretzel. "I was wrong about part of that."

It went in deeper than anything Hensley had said. Adults did not apologize to him. Not because he had always been right; he had been wrong often and with invention. Institutions were only very good at arranging a fight so the youngest person in it carried the whole weight of the outcome.

"You weren't wrong about all of it," he said.

"No."

"I was difficult."

"You remain difficult."

"Thank you."

"You're welcome. You were also right, sometimes, for reasons you used badly."

"That's uncomfortably specific."

"You could see when an assignment measured obedience and not understanding. Then you used that as a permit to do nothing." She nodded at the glass of Room Four. "I told you once that you decide a thing doesn't matter before you find out if you're good at it."

"I remember."

"You've stopped. I wanted to say I noticed, so you'd know somebody was counting." She looked through the glass. "They watch you now. Marcus. The girl who keeps the ledger. That's not a compliment. It's a weight."

"Worse."

"You don't have to become a poster."

"Thank you."

"But if you let them believe the island happened because you were secretly fine the whole time, that's a lie, and it's the same lie Hensley told with the ending on it."

He thought of the interview, and the sixty-one on the paper, and the eighty-four. Measurements could be accurate and incomplete. So could stories.

"Okay."

"Okay?"

"I'm experimenting with being teachable."

"Sounds temporary."

"Don't ruin it."

His band lit against his wrist, and he turned it over out of habit, and stopped chewing.

Asterion Visitor Office. Request approved, with conditions. Attachments: nine.

He had asked Julian on Saturday, because Julian knew which office could say yes to thirty visitors without convening a committee, and Julian had returned the request signed inside eleven minutes with a route plan, two dietary spreadsheets, and a note. *This is a good use of House access. Next time bring a head count first.* Under it, in a different hand, one line. *Darius is not permitted to challenge anyone.* Mara had never met Darius. He had described him accurately.

He had asked for thirty.

"Twelve," he said.

Alvarez looked at the band. "Twelve what."

"Places. Meridian's exhibition night, next month. It's the one House event with visitor seating. I asked for the whole of Room Four." He scrolled. "Visitor ferry allocation, twelve seats. One staff escort. Selection by sending school, according to academic readiness, attendance, and behavioral standing."

He read the last line twice.

"No."

Elena had arrived from the office end of the hall with two paper cups and the look of someone who has been reading attachments over a vice principal's shoulder. "No what."

"If they pick on attendance and behavior, the kids who already know how to fit get the boat." He was surprised at how fast it came, and how much heat there was on it. "That's the whole district. That's the small box. Twelve badges for the kids who'd have been fine in a regular school, and a stream for everybody else."

"What do you want instead," Alvarez said. Not opposition. Work. He knew the voice.

He looked through the glass. Marcus was redrawing the evacuation map in a straight hand. Lacey had taken the marker off Darius before he could add a route through the tire warehouse.

"Some places for people who want Resonance work, or emergency systems, anybody who'd use it. Some the staff pick, because you know things I don't. Some by lottery."

"Lottery among who."

"Anyone who does the safety preparation. There's a briefing. Ferry rules, field rules."

"That's still a compliance gate."

"It's a relevant one. Nobody gets hurt on a visitor field because they skipped the part about where to stand."

Alvarez tilted her head a fraction, which was as much as she gave anyone.

"And the kids who can't travel," Elena said. "There'll be some. There always are."

He looked at the twelve. His first thought had already come and gone, and it had been the easy one: get Julian to call somebody. Ask Sen. Spend some of whatever was attached to his name this week on a bigger boat. It might work. It would also mean the whole thing ran through him, and if it ran through him it stopped when he did, and he had learned exactly what that was worth on a Sunday afternoon with his back against a rail.

"Can the exhibition stream here?"

"On the tablets?" Alvarez said. "They lose the building when the router sneezes."

"Eli can make anything talk to anything. The question will offend him, which is how you know it's yes."

"Good."

"And the twelve who go come back with a job. Not a speech. Stations. Arena rules, equipment, House systems, whatever they learn. They run it for everyone who stayed."

Elena looked at him over the cup.

"You've thought about this."

"For forty seconds."

"A record."

Alvarez took the band gently by the edge, read the criteria line, and let it go. "I'll draft selection rules. You'll read them before you object to them in public."

"That takes away my best setting."

"You'll live."

She went toward Room Four with her pretzels and the ocean form, which Elena said was three pages, and which acknowledged that an ocean existed and that visitors would be crossing it.

Elena stayed.

For a while neither of them said anything. The heater came on somewhere in the building, and the clock above the door of Room Four stuck, considered the new minute, and moved its hand late.

"I used to sit in that room," Elena said, "and ask people to give you one more chance."

"I know."

"Some of the time I was asking them. Some of the time I was asking you."

He ran a thumb along the edge of the band. "I know that too."

"Do you?"

Her voice was gentle, which meant the question was not.

"Not then," he said.

She nodded.

"I thought if I said I wanted it to work, then everybody got to decide the failure was mine."

"Some of it was."

"I know."

"Some of it wasn't." She did not say I always knew. She had not always known. Neither had he, and there was room in that for both of them to stand. "Asterion isn't proof they were wrong about you."

"No."

"It isn't proof you were right, either."

"Deeply unfortunate."

"But you kept asking. Your method was just louder." She touched his shoulder, once, which was not comfort exactly. It was closer to a nurse marking a chart. Then she nodded at the door. "Go tell them the number before they hear it from Russell and start a government."

"Darius would be a terrible revolutionary."

"Darius has snacks and free time. Don't underestimate logistics."

---

He had not planned to stand at the front of Room Four, which was probably why it worked.

"Asterion's strange," he said.

Darius raised a hand. "We know."

"I thought everybody there would be geniuses who'd never failed at anything."

"Are they?" Tessa said.

"Some."

"Awful."

"Deeply." He looked around. "There are also people there who messed up. People who came out of three placements before they got there. People who are brilliant at one thing and useless at the next. A girl ranked seventh on the island who plans four decisions ahead and still reads the rules appendix at dinner. A guy who can build a tactical model on a napkin and can't carry one end of a table across a courtyard without complaining for eight straight seconds."

"Who's that?" Darius said.

"My roommate."

"I like him."

"You would." He looked at Alvarez. She waited. "And they're not all better than you because they got there first."

The room went quiet on him, and he felt it, and did the dangerous thing, which was to keep going.

"Meridian has an exhibition night next month. It's a House event. There's visitor seating. I asked if Northline could come."

Darius sat up. "Come where."

"The island."

The room went up like a pan on heat. How many. Would they fly. Could they see the arena. Would there be searches. Could Darius challenge somebody.

"No."

"I didn't finish."

"Still no."

Alvarez had to raise her voice to get them seated, and Kade stood at the front laughing and understood, with no warning, how much it was going to mean to him to have been the one who opened the door.

"There are twelve places," Alvarez said.

The room changed. He watched it happen. Excitement became arithmetic. Kids counted each other. Friends leaned closer. Darius's grin thinned as his eyes went from Tessa to the back row.

Kade felt the door turn into a ladder in his hands, and he did the one thing he had come here knowing how to do.

"Twelve on the island. Everybody else gets the stream, here, on those." He nodded at the tablets. "I know it's not the same."

"It's not," Tessa said.

"The boat has limits. So do the visitor fields. I can ask for more, and I will, and I'm not going to promise you more before somebody's signed it."

"How do you pick?" the girl by the window said.

Alvarez looked at him. His problem.

"Some places for people who want to work in Resonance or emergency systems. Some the staff choose. Some by lottery, among anyone who does the safety prep."

"Staff can pick favorites," Darius said.

Alvarez took a long drink of coffee. "A strong opening argument for your application."

"I believe in transparency."

"Since when?" Tessa said.

"Since the places got limited."

That was honest enough to get a laugh, and Kade let the laugh do its work, and then went on.

"The rules get posted before anyone applies. No secret categories. Nobody picked for looking good in a brochure." Russell was no longer in the room, which was a mercy. "And the twelve who go come back with a job. You run stations for everyone else. Equipment, arena rules, whatever you learned. Not a speech."

"Can I run a combat demonstration," Darius said.

"You can run sitting through a safety briefing."

"My weakest discipline."

"Then train."

It came out in Julian's voice, and he heard it, and disliked it intensely, and Tessa caught it and laughed at him.

"You really did get weird."

Maybe. But the room no longer looked like twelve winners and a crowd. It looked like a group of people with a constrained system to solve, and he had watched that shape do things this afternoon that a marker in his hand could not have.

When it settled, Marcus put one hand up.

"Do they take people from schools like this?"

It was quiet enough that the room bent around it.

Kade looked at him. He thought of a girl in his House with three placements behind her and a manual she read at dinner. He thought of a blue lanyard and a classified back door and a duffel with a repaired strap.

"Yes."

Marcus did not look convinced. "How?"

He almost said he didn't know. That was true.

"Different ways."

"Like Zero?"

"Hopefully not exactly like that."

A few people laughed, and Marcus nearly did.

"But you don't have to have gone to the right middle school at eleven. That's the part they don't put on the website."

Alvarez stepped to Marcus's desk. "We've got the regional aptitude calendar. And application waivers. Anyone who wants them can ask me without telling the room."

Marcus's shoulders came down half an inch, and Kade heard the second offer inside the first. Wanting something did not have to be a performance before it was ready. He had needed that rule at fourteen and had not had it.

"I can answer questions too," he said. "About the parts the website makes sound simple."

Darius put his hand up. "Can you answer why the website's got six login screens?"

"Nobody can."

"Then what good are you."

"I've got a boat."

"Temporarily useful."

Marcus wrote the date in the corner of his tablet, small, where it would not be seen unless he wanted it to be. Not a future. A date. A door began that small, and Kade had stood on the wrong side of enough of them to know.

---

Darius followed them out to the curb with his hands in his pockets and the hood up, which was how he did the things he could not do with his face.

"You coming back."

The old answer arrived on time, something about surviving the island, something true and useless. He let it go past.

"Yeah."

Darius nodded. "Good."

Kade waited. "That it?"

"That's it."

"Very moving."

"Get in the car before I do the other thing again."

He got in the car.

Elena drove. The engine did its shudder, and she went four blocks without saying anything, past the grocery store where he and Darius had once spent an hour arguing about whether a security robot was legally allowed to follow them, past the bus stop where she had picked him up after the second suspension and had not said one word all the way home. The street that went up to Canyon Ridge went by on the left. He did not look up it, and then he did.

"You called it home," she said.

"I didn't."

"At Boyd's desk."

"There's no audio."

"I have ears."

"Unreliable."

She smiled at the road.

He watched the neighborhood go, and it did not get less real for having Asterion behind it. That was the thing he had gotten wrong. He had thought a place could only be home if it was the only one, that leaving one meant it closed behind you, and that was why you did not go back to places, because there would be nothing there to go back to. Northline had stayed exactly where it was. It had gotten a working metal detector and some new faces and a girl who kept a ledger, and it had kept his chair leaning left.

And the island was becoming one too. Not the building. The chair at the table that nobody had to hold for him.

Two homes was twice the number of places that could hurt when something went wrong. He knew that. The old fear turned up, punctual, and he looked at it the way Park had taught him to look at a headache, and gave it a number, and it was real, and it was not a reason to leave.

That was the difference. It had never been the fear. It had been what he did with it.

His mother drove. His reflection sat in the window over the tire warehouse and the storage yard and the road out, a boy who had been told for four years that he did not belong anywhere and had believed it hard enough to make it true, and he looked at the boy for a while, and then let him go past with the rest.

Belonging was not the opposite of being able to get hurt. It was choosing it. On purpose, with your eyes open, twice.

He was not sure he liked that. He was fairly sure it was too late.

# Chapter 17 — Home

*Forty-seven. Alvarez says Thursday, second block, and don't come early because early is a surprise inspection and she's had enough of those. Boyd says bring your card, he's not making an exception for television. Tessa says the chips are a debt, not a gift, and she wants that understood before you get here. Nobody's said the other thing. I'm not going to either.*

The flag halyard was going against the pole when Elena pulled to the curb, the same two-note clank it had made every windy day of his life, and Kade sat with his hand on the door and listened to it as if it might have learned a third note while he was gone.

It had not. The loading zone still had a cone in it where Boyd had walked one out to keep the news van off Miguel's bicycle, and nobody had walked it back. The self-storage sign next door still had the corner gone out of its S. The blue on the south wall had faded another shade toward the color of a bandage that had been on too long. Through the front glass the district loop was running silent over the vending machine, the woman at the overpass, the seawall, the arena, thirty-one years of Resonance and your turn next, and he had walked under that screen every morning for the better part of a year and never once looked up.

"Second block started four minutes ago," Elena said. "You're inside the window."

"I'm counting the cars in the lot."

"There are six. One's mine." She had come off nights at seven and driven straight to the ferry terminal on coffee and stubbornness, and she had the face she wore at the end of a shift, everything tired except the part that watched. The engine ticked. "You can't do the thing where you sit outside until it's over. I know that one. I taught you that one."

He got out.

The metal detector was working. That stopped him in the doorway harder than the building had. The gray arch had stood dead by the front counter since spring with its cord coiled on the floor behind it, and now a green light sat on top and it chimed at Elena's keys, one clean official note. Somebody had come out and fixed it. He knew who, and when, and why, before Boyd opened his mouth.

"District," Boyd said, without looking up from the bicycle wheel clamped to the edge of his desk. "The week of the vans. They wanted the front of the building to beep on camera. It's been beeping at me since." He set down the spoke wrench and took Kade in the way he checked a bike somebody swore was fine, front to back, no hurry. "Card."

Kade held up the band with the visitor pass lit on it. Boyd read it as if it might be forged.

"Visitor."

"That's what it says."

"How long are you visiting."

"Just today. I've got to be home by the last ferry."

He heard it after it was out. Boyd heard it too, and did nothing with his face, which was a courtesy, and Elena, behind him, did nothing with hers, which was not. Kade put the band down and stood in the front hall of the building he had spent four years planning to leave, and the word he had just used for somewhere else sat in the air with the floor cleaner and the smell of chili from the staff room.

"Well," Boyd said. "Good to have you home."

"It's good to be."

"Which one."

"Boyd."

"Go on." He picked up the wrench. "Tessa's been holding the door on her chips since Tuesday. She's not a patient creditor."

Room Four did not shout. He had braced for shouting, and what he got was worse, which was silence, and Darius standing up slowly out of his chair like a man who wanted the moment to be on record.

"He's shorter," Darius said to the room.

"I'm the same."

"He's the same and he was always shorter than the clip." Darius crossed the floor and put both arms around him and lifted him an inch off the tile, and set him down, and stepped back with his hands up as if there had been a foul. "That was for the school. Not me."

"Noted."

Tessa did not get up. She held her hand out from her seat, palm flat, and he put the bag in it, and she turned it over and checked the flavor against whatever she had written down somewhere, and then she nodded, and the debt was closed, and only then did she look at his face.

"You look tired."

"I got up at four."

"You look tired in the face. Not the eyes." She opened the bag. "The eyes are fine."

Miguel was at the back in a coat one size short in the sleeves, and Miguel gave him a nod that was polite and nothing else.

"Your van blocked the rack two days," Miguel said. "I had to bring the bike in past Boyd both mornings. He said it was fine, but it wasn't fine, it was two mornings."

"It wasn't my van."

"It had your name on the side." Not angry. Filed. "I'm just saying where it was."

"That's fair."

"I know it's fair."

Ms. Alvarez was behind her desk with her coffee at the level it reached by second block, and she stood up and came around it and hugged him once, short and hard, no words attached, and then held him off at arm's length and looked at him the way she looked at a form.

"You've stopped doing the thing with your shoulders."

"Which thing."

"Up around your ears. Like you're waiting to be told to leave." She let go. "Somebody's been feeding you, too."

"There's a dining hall."

"I've heard." She looked past him at Elena in the doorway. "You look worse than he does."

"I've been up longer than he has."

"Sit. Both of you. He's got twenty minutes before I use him."

He sat in his old chair. He had not planned to. It leaned left, and his feet went to the two places on the floor that stopped the desk from rocking without asking his permission, and something in his chest that had been standing since the ferry sat down with the rest of him.

The questions came from everywhere. Did he have a bodyguard. Did the rail hurt. Was the food free, and if it was, why did he look like that. Whether the fights were real or the way wrestling was real. Whether Julian Cross was that fast or whether they slowed everyone else down in the edit.

"He's that fast. He put me on a mat eleven times in one morning and I got up eleven times and none of them were on the head. My mother has the sheet."

"I have the sheet," Elena said from the door.

"Was it a real fight?"

"It was a lesson that looked like one. He wasn't trying to win. He was showing me where I lose."

"Is Mara Vey that scary?"

He did not answer that, and the not answering took a second too long, and Darius sat back with his arms folded and said nothing with such enormous restraint that Tessa hit him with the empty chip bag.

Then Marcus asked his.

Kade had not known him. Thirteen, maybe fourteen, by the heater, with a tablet he held in both hands like something that could be confiscated, and he had not laughed at anything, and now he said, "What does one thousand nine hundred four mean," as if the number were a debt and he wanted to know who held it.

The room came down a step.

"It means one thousand nine hundred three people on the island are put above me by a list of things the school turns into one number."

"So they're all better."

"At something. Probably every one of them, at something."

"Then why do they put you on the screen."

That was the real question, and he gave it the real answer.

"Because I started last. Out of ten thousand four hundred eighty-two. They like a number that moves. They don't put up the part where it didn't move for a month, or the part where I nearly came home." He held up the band before Tessa could ask. "The Conduit's under this. You can see the plate. You can't see it do anything, because it doesn't do anything here."

"Why not."

"Because there's a doctor on the island with a card that has my name on it and three lines under my name, and if I put a number on any of those lines in this room, she's the one who finds out, and I don't get back on the boat." He turned his wrist over so the light could get at the dull curve of silver along the bone and the pale strip beside it, and let them look, and turned it back. "Also the ceiling's got a stain over the heater the size of a dinner plate and I'm not the one who's going to make it bigger."

"That stain was here before you," Darius said.

"It's grown."

"Personal growth," Darius said, and looked at Alvarez, and Alvarez looked at the clock, and he sat down again without being asked, and Marcus, by the heater, traced the edge of his tablet with one finger and did not laugh, and Kade let him not laugh. Somewhere in there a kid was working out the difference between a test that could not measure something and a person who could not be measured, and that took time, and nobody had given Kade the time at fourteen. He was not going to take it from Marcus by explaining it.

---

Alvarez did not use butcher paper. She took the laminated evacuation plan off the wall beside the door, the one with the district seal and the date from before Kade had arrived, and put it flat on the two front tables under the projector light.

"Power fails. Emergency lights only. There's a fire above the east hall ceiling, and I've put a red chair in the hall so you'll know where it is. Fifty people in the building. Get them out." She tapped the laminate. "This says how. The district paid for it. Find out if it's true."

"How do we find out," Tessa said.

"You walk it."

Kade had been given to Marcus's group as a body. Alvarez had said so, in front of everyone: he did not know the building anymore, he was a visitor, he could carry a clipboard. Marcus had not looked pleased about it. Marcus had his own idea and had had it before the laminate hit the table, and he laid it out fast and clean with a dry-erase pen on the plastic. Front doors. Everybody. Nearest exit, shortest line, out and count at the flagpole.

It was wrong. Kade saw where it was wrong before Marcus had capped the pen, and his hand actually moved, an inch, toward the pen, before he put it under the table.

At Asterion he would have taken it. He would have drawn the second route and the third, put a lead in every room, solved the whole building before the clock hit ten, and it would have been correct, and it would have been his, and he had spent a Sunday afternoon with his back against a rail learning exactly what a plan was worth when it needed him on the floor to work.

"Front's nearest," Marcus said, daring anyone.

"It's nearest," Kade agreed.

Darius looked at him sideways.

They ran it. Alvarez borrowed the other block for bodies and lined thirty-some people through the rooms, and put Tessa in the staff corridor, and Lacey somewhere Kade did not catch, a quiet girl who stood as though she had been trained not to take up space, and then she killed the lights at the panel.

The emergency fixtures came on. One of them thought about it first.

"Go."

The line moved well for four seconds. Then it reached the front vestibule, and the front vestibule had a metal detector in it.

The arch stood where the district had put it, square in the middle of the way out, one person wide, and the first kid through it set it chiming, and the second kid stopped, because a chime meant stop, because that was what a chime had meant every morning of their lives. The third walked into the second. Somebody near the counter asked if they were supposed to go around it. Boyd said, "There isn't an around it," in the voice of a man who had said that to the district in a memo. Then Alvarez called, "Fire's in the east hall, red chair, you can't cross it," and the three at the back of the pack who had been coming from Room Six turned around in a corridor that did not have room to turn around in, and the whole thing set like concrete.

"Room Four still has nine," Darius called from inside it. "Ten. One came back for a coat."

Marcus stood at the front doors with the stopwatch and looked at the arch as if it had done this to him on purpose.

"Time," Alvarez said. "Two minutes ten. Building's on fire. Back to the table."

Nobody talked on the walk back. Marcus uncapped the pen and drew a line through his own front arrow, hard enough to dent the laminate.

"The arch," he said.

"The arch is new," Kade said. "The plan's older than the arch. What else is the plan older than."

Tessa put her finger on the loading dock. "It says dock. The dock gate fails shut when the power drops, it's on a motor, so this whole arrow's a lie." She moved her finger a centimeter. "But the door next to it. The people door. That's a push bar. It doesn't care about power."

"Since when," Darius said.

"Since you went out it to avoid Russell in March."

"I don't remember doing that."

"You never remember the useful ones."

Marcus was already cutting the building into halves. Front rooms to the front, one at a time through the arch, and don't stop for the chime. Back rooms out the dock door. He had the two arrows drawn and was reaching for a third when Lacey said, quietly, from the end of the table, "Mr. Omondi can't do either."

The pen stopped.

"He's in Six. The chair. Front's got the threshold and the dock's got the two steps down. On a good day he takes the front at speed. This isn't a good day." She was not arguing. She was reading it off the building. "The north door's level. Manual bar. It's the only way out that doesn't need power or a step."

Kade had been reaching for that. He had been one breath from saying it, and what he would have said was, put someone on Mr. Omondi, and Lacey said instead, "Somebody asks him what he wants. You don't make him a job."

"My brother's in a chair," she said, when Kade looked at her, and shrugged, and it was not an explanation. It was a credential.

Marcus drew the third route, north, with Room Six on it, and wrote ASK above it in capitals, and Kade sat on his hands and felt the second pass by where he would have drawn a man as cargo, and it felt exactly like the second on the qualifier floor when the team had put him on a plate and been right to.

"Fifty," Darius said, counting caps on the plan. "Forty-nine."

"You said fifty."

"I said fifty and I count forty-nine and I don't like either."

Tessa found it. The restroom at the end of the west hall. Nobody had a sweep for it. Marcus wrote SWEEP on the west hall and put Lacey's initial on it and Lacey did not object.

The second run used three doors and a chain of people who each knew one thing. Tessa held the dock door with her foot. Lacey stood where she could see the north exit and Room Six both. Marcus went through the arch first so the chime was already ringing when the line arrived and there was nothing to stop for. And when Alvarez killed the router as well as the lights and every class tablet lost the building and went to a spinning wheel, Darius took a paper sheet off her desk and a pencil and stood at the flagpole and counted heads by hand, because paper, as he said to nobody, does not need a network.

Seventy-eight seconds. Marcus held the stopwatch up like a verdict.

"Seventy-six," Darius said.

"It says seventy-eight."

"Mine includes morale."

"Yours includes lying."

Kade leaned on the wall by the fire extinguisher and let them have it. The first plan had been his kind of problem: visible, bounded, sitting there waiting for the fastest person in the room. The second one had worked because Tessa knew the door and Lacey knew the man and Marcus was willing to cross out his own line and Darius, against every precedent in the building, had counted. Nobody had solved it. Everybody had.

Darius came and leaned beside him with the pencil behind his ear.

"You had it," he said.

"Had what."

"The arch. I saw your hand go."

"Yeah."

"You used to just take the pen."

"I used to lose Sundays." Kade watched Marcus redraw the whole thing neat. "There's a girl on the island who'd have had it three moves before I did, and she'd have let him find it too. I'm copying her."

"That's a horrible thing to admit."

"It's cheaper than the other way."

The break was twenty minutes, and Darius spent it on the loading dock with the people door propped on his heel so it could not lock them out, and Kade sat on the edge of the concrete beside him with his legs over the drop where the trucks used to back in, and the tire warehouse breathed rubber across the fence the way it always had.

Darius did not look at him. He looked at the fence.

"Forty-seven," he said.

"I know the number."

"You answered nine. I went back and counted. Nine, and one of them was a face, and one was the word stop, and one was a picture of the ocean with no words on it, which I still don't know what that was."

"That was the ocean."

"I gathered." He took the pencil from behind his ear and turned it over. "Then you're on every screen in the county going over a rail backward, and my mom's watching it at the pharmacy, and Alvarez has your rank on the board before I've got a text. Before I've got a text, Kade. She follows the reports."

"I know she does."

"I don't follow the reports. I follow you."

The tire warehouse fan kicked on across the fence. There was no volume in what Darius had said, and that was the hard part. Darius could shout. Kade had a door for shouting. He had been walking out of it since he was nine.

"I should have called," he said.

Darius's face did a thing, a very small thing, and Kade knew it, because he had done it in every office in the district: the look of a person who has walked in with a whole argument and been handed the conclusion before he could start.

"Yeah," Darius said. "You should have."

"I kept waiting until I had something that made sense to say. First I hated it. Then I didn't, and I didn't have a sentence for that, and I wasn't going to send you a face about it."

"You go to a fighting school on an island. Nothing was going to make sense. That was never the deal."

"I spent four years telling everybody I didn't need a school to want me. Then one did. It put it on a screen with a number under it." He looked at the fence. "And I was angry, and it wasn't at you, and I didn't have anywhere to put it, so I didn't put it anywhere. That's not a reason. It's what happened."

Darius was quiet. Then he was looking at Kade's right hand, on the concrete between them, where the last two fingers had begun their small separate business the way they did when he had been sitting upright for a while, and Kade had stopped noticing it the way you stopped noticing weather, and Darius had not.

"What's that."

"Fingers."

"I know what fingers are. Since when."

"Since the floor. It's on the card. It's the middle line of three." Kade flattened the hand on the concrete and it did not entirely agree. "Doesn't hurt. The hard work gives me a headache after. There's a doctor who makes me put a number on the headache every time and stops me two before I'd stop myself. That's the whole of it. I'd have told you if you'd asked."

"I did ask. Thirteen. I said answer that one."

"You did."

"You sent a face."

"I was fourteen hundred places from the bottom and scared of everything. I'm nineteen hundred from the top now and scared of about half."

Darius put the pencil back behind his ear. When he spoke again he had picked the terms, and they were small, and that was how Kade knew they were the real ones.

"Before. Not after. Next time you're about to be confusing on television, I get a call before. I don't need it to make sense. I need it to be first."

It was not a promise to get good at staying in touch. He was never going to be good at that. It was a promise to stop using not understanding himself as a reason to disappear, and that one he could keep.

"Before," Kade said.

"Good." Darius took his heel off the door. "I'm going to tell everyone you cried."

"I didn't."

"Nobody's going to check."

---

The arch chimed three times in a row while they were coming back up the hall, three sets of keys, and Kade's back went tight before his head knew why. Old reactions had better memory than people.

Vice Principal Russell came through first with the smile he put on in parking lots. Behind him, Mr. Hensley, who had taught sophomore physics at Canyon Ridge and had once told Kade that being right and refusing the assignment was still a zero, which had been correct, and which Kade had hated him for across the whole of a spring. And a half step back, arms folded, looking like a person who had been asked to come along in the car and had not enjoyed the ride, Ms. Rook, who had taught English there and had been one of maybe two adults in that building who stopped arguing when he said he had to leave a room before he said something worse.

"Kade." Russell had his hand out before he was through the arch. "We heard you were in the building. Some of your Canyon Ridge people wanted to say hello."

He shook it. Elena was at the staff room door with a paper cup and had turned all the way around.

"I flagged you," Hensley said. He had the handshake of a man closing something. "Sophomore year. I put it in a meeting note. Exceptional systems reasoning, poor task compliance, and I said, watch this one, and there's a record of that. There's a record."

Heat came up under Kade's collar so fast he could feel the line it made.

It was not that it was false. It was that it was nearly true in the most dishonest way a thing could be true. There had been a record. It had said he was smart. It had also said that being smart made every failure his, that the building was working and the boy was not, and every meeting after it had been a negotiation over how little trouble he could cost the district before the district stopped paying for him. And now the ending had arrived, and everybody in the story had turned out to have seen it coming.

He had a sentence ready. It was a good sentence. It would have gone through Hensley like the arch went through the vestibule.

Then he saw Marcus.

The kid was in the doorway of Room Four with his tablet against his chest, twenty feet down the hall, not hiding, watching the adults do this to somebody, and Kade knew exactly what Marcus was learning from it, because he had stood in that doorway and learned it. He was learning how the story got told about you. He was about to learn how you answered.

"You thought I was smart," Kade said to Hensley. Level.

"Exactly. I said."

"You did say. And you thought smart meant I ought to do fine inside a room that wasn't working, and that if I didn't, it proved something about me and nothing about the room."

The handshake let go on its own.

"I made it worse," Kade said, before Russell could rescue anybody. "A lot worse. I want that on the record too, since we're keeping one. I didn't hand things in because some days I wanted the zero more than I wanted somebody else deciding if I was good enough. I walked out of rooms. I argued when I already knew I'd lost and was only arguing. I cost you time on purpose and I knew I was doing it. That's mine. I'm not handing it to the building."

Russell nodded at that the careful way a man nods at a road he has not driven.

"What changed wasn't that somebody finally saw the potential. Somebody here stopped treating doing what you're told as the same thing as learning something." He did not look at Alvarez, who had come to her door and gone very interested in the laminate she was holding. "You don't have to have seen the island coming. Nobody did. I didn't."

The hall had gone quiet the way a school only goes quiet when something is happening to an adult.

"We're proud of you," Russell said, after a moment. "That's all we came to say."

Kade breathed out. "That part I'll take."

Hensley looked at the floor, and then at the door, and found the door more interesting.

Then Ms. Rook unfolded her arms.

"I had a folder," she said. "Things of yours I meant to argue about. A response paper I gave a fifty because it was three days late and it was the best one in the stack. I'd have argued it with the right person if the right person had ever been in the room. Then I stopped putting things in the folder, because I'd decided you didn't care enough for it to matter, and that was easier than it should have been." She said it plainly and did not dress it. "I owe you that. I'm not asking you to do anything with it."

It went in somewhere the other had not, because he had liked her, and because she had not made it a speech.

"I made it easy to decide that," he said.

"You did."

"Still."

"Still. Yes." She nodded once and refolded her arms, and that was the whole of what she was going to give it, and it was exactly the right amount.

"Okay," Kade said.

That was all. No forgiveness, because none had been asked for. A true sentence set down where a false one had been standing, and it held the weight.

They stayed six minutes and asked about the boat. When the arch had chimed them out again, Darius, at his shoulder, said, "You didn't say the other sentence."

"What other sentence."

"I saw it. You had a good one."

"I had a kid in a doorway."

Darius looked down the hall at Marcus, who had gone back in.

"Huh," Darius said, and let it be.

Alvarez took him outside to walk the cone back.

It had been in the loading zone long enough that the asphalt under it was a different color, and she picked it up herself and carried it to the wall, and set it with the other two, and stood with her back to the school and her coffee in her hand and the halyard going above them.

"I've got a thing to say and I'd like to say it out here, where it doesn't become a lesson."

"Okay."

"I saw that you were bored. And angry. And that you'd been made defensive by people who should have known better. I saw more of it than most." She drank. "And I still thought, some of the time, that if you'd file the corners off and hand the thing in, your life would get easier, and I'd have done you a favor by making you. I was wrong about part of that. Not all. Part."

He had been apologized to by exactly two adults in his life before today and now it was four, and the fourth one landed harder than the third, because she had been on his side the whole time and was saying it anyway.

"You weren't wrong that I was difficult."

"You remain difficult."

"Thanks."

"You were also right, some of the time, for reasons you then used badly. You could see when an assignment measured obedience instead of understanding. You used that as a permit to do nothing." She nodded at the front glass, where Room Four's door was visible down the hall, and the heater side of it, where a kid with a tablet sat. "They watch you now. Marcus. Miguel, though he'd deny it. That's not a compliment. It's a load. You don't have to become a poster. But if you let them believe the island happened because you were secretly fine underneath the whole time, that's the same lie Hensley told, with a nicer ending."

He thought of the sixty-one on the proof, and the eighty-four, and of a girl outside Fresno who had asked whether his school had been wrong about him. Measurements could be accurate and incomplete. So could the story you told a kid about yourself.

"I'm not going to tell them I was fine."

"Good."

"I was not fine. I was a mess with good aim."

"That's the first thing you've said today I'd put on the wall."

His band lit against his wrist. He turned it over out of habit and then stopped, because the sender was the Asterion Visitor Office, and the subject line said APPROVED, and under it, WITH CONDITIONS, and there were nine attachments.

He had asked Julian on Saturday, because Julian knew which office could say yes to thirty visitors without convening a committee, and Julian had sent the request back signed inside eleven minutes with a route plan, two dietary spreadsheets, and a note that said this was a good use of House access and that next time he should bring a head count first. A different hand had added one line under it. Darius was not permitted to challenge anyone. Mara had never met Darius. Kade had described him accurately.

He had asked for thirty.

"Twelve," he said.

Alvarez waited.

"Meridian's exhibition night, next month. It's the one House event with visitor seating. I asked for all of Room Four. They've allocated twelve ferry seats and one staff escort." He scrolled. "Selected by the sending school, by academic readiness, attendance, and behavioral standing."

He read the last line twice and felt his face go the way it had gone in the hall.

"No."

"No what," Alvarez said. Not opposition. Work. He knew the voice.

"If you pick on attendance and behavior, the kids who already know how to fit get the boat. That's the whole district in one line. Twelve badges for the ones who'd have been fine at Canyon Ridge, and a video for everyone else."

"Then what."

His first answer had already come and gone, and it had been the easy one. Get Julian to call somebody. Ask Sen. Spend whatever was attached to his name this week on a bigger boat. It might even work. And then the whole thing would run through him, and anything that ran through him stopped when he did, and he had learned that on a Sunday with his back against a rail.

"I don't know yet," he said. "But it's not mine to know. It's theirs. They just spent an hour proving they can solve a building. Give them this one."

Alvarez looked at him a long moment, and then at the cone she had put back, and something at the corner of her mouth agreed with him without saying so.

"I'll draft rules from whatever they decide. You'll read them before you object to them in public."

"That takes away my best setting."

"You'll survive it. Go tell them the number before Russell does and they start a government."

"Darius would be a terrible revolutionary."

"Darius has snacks and free time. Don't underestimate logistics."

---

He did not plan to stand at the front of the room, which was probably why it worked.

"There's a thing on the island next month," he said. "A House event. Exhibition night. There's seating for visitors. I asked if Northline could come."

The room came up like a pan on heat. Where. How. Would there be a boat. Would there be searches. Would Darius be allowed to challenge someone.

"No."

"You didn't let me finish."

"It's in writing. Somebody you've never met wrote it down."

Alvarez had to raise her voice to get them seated, and Kade stood at the front laughing and understood, with no warning at all, how much it was going to matter to him to have been the one who held this door.

"There are twelve places," he said. "I asked for thirty. I got twelve. That's the boat and the visitor field, not me, and I can ask for more and I will and I'm not going to promise you more than somebody's signed."

He watched it happen. The heat went out of the room and arithmetic came in. Kids counted each other without meaning to. Tessa looked at Miguel and then didn't. Darius's grin thinned as his eyes went from the front row to the back, doing the sum.

Kade felt the door turn into a ladder in his hands, and he did the one thing he had come here already knowing how to do, which was to let go of it.

"They want you picked on attendance and behavior," he said. "I said no to that. So the question's how, and I'm not the one who answers it. You are. Same as the building. You've got fifteen minutes and a pen and Alvarez writes down whatever survives."

For a second nobody moved.

Then Tessa said, "Some of them should go to people who'd actually use it. Resonance work, emergency work. Not just who wants a boat ride."

"Some of them staff pick," Darius said, "because staff know things, and I want that on the record because it makes me look reasonable."

"Staff pick favorites," said Miguel.

Alvarez drank her coffee. "A strong opening argument for your application."

"And some of them a lottery," Lacey said, "so it's not all people who talk."

"Lottery among who?" Marcus said.

"Anyone who does the safety prep," Kade said. "There's a briefing. Ferry rules, where you stand on a visitor field, what a horn means. Nobody goes who skipped that. It's a gate, but it's a gate that keeps you off a floor that moves."

"Rules go up before anybody applies," Tessa said. "No secret categories."

"Nobody picked for looking good in a picture," Darius said, with the van in his voice.

Miguel had not put his hand up. He said, to the desk, "My mom's not signing anything with the word ocean in it. So there's that."

The room went a degree quieter, and Kade was glad of it, because it was the part he would have missed.

"Then it streams here," he said. "The whole exhibition, on those." He nodded at the tablets. "They lose the building when the router sneezes. I've got a roommate who can make anything talk to anything, and the question will offend him, which is how you know it's yes. And the twelve who go don't come back with a story. They come back with a job. Stations. Arena rules, equipment, House systems, whatever they learned. They run it for everyone who stayed."

"Can my station be a combat demonstration," Darius said.

"Your station can be sitting through the safety briefing."

"My weakest discipline."

"Then train."

It came out in Julian's voice, flat and certain, and he heard it and disliked it intensely, and Tessa caught it and laughed at him out loud.

"You got so weird."

Maybe. But the room no longer looked like twelve winners and a crowd. It looked like a group of people with a constrained system in front of them and a pen, and he had watched that shape do things this afternoon that his hand on the pen could not have done.

When it had settled, Marcus put his hand up. Not high.

"Do they take people from schools like this."

It was quiet enough that the room bent around it.

Kade thought of a girl in his House with three placements behind her who read an emergency manual at dinner. He thought of a blue lanyard, and a back door with a federal seal on it, and a duffel with a strap his mother had repaired with mismatched thread.

"Yes."

Marcus did not look convinced. "How."

He nearly said he did not know, which was true.

"Different ways. Mine was the strange one. Hopefully nobody has to do it like that."

A few people laughed. Marcus almost did.

"But you don't have to have gone to the right middle school at eleven. That's the part the website leaves off."

Alvarez went and stood by Marcus's desk. "There's a regional aptitude calendar in my drawer," she said, to him and not the room. "And application waivers. Anybody who wants either can ask me without announcing it."

Marcus's shoulders came down half an inch. Kade heard the second offer inside the first, the one that said wanting something did not have to be a performance before it was ready, and he had needed that rule at fourteen and nobody had had it to give.

Marcus wrote something in the corner of his tablet, small, where it would not show unless he wanted it to. Not a future. A date. A door began that small. Kade had stood on the wrong side of enough of them to know.

Darius walked them to the curb with his hood up, which was how he did the things his face was not available for.

"Forty-eight's going to be about the ocean form," he said. "I want you to be ready."

"I'll answer it."

"Before or after."

"Before."

Darius nodded at the car, and at Elena, and at the cone against the wall, and then he did the other thing again, briefly, and pushed Kade toward the door.

"Go home," he said. "Whichever one."

---

Elena drove four blocks without saying anything. Past the grocery store where a security robot had followed him and Darius for an hour and they had argued the entire time about whether it was allowed to. Past the bus stop where she had picked him up after the second suspension and said nothing, all the way home, which had been the loudest thing she had ever done. The street that climbed toward Canyon Ridge went by on the left, and he did not look up it, and then he did.

"At the desk," she said. "You told Boyd you had to be home by the last ferry."

"I know what I said."

"You meant the island."

"I know what I meant."

She drove. The engine did the thing it did after a stop now, a shudder that came up through the seat, that he had started hearing as a line on page two of a contract.

"I used to sit in that room," Elena said, "and ask people to give you one more chance. Some of the time I was asking them. Some of the time I was asking you."

"I know."

"You didn't then."

"No. Not then." He watched the tire warehouse go by, and the storage yard, and the road out. "I thought if I said out loud that I wanted it to work, then everybody got to decide the failure was mine when it didn't."

"Some of it was."

"I know."

"Some of it wasn't." She did not say she had always known. She had not always known. Neither had he. There was room in that for both of them to stand. "The island isn't proof they were wrong about you. It isn't proof you were right, either."

"Deeply unfortunate."

"You kept asking. Your method was just louder." She touched his shoulder once at the light, which was not comfort, exactly. It was closer to a nurse marking a chart.

He sat with the word he had used at Boyd's desk. He had spent four years believing a place could only be home if it was the only one. That was why you did not go back to places. Leaving one closed it behind you, and there was nothing there to go back to, and the trick was to leave first so you never had to find that out. Northline had not closed. It had stayed exactly where it was and gotten a working metal detector and a kid named Marcus and a girl who knew what a chair needed, and it had kept his desk leaning left.

And the island was home too. Not the building. The chair at a table that nobody had to hold for him.

Two homes was twice the number of places that could hurt him when something went wrong. The old fear turned up on schedule to say so, and he looked at it the way Park had taught him to look at a headache, and gave it its number, and it was real, and it was not a reason to leave. It had never been the fear. It had been what he did with it.

His reflection sat in the window over the road out, a boy who had been told for four years that he belonged nowhere and had believed it hard enough to arrange it, and he looked at him for a while, and let him go by with the rest.

The word back, he noticed, now pointed in two directions. He was not sure he liked that. He was fairly sure it was too late.

# Chapter 24 — The Quiet War

There were four chairs in front of Director Sen's desk. Kade had been in that office five times and had never seen more than two.

"You counted us," he said.

"I read your filing. It had three attachments in three different hands, and then there was you." Sen stood at the window wall with the storm behind her. The rain was coming across the water in long grey sheets, and the horizon had gone. For once the island looked like something small with a great deal of ocean around it. "Sit down, please. You are dripping on a carpet that is older than you are."

Sera took the chair nearest the door, after she had looked at the door. Eli sat with his jacket still zipped over the tablet. Kade stayed on his feet a moment longer than the others, because sitting felt like agreeing to something.

On the wall behind the desk Sen had already put up the line from the old cache. MERCER, AARON J. STANDARD VIII. STATUS: SEALED.

"You knew him," Kade said.

"I did."

"You knew what he was."

"I knew his classification. That is a smaller thing than what you asked, and I know it."

"Did you know I'd trip the same protocol?"

"No." It came at once, with nothing arranged in front of it. "I knew where Zero Protocol began. I did not know it would wake up for you."

He believed her. After five months he knew how she folded her hands when she was choosing words, and how still she went when somebody came near a thing she had decided was dangerous. It annoyed him that he could tell her truths from her omissions.

"In October I asked you whether anyone had done what I do. You looked at me and said no."

"I said there was no verified modern program."

"You knew what I'd hear."

"I chose every word so that you would hear it." She did not look away from him. "If I had told you that I knew your father, your next question would have been how. I was not prepared to answer it. That does not improve the lie. I am telling you what it was for. I am not telling you it was right."

He had crossed the bridge with an argument ready for every defense she might make. She had not made one.

"My father asked me about Kade's floor," Mara said. "He called it a structural event. That was before any report used the phrase, and he knew the name Mercer before I said it."

Sen's face changed very slightly. "Alaric asked you himself? Directly?"

"At dinner. He told me not to look into it."

Sen went to her desk and touched two controls. The door bolt went home with a heavy sound. The display behind her dropped to a single amber light in one corner, and the small network mark on Kade's band, which was always there, went out.

"Should I be worried about how fast you did that?" Sera said.

"Yes."

"Good. Consistency helps."

"Mr. Navarro rang a bell tonight at twenty minutes to eight," Sen said. "That notice comes to my desk. I would like to tell you that it comes only to my desk, and I cannot. So we will do this once, in a room that is not connected to anything." She looked at Eli. "You found him in the calibration tables."

Eli stared. "How did you know that?"

"Because nobody ever managed to take Aaron out of the technical literature. A biography can be erased. A denominator is a good deal more stubborn."

"What were they measuring?" Kade said. "Twelve people, tested as one thing. What for?"

"You need the part we buried underneath that first."

She brought the wall up again on the room's own memory. It showed a field of stars, and in the middle of them a dark shape. It was not a ship, or not any kind that Kade knew. It was irregular, and one side of it looked torn. There was nothing beside it to give the size.

"What is that?" Eli said, very quietly.

"We called it Contact Zero."

Nobody moved. Thunder went over the building and down the far side.

Kade knew the public history of Resonance as he knew his own address. Thirty-one years ago there had been new sensors, and a cascade of results in experimental physics, and then the discovery that a few human nervous systems could couple to a field nobody had known how to see.

"The history you were taught is not false," Sen said.

"That's a great start," said Kade.

"It is incomplete in the way governments like a fact to be incomplete. A deep survey picked up a patterned emission beyond the orbit of Neptune. It was repeating, but it was not stable. The thing producing it was damaged. It was not made by human beings."

Kade had expected that sentence to feel impossible. It did not. Thirty-one years of miracles had needed to come from somewhere, and somebody had been careful not to say where.

"Nonhuman," Eli said.

"Yes. We never understood its language, if it has one. What we understood was that its emissions did things to human neural activity that no model predicted. We did not invent Resonance, Mr. Navarro. We learned to talk, badly, to a system that already knew how."

"Is it alive?" Kade said.

"I do not know how to answer that cleanly."

"Try."

"It adapts. It changed what it was doing in response to some of the people we put near it, and it ignored others entirely."

"A computer?" Eli had come forward to the edge of his chair. "Or a vehicle? Is it a beacon?"

"Maybe," Sen said, three times, in the same voice.

Eli's face was lit up like a boy's at a launch. It lasted about three seconds more. "If it was transmitting," he said, "who was it transmitting to?"

"We do not know."

"And if somebody picked up?"

"We do not know that either."

The light went out of him.

Sera asked what nobody else had thought to ask. "Who found it? Whose ship?"

"It was a multinational survey consortium."

"Military?"

"Not at first."

"That sounds like eventually."

"It was eventually. Fewer than two hundred people alive know the whole of what I am telling you. Several thousand know a piece of it and have never been told what their piece connects to."

So that was how a thing this size stayed hidden. It was built in compartments, as a ship's hull is. It explained why everybody Kade had questioned this year had seemed to be holding a different edge of the same shape.

"Where does my father come in?"

"Not at the discovery. Contact Zero is the finding itself. Aaron came afterward, as one of the program's first compatible subjects."

"Subject," Kade said.

"That is what we called him. I called him that myself, in reports with my name at the bottom."

"What did you do to him?"

Mara glanced at him. Sen did not flinch. "We did the things we believed were necessary. I know that isn't an answer. It is the beginning of an apology that I have not earned the right to make, and I will not insult you by making it tonight."

She changed the wall. Twelve outlined figures stood in a ring around a drawing of a field, each with an identifier under it. Eight-dash-twelve was at the bottom of the ring.

"These are the coupling trials," Eli said.

"They came later, when Resonance was already a science. We were trying to understand why certain subjects could steady one another. It was multi-operator Resonance, and it was very crude."

Kade thought of Vector Class, and saying to Mara, *stop treating them as two.* His face must have done something, because Mara said, "So it isn't new. What he does."

"The architecture is older than both of you," Sen said to Kade. "His trace never sat in one category. It crossed between them. At first we filed that as instrument error. Then we understood that he was not producing more field than anyone else. He was changing which interactions belonged to the system we were measuring."

"Was he born like that?"

"We studied him for years, and we do not know."

"There is one more thing from before," Sen said. "I thought about whether to show it to you. Then I thought that it was not mine to keep."

It was old lab footage, soft and streaked. Twelve people in grey coveralls stood about under a hanging instrument, waiting for something to be ready. A label floated over one of them. MERCER.

The man under the label shifted his weight. He said something to the woman next to him, tipped his head to catch her answer, and laughed.

There was no sound on it. Kade stopped hearing the room all the same.

He had two photographs of a young man who hated the camera. Neither of them moved. This one put his head a little on one side when he listened, and Kade did that. Teachers had been telling him for years that it made him look as if he was about to argue. He had never known it came from anywhere.

The clip was nine seconds long. It stopped on the laugh.

"Again," Kade said.

She ran it again. There was the shifted weight and the tipped head and the laugh, in a room nobody had told him the address of.

He did not ask a third time. Mara was looking at the rain, giving him what privacy a person can get in a room with four other people in it.

---

Sen showed them a map of research sites and government partnerships, and the first academy facilities. This island was on it under a different name, with no school on it at all.

"The academies were founded to train Resonants safely. That was true then and it is true tonight. They were also founded because governments understood, very early, that capability would have to be found and measured and spread about the world before anybody knew whether Contact Zero was the only one."

"You built schools to make soldiers," Eli said.

"We built schools to make people who could survive not knowing. That is the polished version, and I know it."

"Is there a war coming?" said Kade.

Sen looked at the dark shape in the corner of the wall. "We do not know."

He would rather she had said yes.

"Why the rankings?" Mara said. Her voice had gone flat and cold.

"Competition gives very good observation of people under stress."

There were sixty-four categories behind one public number, and a research clause for partners nobody would name. "It's a sensor," he said. "The ladder. I read the audit."

"It is a school ranking system."

"That wasn't my question."

"It is also one of several instruments that help to find unusual interaction profiles. It finds other things as well."

"My family built my whole life around that ladder," Mara said. "Nobody told me what it was for."

"I do not know what your father told you."

"That is not an answer."

"No, it isn't."

"Does winning matter?" Sera said. "I don't mean to the school. I mean to whoever's reading the sensor."

"It matters less than people assume."

"Then what does?"

Sen looked at the twelve outlines. "They want to know how people adapt, and whether they can coordinate. They want to know whether they stay themselves under pressure, and whether power leaves them more able to share control or less."

*Share control.* Kade felt the words go past him toward something he could not see yet, and let them go. He had enough to carry for one night.

"Why was he erased?" said Mara.

"Contact Zero split the people who studied it." Sen put four blank blocks on the wall and did not label them. "Some of us wanted it disclosed. Some wanted it under international control. Some wanted the whole field restricted until we knew where it came from. And some wanted weapons, before anybody else had them."

"That's the Continuity Directorate," Mara said.

Sen's head came round sharply. "That name is not public."

"It's on a routing stamp in the precursor cards. I read stamps."

"It began as an honest planning group. Its work was continuity of government, what to do if the worst came. It changed slowly. When people believe a catastrophe is certain, they become very comfortable with whatever control they take in its name." She paused. "Aaron was a member for a time, and so was I."

"You," said Kade.

"I was young, and frightened, and quite sure the secrecy was temporary."

*Small felt safe,* his mother had said that afternoon. Fear seemed to have built about half the world he lived in.

"Aaron believed the coupling work was being steered toward command. The idea was one operator with eleven instruments. He objected, loudly, and then the last trial happened." She stopped.

"Don't do that," Kade said. "You don't walk me all the way out here and then tell me the water's deep."

"I was not going to." She looked at each of the others in turn, and then at him. "The final trial produced a stable coupled event, with all twelve of us. It held for eleven point four seconds, and then it came apart. Several of us were injured, and one died some weeks later. In the middle of it Aaron did something that none of us has ever reproduced. He altered the boundary of the coupled system itself. After that the program broke. Aaron demanded that the work stop, and the Directorate did not agree."

"Show us," Kade said.

"There is no footage."

"You gave me the duration to a decimal place, so there's a record. All night I've taken your word, because there hasn't been anything else to take. Your word is what I came here about. I want to see something that doesn't depend on it."

Sen rested two fingers on the desk control. "It does not leave this room."

"Nothing seems to be allowed to leave this room," Sera said. "I haven't noticed it making the room any safer."

Sen held her eye for a moment. Then the map went away.

Twelve thin traces stood in a ring around an empty middle. Each had an identifier, and a field level, and a second, fainter line beneath it. At the bottom of the ring somebody had once labelled the twelfth trace MERCER, A., and nobody had ever taken the label off.

Sen started it.

At first they moved separately. One climbed as another fell, and two of them matched for a moment and then parted. Then all twelve began to lean inward, each at its own rate, toward the blank in the middle.

"Stop," said Mara. She was out of her chair and at the wall, her bad shoulder held a little high. "The levels are still different. That one is twice the size of that one."

"They stayed distinct through the whole of the stable interval."

"Then being coherent didn't mean being the same."

"No. We found that out afterward. It cost us a good deal."

Mara looked back at Kade. Eight people had agreed about a problem on a Trial floor without turning into eight of him. He had called that good tactics. The people in this ring had paid to find out that it was physics.

"Go on," he said.

Eli had come up beside Mara, following the timestamps with one finger. "There," he said. "Look at the edge."

A thin marker ran round the outside of the ring, the line the software drew to show what was inside the experiment. It flickered. Eleven traces spiked against it. The twelfth went through.

The display tried to cope. For one frame it drew Aaron's trace outside the ring. Then it drew him inside. Then, for a frame, it drew him as part of the marker, a length of the line that said where the system stopped. Then it gave up, and printed two words beside his name. CATEGORY INVALID.

"It lost him," Eli said. "It's got two boxes. You're an operator, or you're an input. He stopped being either, and it hasn't got a third box."

Kade was standing at the wall, though he did not remember getting up. He knew that failure. Every instrument on this island had shown him a tidier version of it: a zero where a number should be, a frozen scoreboard, a floor that broke with no path to show how.

"He changed what counted as the system," he said.

"That was our conclusion," said Sen. "The machine met the same shape again in your national exam, and fell over in the same place."

Sera had not left her chair. "Those faint lines under each one. They're medical."

"They are."

"Four of them have changed color, and we aren't at the end yet."

Kade found them. Two had gone amber and two red. The bright geometry above had hidden them until somebody who read that kind of line said what they were.

"Play the rest," Sera said. Nobody moved. "She keeps saying eleven point four seconds as if it were a score. I want to see what it cost."

Sen played the rest. More of the faint lines turned. The ring held, and held, and at eleven point four it went. The wall filled with white error text. Then the traces came back one at a time, and Kade counted them as they came. The twelfth line came back with no trace on it, only an emergency flag.

Sen shut it off before the medical lines resolved. Nobody asked her which of them had died later.

Sen had given him eleven point four seconds as a piece of history. The record gave it back to him as twelve people.

"What happened to him?" Kade said.

"The official accident did not kill him." She said it carefully and evenly. "I know that, because I saw him after it."

The room narrowed until all he could see was her.

"When was that? And where?"

"I will not tell you that tonight."

"Is he alive?"

"I will not answer that either." Her hand came up before he could speak. "I do not enjoy it. The moment certain people learn that you know what followed that trial, you stop being an unusual student and become a strategic problem."

"It's too late for that."

"Probably," Sen said quietly, and he found that more frightening than anything else she had said. "You have an index with a name beside every hole. Put this one on it tonight. The withholding authority is Amara Sen. When you come to stand in front of it, I am the person you stand in front of."

"You went to my mother's table. You told her to keep quiet."

"I told her what I believed would keep the two of you alive."

"You were wrong."

"I was." Her face tightened, and that was all. He had wanted something simple enough to hate, and she would not give him that either.

Eli was still looking at the dark shape in the corner of the wall. "What happened to the object? Where is it now?"

"I am not authorized to tell you that."

"Who authorizes you?" said Kade.

"It isn't us," said Sera.

"No. None of us in this room owns that authority."

"That's the whole problem, isn't it?"

Sen looked old for a moment, and did nothing to hide it. "You are not wrong, Ms. Vale."

Kade had one question left. "Was he right? I mean about the weapons."

"He was, entirely."

"Was he wrong about anything?"

"He was wrong about many things. He believed that if he refused to take part, he would be outside the structure." Her eyes stayed on him, and he understood that she was aiming. "He learned too late that you can leave a structure, and it will go on using the space where you stood."

Kade thought of a bay in a curved wall, with its anchors and its conduit run and a painted panel that did not quite match the stone. He could not tell whether she meant Aaron or him, and he supposed she meant both.

---

It was past midnight when they came out, and the rain had stopped. The paths shone under the lamps. Nobody said a word as far as the forum fountain, where Eli stopped walking.

"Aliens," he said.

"You held that in for twenty minutes," said Sera.

"I was being respectful." He turned right round on the wet stone, as if the island ought to look different. "There's a Conduit in my head, and it's built on work somebody did by studying an actual—" He stopped, and the rest of the night caught up with him. "And somebody died. And your dad's gone. And it might have been calling someone."

"We don't know that," said Mara.

"That sentence has had a very long shift."

Sera sat down on the rim of the fountain. "I need this to be smaller for ten minutes. I want food."

"After first contact?"

"Especially after first contact."

They walked home. Meridian stood up dark ahead of them with the Commons windows lit, and the bell was a black shape on the roofline.

In the autumn he had wanted this place to be a fraud. A fraud could be left, as he had left things at Northline, with a joke at the door. It was not a fraud. The late kitchen had put out soup and bread under a cloth. A House rule older than anyone in the building said that nobody came back from a bad night to a cold counter. The same island held sixty-four columns on how he behaved when he was frightened, and sent them to people who would not give their names. There was only the one institution. It fed him and it measured him, and it meant both. It was his home and it was a machine, and he would not get to pick one and be angry at the other.

He had stayed for the people. The reason still held. He had not been sure on the bridge that it would.

For the first five minutes nobody mentioned any of it. They argued about whether the soup wanted salt. Then Mara put her spoon down. "We can't tell anyone."

"I hate that sentence," Kade said.

"So do I. Knowing that secrecy is wrong doesn't make every disclosure safe."

For five months he had been furious with people who decided what he could handle. Now he knew a thing that could put his mother, and Room Four, and everyone asleep upstairs, inside a fight that was older than he was. He did not enjoy the resemblance.

"There's a difference," Sera said. "One is keeping somebody in the dark so that you can steer them. The other is not shouting classified first contact across a dining hall. We're going to decide what we're doing now, before one of us gets tired enough to decide it alone."

She slid the paper sleeve off her spoon and flattened it on the table. She held out a hand for Mara's pen and ruled it into three columns.

"What we know. What we think. What we're going to do."

"You've turned first contact into an incident form," Kade said.

"Panic can't remember which facts are real. Forms can."

Mara took the pen. Under KNOW she wrote in small square capitals. CONTACT ZERO IS NOT HUMAN. RESONANCE SCIENCE BEGAN WITH IT. THE LADDER ALSO MEASURES. AARON SURVIVED THE OFFICIAL ACCIDENT. SEN SAW HIM.

Kade read the last entry twice. It did not say *might have*. His mother had signed for a box of effects and no body. For some length of time after that, a man who tipped his head when he listened had been alive somewhere.

Under THINK she wrote, THE OBJECT MAY BE A BEACON. THE DIRECTORATE MAY STILL BE INSIDE ASTERION. AARON MAY BE ALIVE.

"That last one's being very hard on the word *may*," Eli said gently.

"It's the word we've got."

Kade reached for the pen. Under DO he wrote, TELL MOM WHAT SEN CONFIRMED. The letters came out too big.

"Tonight?" said Sera.

"If I wait for morning, then I've decided she can have one more night of believing something I know is false, because the timing suits me. I know what that's called."

"You can't put the rest of it on an open call," Mara said. His hand tightened on the pen. "I am not telling you what Elena can take. I am telling you that a normal call makes a record, and none of us controls where it goes."

It was a real distinction, and he disliked it anyway. "I tell her about Dad, and I tell her there's more. I tell her why I'm not saying it on that line, and that it's my decision. Then she gets to be angry about the choice I actually made."

"Write that down," said Sera, and he did.

Eli took the pen and wrote, DO NOT COPY SEN'S FILES. Then he looked at it as if it had said something about his mother.

"That hurt you," said Kade.

"It hurt a lot. But if I cope with being scared by collecting classified telemetry, I'm one of the people in her four blocks. I don't want to find out which one."

"You can be taught," said Sera.

She took the pen back and wrote, NO PROMISE OF PERMANENT SILENCE.

"Explain that one," said Mara.

"Sen gave us a locked door. I'm not building another one down here. Nobody tells the whole thing tonight. If one of us decides that somebody else needs to know, we come back to this table first and say what the risk is, out loud. But nobody gets to say *we agreed* as though that ends the argument forever."

"And if the table says no?" said Kade.

"Then it's still their choice. It's also their name on whatever happens to everyone it touches."

Mara drew a line under it.

"I'm not calling my father tonight," she said. "First I'm going to separate what he actually said to me from what she said in that room. He knew a phrase before he should have. That's all that goes in the first column." She wrote, ALARIC. FACTS FIRST.

He stood up. "I'm calling her."

"Do you want someone with you?" Mara said.

The old answer came up first. It was *no*, and it would have put up a wall in one second and left him to staff it alone all night.

"Not on the call," he said. "But stay down here."

"We will."

Sera dropped the sleeve into her empty bowl. Eli poured the last of his tea over it. The ink let go in grey threads, and Mara turned the pulp over with a spoon until the columns were gone. There was no new file for somebody else to find.

---

He called at thirteen minutes to one, from the far corner of the Commons, where the kitchen light did not reach.

Elena picked up on the first ring. She was home, on the couch, in the old grey cardigan, and she had not been asleep.

"You found something. It's your father."

She waited. He had always assumed that people who held things back enjoyed it. But he had a sentence, and once he said it her life would be different.

"Sen confirmed all of it. He was Standard Eight, and Zero came out of the same program. She was there." He took a breath. "Mom, she saw him after the accident. After the date on the certificate."

Elena's eyes closed. When they opened she was looking at something off to the side of the screen.

"How long after?"

"She wouldn't tell me."

"But he was alive when she saw him. Breathing, on his feet?"

"Yes, he was alive."

She turned away from the camera. He heard one hard breath, and then another. He said nothing. It was most of a minute before she came back, wiping at one eye with the heel of her hand as if it had annoyed her.

"I've given that news," she said. "I've given it to families the other way round. You sit them down and you tell them he's gone, and you stay while it lands. Nobody ever trained me for having it taken back." She breathed. "I signed for a box, Kade. I thought, they wouldn't hand me his things if there were any doubt. I built seventeen years on a cardboard box."

"Is he alive now?"

"She won't say. She put her own name on it as the one who's refusing."

"Do you believe her?"

"I believe everything she told me was true. I don't believe it was everything."

"Those aren't the same thing."

"No. They aren't."

"What else is there? I can see it on you."

"There's a lot more. It's bigger than Dad, and I'm not going to tell you on this call."

He waited for the anger. She looked at him for a long moment, as she would have looked at a chart. "Is that your decision, or theirs?"

"It's mine. I decided it at a table downstairs, with three people watching me do it."

"Why?"

"Because I learned enough tonight to know that I don't know who listens to this line. Some of it would make you a person worth listening to, and I'm not doing that to you over a phone."

Elena nodded slowly. "I hate that. But you answered me. Nobody at my table ever did, not one of them. I asked them why, and they said it was for my safety, as if that were an answer." She pulled the cardigan closed. "So here's what I need from you, and don't give me a clipped little yes. I don't want *when it's safe*. People have told me that for seventeen years, and it turned out to mean never. You tell me what safe is. What would have to be true?"

He had not even asked himself. He built it aloud, badly, as he built things at the board for Amadi. "It has to be in person, and it can't go through anything that carries a signal. And I don't get to leave it open. When I'm home at the break we sit at the kitchen table, and I tell you where it stands, and you decide with me whether waiting is still worth it."

"And if something changes about him, before then?"

"Then you know the same day I do. That part doesn't wait for anything."

"All right," Elena said. "I can live inside that. I can't live inside *trust me*."

"Mom. I'm sorry I'm the one who—"

"No. You didn't do this. You told me the truth within the hour, and some very senior people never managed that in seventeen years." Her voice gave slightly and she took it back. "I'm going to sit here for a while and be angry at a man who may or may not be dead. I don't know how to chart that. I love you."

"Love you too."

The screen went dark. Across the long room three people were being quiet over cold tea and not watching him. He was alone in the corner, but it was not the old way of being alone.

---

In the morning the island was the same as it had been, and he found that faintly insulting.

The breakfast line went out through the door. Bell Watch had started a furious argument over whether the days since the Trial win ought to be counted from zero or from one. Thirty-one years ago somebody had found a broken machine out beyond Neptune that human beings had not built, and Kade had Vector Analysis at nine.

He stopped dead in the doorway. Amadi had the old calibration matrix up on the board, all twelve columns of it. The girl behind him walked into his shoulder and told him to move or become furniture.

It was the same set from his first month. The twelfth column sat at the right-hand edge of the board. It had no name and no face, but it had its numbers. Class after class of first-years had learned from that data. Nobody had needed to destroy a thing. They had only needed to separate the useful part of a man from the man who had paid for it.

Amadi came in on the stroke of nine and set a paper cup on the lectern. "You all did well enough at Midyear to become overconfident. Today we repair that. The published solution treats each column as an independent source. Tell me why that's wrong. Mercer."

Her voice was perfectly ordinary. Either she had no idea what she was standing in front of, or she knew and had better practice than Sen. The second idea came by itself, and he hated it.

"If you treat them as independent, each transfer gets assigned to the nearest operator," he said. "But the loads move when the boundary of the system moves. You have to conserve the shared state first, and then work out who contributed what."

"Better. Write it."

Tablets came out. Kade put his hand up, and Amadi's eyebrows rose. He did not usually ask leave before making her class more difficult.

"Why twelve?"

"We've covered this. The teaching set preserves a legacy schema."

"That's why the table has twelve headings. I'm asking why the data has twelve sources. Those are measurements. Somebody was standing there."

Amadi picked up her tablet and went into the instructional record. No door locked and nothing flashed. Her frown got deeper as she scrolled.

"The attribution field is sealed," she said.

"Has it always been?"

"It has been for as long as I've taught the set."

"And that never bothered you?"

It came out sharper than a classroom deserved. He knew before she answered that he had done the easy thing. He had found the nearest person who had repeated a clean story in good faith, and sent her the bill.

Amadi put the tablet down. "It bothers me now." She did not defend herself, and he thought of Halden with his hands coming slowly from behind his back. "You will note that I don't know who they were. Neither do you, officially, so don't sit there looking like the sole proprietor of the question. It belongs to the room now."

She turned to the class. "New assignment. For next session, audit every step of the published solution that depends on these sources being interchangeable. We have kept a set of people's measurements and thrown away the people. We should at least know which of our conclusions that can no longer support." There was a groan, the ordinary kind, about homework and not the end of the world. "Mercer, you'll take the attribution problem. You found it, so you can carry it."

"I gave myself homework."

"Consequences drive learning."

He bent over column twelve and started. He could not tell fifty people what it was. But he could refuse to let it sit there as furniture, and he could do that without setting up as the one person entitled to explain it.

At lunch he had the audit open beside his tray. Across the top of it he had written Halden's question, because it was the right one. WHAT DID IT COST TO MAKE IT SHORT, AND WHO WAS CHARGED?

"You look like a man who learned something unpleasant."

Taren put his tray down and sat. Kade's hand turned the tablet face down on the table. It did it before he had decided anything.

"What makes you say that?"

"You went into the Director's office with three people, and you came out after midnight."

"Did you get that off a House schedule?"

"No," said Taren easily. "I saw you."

It might even have been true. Half the island could see that bridge. Kade put it on the pile.

"Two hundred and eleven," Taren said, and lifted his cup. "Congratulations, again. I'm consistent. It's my best quality." He began on his lunch, and talked about the fish.

Kade had kept a closed face around people all his life, to keep them out. This was the first time he had kept anything back from a friend out of caution and not fear, and he did not like how it felt. Taren's eyes went once to the back of the tablet and came away again. He asked nothing. He went on asking nothing, pleasantly, for the whole meal.

Kade sat in a bright hall full of the noise of people who were his people, and all of it was being counted by someone he had never met. It had been going on for thirty-one years, a quiet war over what human beings ought to become, and over what could be done to them in the meantime. His father had gone into it somewhere and had not come out.

Kade no longer knew what finding him would mean. It might be a grave. It might be a locked room. It might be something that Sen was still more frightened of than either. He turned the tablet over again, with Taren watching, and went on with column twelve.

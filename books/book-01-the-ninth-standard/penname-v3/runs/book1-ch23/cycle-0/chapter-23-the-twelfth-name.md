# Chapter 23 — The Twelfth Name

Resonance Institutions and Public History met in the only lecture hall on the island with no field dampers in the walls. Nothing more dangerous than a question had ever been thrown in it.

It was two days after the Open. Kade's forearm had gone from plum to a yellowish green that Eli called encouraging, and a hundred first-years sat in tiered rows pretending they were not checking the recalculated ladder under their desks. Two rows down, a boy had Kade's line up on his tablet and was showing it to his neighbor. Two hundred and eleven. The neighbor turned, found Kade looking back, and became very interested in the front of the room.

On the front wall were eleven faces.

Everybody knew them. They were in airports and on stamps. Professor Halden had put them up in the official arrangement, in three rows, each with a name underneath. Amara Sen was in the second row, twenty-six years old, regarding the camera as if it owed her a citation.

"The Eleven were symbols before anybody outside a laboratory could have told you what Standard Eight meant," Halden said. He was a narrow man who lectured with his hands in his cardigan pockets, as though he did not trust them near the evidence. "Remember that order of events. An institution will settle around a story long before it settles around the science. Within five years these names were on schools, treaties, and a disaster-response doctrine. Within ten, the phrase had stopped needing a noun. The Eleven what? Nobody asks."

A hand went up near the front. "Were they actually the first?"

"They were the first publicly verified Standard Eights," Halden said pleasantly, and moved on.

Kade did not move on. Four months at Asterion had taught him that when a careful person chose a careful word, something was usually standing behind it. He wrote PUBLICLY in the margin of his notes. He had started keeping a list of careful words, the way Sera kept a list of doors.

Halden cleared the faces, and three things replaced them.

The first was a photograph. Chen Wei stood on the broken east parapet of the Lanzhou Dam with one arm out into the rain. Under it ran the sentence from the bronze plaque at the site. Chen Wei held the failing spillway for nineteen minutes and saved the valley below.

The second was an operations log, four pages of it, in narrow columns. There were gate technicians and municipal engineers. There was a dispatcher who had kept two roads open after the main network went down, and six village crews who had cleared the auxiliary channels by hand. Chen appeared halfway down the fourth page, beside a field-output estimate and a medical override he had ignored.

The third was a paragraph from a schoolbook. It was forty-one words long, and Kade had read it at the age of nine.

"Tables of four," Halden said. "You will write the text for a public memorial. Seventy-five words, twelve minutes. Every fact in it must survive contact with the record. When you finish, you will read it aloud, once, to the table beside you. A minute later they will tell you what they remember. That is the only grade."

Kade got a Sol girl with color-coded tabs, a Kepler boy who began typing names out of the log before Halden had stopped talking, and a fourth student who said nothing and opened the map.

"That's forty names," Kade said to the Kepler boy. "You're past the limit before you reach a verb."

"People deserve credit."

"Agreed. They deserve a sentence as well."

The Sol girl highlighted the plaque. "We keep Chen. He's why anybody visits."

"The channels are why there's still a valley to visit."

"Nobody is putting six drainage crews on a memorial, Mercer."

"Then it's a poster."

She gave him the look of someone confirming a rumor. "The field load on that spillway would have killed anybody else alive. It should have killed him. You want to cut that?"

"I'm not cutting Chen. You decided I was before I'd said anything." He pulled the plaque sentence across. "I'm objecting to this. He held. He saved. There are two verbs in it, and he has both. Four pages of people and nobody else gets to do anything."

That stopped her. She did not agree, but her stylus hovered.

The fourth student turned the map to face them. The dam crossed the river in a shallow gray arc. Red marked the parapet failure and two jammed gates. Blue marked the auxiliary channels, and yellow marked the roads running down the valley. She put her finger on a line near the bottom of the log.

"South dispatch rerouted the hospital convoy six minutes before Chen reached the parapet. Nobody had confirmed the failure yet. She just did it."

"That should go in," the Sol girl said, and looked annoyed to have said it.

"Seventy-five words," Kade said.

"I can count."

They wrote it three times. The first version was built around Chen, came in at sixty-nine words, and made the gate crews sound like equipment he had brought along. The second was built around the evacuation and made nineteen minutes on a breaking wall sound like a scheduling detail, which was also a lie. The third went in strict order of events, and nobody at the table could repeat it thirty seconds after they closed the document.

Four minutes were left. Around them the hall was arguing, and Halden was declining to answer any question containing the words *supposed to*.

Kade went back to the map. It was a system, and he knew what to ask a system.

"What changed?"

"The dam broke," the Sol girl said.

"It was already breaking. What changed between everybody dying and everybody not?"

The fourth student traced it with her finger. "Dispatch moved the low ground first. The gate crews took pressure off. The villages opened the channels so the water had somewhere to go. Chen held the spillway until the level dropped below the crack."

"So he bought the time," Kade said, "and they spent it."

They wrote that. It named Chen, the dispatcher, the gate crews, and the villages, and it called the nineteen minutes what they were, time bought for other people's work. It ran seventy-eight words. Kade deleted *historic*, *courageous*, and *unprecedented*, and lost nothing.

"I hate that those were free," the Sol girl said.

Then they read. The next table went first, and their text was all Chen, in clean heroic order. A minute later Kade's table gave it back nearly word for word.

The Sol girl read theirs. She read it well. Halden watched the clock on the wall for one minute and then nodded at the other table.

"Chen held the dam," a boy said. "For nineteen minutes. And there were crews." He frowned. "Some kind of crews."

"And?"

"Somebody drove a convoy?"

The dispatcher had lasted sixty seconds. Kade looked at her line in the log. She had moved a hospital on her own judgment with nobody's permission, and a room full of people who had just been told about her could not keep her for a minute.

"The plaque is not false," Halden said. He left both texts on the wall. "Chen Wei held that spillway for nineteen minutes, and every medical model we own says it should have killed him. The log is not false either. If the gate crews walk off, he buys four minutes and not nineteen. If the dispatcher waits for confirmation, the convoy is in the floodplain when the berm goes. There is no version without an edge. The space on the wall is an edge, and so are the visitor's attention, and classification law, and grief, and what the survivors will permit. Public history is the business of deciding what gets across."

"So the short version always wins," the Sol girl said.

"The short version is necessary. Memory is compression, and compression is not the failure. The failure is when the short version changes who acted, who paid, or what made the thing possible."

The boy near the front had his hand up again. "What if the long version is sealed? How would we know what was cut?"

"Sometimes you will not. For a while." Halden put the eleven faces back. "But records leave shapes. A citation that points at nothing. A budget that does not add. A room built for a purpose nobody will name. A careful historian does not invent something to fill a gap. She also does not call the gap empty."

Kade wrote that down. Under PUBLICLY he added a second line. WHO GOT THE VERBS?

Halden's last slide was the Hall of Standards, photographed from the west door. It showed eleven bronzes along the curved wall. Kade did not have to count the bays behind them, because he had done that months ago, on his way to break a floor. There was a twelfth, past the last plate, with a painted panel in it. The photograph ended a hand's width short of it. Somebody had chosen where the picture stopped.

The bell went. "Next week," Halden called over the noise, "why the Standard system has survived, although every serious physicist alive dislikes it."

Kade stopped at the door. "That sounds promising."

"Mercer, you are a walking argument for the syllabus."

"That feels personal."

"It's historical."

He left before he could be caught enjoying it.

---

"There are eleven," Eli said.

It was past nine, and Meridian Commons had thinned to the people who ate late on purpose. Kade had spent the hour before dinner on the good tape with Mara, learning which parts of his face announced a sore arm. Mara sat across from him with a bowl of rice and the Julian footage. At the next table Sera was working through a Response Corps certification module with her jaw set. Taren had gone off after dinner to fix somebody's scheduling problem. He had said it as though it explained itself, and with Taren it generally did.

"Eleven what?"

"Public Standard Eights."

"That's famously true. I sat through a lecture on it."

"It's famously incomplete." Eli turned his tablet round and pushed it between the plates. "I was tidying your father's identifiers, the ones from the archive cards. I wanted to know what else used that number format, so I went looking in the old Authority registry. The open one. Anybody with a student login can read it. Nobody does, because it's a list of numbers."

The screen showed a column of identifier blocks, each with a name beside it. Kade knew the names. He had looked at them that morning, on a wall, in three rows.

"Look at the numbers, not the names," Eli said.

Mara had already put her spoon down. "There are twelve slots."

"Thank you."

Kade counted anyway. There were eleven names and twelve sequential identifiers, and one line in the run had a number and nothing beside it.

"That could be administrative," he said.

He heard how fast it came out. Mara heard it as well, and glanced up at him, and had the grace not to say anything.

"Not in this system," Eli said. "These are permanent. They're assigned once and never reused. You can die, retire, be disgraced, be classified, and the slot stays yours. It's the one thing the Authority got right. There isn't a mechanism for an empty one."

Sera had turned her chair. "So somebody was removed."

"Hidden," Mara said. "Removing them would have closed the gap."

Kade found he had stopped eating. He could see where this went. He had seen it the moment Mara said twelve. A column in Amadi's table that nobody would name. A screw with no medallion on his own House roof. A bay cut for a bronze that was never hung. He had stacked all three in the same place in his head and carefully never looked at the stack.

The strange thing was that he did not want to look. All his life a closed door had been an insult. This one was close enough to touch, and some cowardly part of him wanted Eli to turn the tablet back round.

His father was already a Zero. He was already a name on a precursor roster, and a movement order twenty-three days too early, and a lie Sen had told to Kade's face. There ought to have been a limit on how much a dead man could go on changing.

"It shows up in the old calibration tables," Eli said, more quietly. "No name, only the number. Twelve subject columns. Eleven map to the public identifiers and one doesn't map to anything."

"Why would they leave it in?"

"Because you can take a person out of a biography. If you take a column out of a working table, the table stops working."

"Cross it against Aaron's subject number," Mara said.

"I did that before dinner." Eli did not touch the screen. He had his hand flat on the table beside it, and he was looking at Kade and not at the result. "It's one tap. I haven't opened it. Do you want me to stop?"

Eli could have opened it an hour ago out of plain curiosity. Mara could have said that evidence did not wait on comfort. Nobody said anything. The question sat in front of him, and it was his.

For seventeen years his father had been a finished story. It had been coming unfinished for four months, a week at a time. Kade thought of burned rice, and a tune that was a draft.

"No," he said. "Open it."

Eli tapped. The screen showed a metadata conflict and hung on it. Then an old cache resolved underneath.

MERCER, AARON J. HISTORICAL CLASSIFICATION: STANDARD VIII. STATUS: SEALED.

"Standard Eight," Kade said aloud, because somebody had to.

Nothing in the room changed. A blender ran behind the service counter. Somebody laughed on the stairs. Anton dropped a tray in the kitchen and apologized to it. The House went on with its evening while his father became one of the dozen most capable people in the history of the field.

Then he was at the window without having decided to stand up. The island lay below in clean lines of light, the training halls and the bridges and the Spire, a school that had been something else first. He tried to put a twelfth bronze on that curved wall and could not do it. He had no older face to put there. He had two photographs of a young man who hated being photographed, and his mother said the eyebrows were Kade's. That face belonged in one front room in one small apartment. Three meters of bronze was the wrong size for it, and the wrongness was what hurt.

Sera had come to stand a few paces behind him. She had left him room, and she stayed.

"Sen knew," he said to the glass. "She's on that wall. She'd have had to."

"Yes," Mara said from the table, and did not soften it. He was grateful.

He wanted to be angry, because anger was clean and he knew how it went. Something else kept getting in the way, and it took him a moment to place it as embarrassment. It was not on his own account. Eleven was a number everybody knew without remembering having learned it. He wondered how many people it took to make a false number into common knowledge. Probably far fewer than he would have liked.

He came back and sat down. "All right. What else?"

"Before anything else," Mara said, "prove the match."

"It has his name on it."

"I can read." She pulled Eli's tablet a little toward her. "It's a cache. I want to know it's authentic, and that the subject number wasn't reassigned, and that a later migration didn't renumber the sequence. You filed five forms for Hale last week. What's the condition that proves this wrong?"

He almost snapped at her. Then he saw what she was doing, and it was not doubt. He needed this to be true badly enough that he would have taken it on a sticky note, and she was standing between the evidence and that need.

"A second record that disagrees," he said slowly. "Or any sign that the number had another owner."

"Good. Eli."

Eli was already in the provenance. "There are three archive migrations. Two cryptographic signatures, in a standard nobody has used in twenty years. And a checksum that was printed. On paper, in a bound technical index. Whoever built this expected the computers to die before the bureaucracy did."

"Could it be forged?" Sera asked.

"Anything can be forged."

"That isn't reassuring."

"You'd have to rewrite three independent archives, and one of them went to the Authority before this academy existed. It's possible. It would also be a lot more work than hiding one man."

"Good," Mara said.

Kade stared at her. "*Good?* That's your reaction?"

"No." Her voice changed, only a little. "My reaction is that my father knew your family's name before I ever said it to him. The Director lied to your face. And we are looking at proof that the history I grew up reciting was built around a hole. I would like not to contaminate that with sloppy verification."

"Right," Kade said. "Sorry."

Eli found the last piece himself. It was a surviving public paper from the precursor years, and its caption listed eleven participant traces. The graph underneath had twelve colors. The twelfth was labeled CONTROL ARTIFACT.

"That isn't an artifact," Eli said.

"How can you tell?"

"Watch it." He ran a finger along the time axis. "When the others destabilize, it climbs. When they settle, it comes down. It's responding to them. Noise doesn't care what the other lines are doing." He sat back. "That's a person. Somebody changed the label and left him in the data, because without him the graph doesn't balance."

Kade felt something in his chest settle. It was certainty. He had expected that certainty would feel better than it did.

"No second owner," he said. "No disagreement."

"Now we know," Mara said.

Eli opened the identifier's history, and it was short. For six years the twelfth slot appeared in restricted technical publications under a generic subject code. Then the references stopped, all in the same quarter. The first public list of Standard Eights came out two years later. Aaron was never on any draft of it.

"So they didn't edit him out afterward," Mara said. "The Eleven were eleven from the first printing."

"Why?" said Kade.

Nobody answered.

"Whatever we guess tonight will be worse than what we can prove next week," Sera said.

"That's extremely inconvenient."

"I practice."

Mara pulled the calibration table across. "What were the twelve columns measuring?"

Eli enlarged the headings. COUPLED STABILITY. MULTI-SUBJECT TRANSFER. COHERENCE DURATION.

The back of Kade's neck went cold, and for once it was only his skin. *Coupled* was the word from Vector Class, from the night he had told Mara to stop treating two operators on one beam as two. It turned up whenever somebody near him tried to describe things that would not stay separate.

"Multi-subject," he said. "These weren't twelve people being tested. It was twelve people being tested as one thing."

"Can you open the study under it?" Sera asked.

"No," said Eli, and tried.

ACCESS DENIED. Under that appeared a second line, which none of them had ever seen on a student terminal. QUERY LOGGED. OVERSIGHT NOTIFICATION GENERATED.

Eli closed the session and put the tablet face down. "That seems bad."

"You think?"

"I prefer optimism. It was a permitted request. I asked, it said no, and I stopped. I didn't break anything."

"You rang a bell," Mara said. She was standing. "Save everything we opened, with the checksums. All of it was public. Then we go to Sen, before whoever heard that bell comes to us."

"*Now* we go to Sen. She lied about knowing him. She knew what he was."

"Probably."

"I'm finished with probably." It came out sharper than he had meant, and the next table glanced over. He breathed. "Sorry."

"You're allowed to be angry."

"I'm tired of people telling me that."

"Then stop making it relevant."

It very nearly got a laugh out of him.

Sera had shut her module. "I'm coming."

"Why?"

"If an oversight team turns up, I want one person in the room whose first instinct is the exit and not the argument."

Eli raised a hand. "Mine is usually technical curiosity."

"Yes. That's why it isn't you."

Mara had one more thing open. It was a photograph she had taken months ago in the Hall, on her way back from the archive, of eleven bronzes and the painted panel at the end. Eli laid the precursor building plan over it, from the public facilities record. The curved wall was older than any of the portraits. It had twelve bays, and twelve sets of mounting anchors, drawn by the same hand on the same day.

It was no longer only a number. There was a place in a wall, the right size for a face.

"I want to see it," Kade said.

"Sen first," said Sera.

"Five minutes. I've walked past it for four months."

Mara looked at him, and then at Sera. "It's on the way."

Sera sighed. "Four."

---

The Hall of Standards ran along the eastern base of the Spire, and at night almost nobody was in it. The bronzes were lit from below. Chen Wei had rain cast into his hair. Nadia Okonkwo wore the collar ring of an orbital suit. Sen was fifth from the left. They were the official beginning of the world he lived in, and he had known their faces before he knew what Resonance was.

They walked the curve to the end. Legacy bay twelve was cut stone with a flat panel in it, painted to match and not quite matching, and a tag that said ACCESS SEALED.

Eli held his tablet to the panel's edge. "It has a different installation date from the rest."

"How can you possibly know that?" said Sera.

"Maintenance tag. It's broadcasting."

"You scanned a memorial."

"It scanned me."

"An installation date proves a renovation," Mara said, and crouched by the base molding.

Eli crouched beside her. "It has a device address, too. It's in the public maintenance register, along with the portraits. I once told you this was utilities. I'd like that struck from the record." He turned the screen. Eleven addresses reported illumination, climate, and proximity sensors, all normal. A twelfth sat at the end of the run. It was labeled ARCHITECTURAL FILL, and its power draw was zero.

"The stone expects power," Kade said.

"Yes."

"Can you wake it?"

"Probably."

"No," Sera said.

Eli's hand came down before it had reached the control.

"We've already tripped one notification," Kade said.

"Exactly. We're not tripping a second by energizing unknown wiring in a wall full of famous people."

"They're bronzes."

"Then the paperwork will be even more embarrassing."

He did want it. He looked at the panel, and at the wanting. It was what he had felt with his back to the east post, when a thing was available and ten thousand people were stamping for him to use it. He took his hand off the wall.

"We don't need it live," Mara said. She had the plan lined up against the wall in front of her. "Each bay has four load anchors and one conduit run for the environmental controls. This one has the anchors, and the conduit too. They didn't pull it. It stops behind the panel."

Eli had found the renovation ledger. "Eleven portrait assemblies transferred to the new mounts. One assembly removed, sealed disposition."

"Whose?"

"It doesn't say."

"Date?"

Eli read it out. It was two years before the first public list, the same two years as the gap in the technical record. Two different archives disagreed with the public story in the same place, and they matched each other.

Mara saved the ledger page and its checksum, and closed the register. "Now it's more than odd symmetry."

"It proves somebody took a portrait down," Sera said. "It doesn't prove whose."

"Not by itself," Kade said. The evidence was on his side, and it did not have to be everything he wanted in order to be that. He put his palm flat on the panel. It was cold, and there was no seam and no scar, nothing to say that anyone had ever been there. That would have been the point.

Voices came from the west door. It was a visiting-school group in matching jackets, late off the last ferry, with a guide in an Asterion blazer who was already in the middle of the speech.

"The Eleven represent humanity's first confirmed Standard Eight generation."

*First confirmed.* That was another phrase with room inside it for something to hide. He added it to the list.

A small girl near the front had her head tipped all the way back. "Were there only eleven?"

"Yes," said the guide, warmly, and without any pause at all.

Kade had his mouth open.

She believed it. That was what closed it again. She had been handed the short version by somebody who had been handed it, and she was passing it on in good faith to a child who would repeat it in a classroom. He saw then how large the erasure really was. Nobody needed a million liars. A few people had taken out the evidence early enough, and everybody after them had told the clean version honestly.

He could say it. *There were twelve.* It would be true. It would feel wonderful for about four seconds. After that he would be a boy shouting in a hall, holding one cached record, with no idea why his father had been cut out or whom the cutting had been protecting. He would be handing a nine-year-old a short version of his own, with himself holding every verb in it.

He shut his mouth. It felt unpleasantly adult. Sera had watched him decide, and she did not congratulate him. He was grateful for that.

The tour moved on up the curve. He stepped back from the panel. "Okay. Sen."

He got as far as the east door and stopped again. Mara waited.

"If this gets out, he stops being mine."

He had not known he was going to say it. Mara's face changed. "What do you mean?"

"Right now Aaron Mercer is my dad. He's my mother's husband. He couldn't cook rice, and he hummed without a tune, and apparently he thought notation was showing off." He tipped his head toward the bronzes. "If this goes public, he's the Twelfth. Governments will explain him. People will make documentaries and decide what he meant. I know almost nothing about him, so everybody gets the public version before I've got the real one."

Eli had lowered the tablet. For once he had no joke.

Kade had spent years resenting how small his father's story was. It had not occurred to him that a huge one could be a theft too.

"You don't have to decide tonight who gets the truth," Sera said.

"Sen decided. For seventeen years."

"Yes. That's different."

"It is."

"Truth and timing aren't the same thing," Mara said.

He nearly objected. It was a sentence that could excuse every lie Sen had ever told him. It could also be correct, and he had just proved that himself, in front of a child in a matching jacket.

"What do you want?" Mara asked.

He looked back down the curve at the panel. "I want to know who he was before somebody tells me what he represents."

"That seems reasonable."

"Careful."

"I said reasonable. I didn't say easy."

---

They took the pedestrian bridge and not the transit line. A fine rain had come in off the water, and it ticked on the canopy. Sera walked a step ahead. Kade had Mara on one side and Eli on the other, and for half the span nobody spoke.

"I want to ask my mother," he said.

"About the classification?" said Mara. "She told you she never knew it."

"She did."

"Do you believe her?"

"Yes." He did not have to think about it. He was not finished being angry with Elena, but he knew by now that her fear was a different thing from Sen's management. He called.

She answered from the kitchen at home, with her hair down and a mug in her hand. "Kade? It's late."

"Did Dad ever tell you he was Standard Eight?"

Her face went still. "No."

"Did anyone?"

"No."

He turned his band so the camera took in Eli's tablet and the cached record. He watched her read it. Her hand came up to her mouth.

"Oh," she said.

She said it the way she said it after a bad call at work. Nobody could have faked it.

"Mom?"

"He told me they couldn't classify him. That the Standards didn't fit what he did. He thought it was funny at first. Then he stopped thinking it was funny, and then he stopped talking about the work at all."

Kade looked at Mara. The system had not fit, and somebody had filed Aaron under Eight regardless. An institution had needed a box, and it had used the nearest one.

Eli brought up the calibration table. Elena leaned toward her screen. "Twelve columns."

"Do you know who they were?"

"No. But that number." She shook her head slowly. "He said once that there were twelve people in the room when everything changed. Those words."

Kade had stopped walking, and the others stopped with him. "What changed?"

"I don't know. He never said. It was before you were born. He'd been having nightmares, and I asked, and that was all I got."

Rain hissed on the canopy. *Multi-subject transfer. Coherence duration.* There had been twelve people in a room. Something had changed, and his father had stopped talking, and then he had stopped being anywhere at all.

"Kade. Don't go any deeper into this without Sen."

"That's where we're walking."

She looked relieved and unhappy at once. "Call me after."

"I will." He nearly ended it there, and found he could not. "Mom. If he was that important." It would not go, and he tried again. "Did you never wonder why the accident barely made the news?"

"Every day. For a while."

"And?"

"I was twenty-four, and you were a baby. People with government credentials sat at my table and told me that attention would make things worse."

"So they erased him, and you let them."

It landed harder than he had thrown it. She flinched. He shut his eyes. "I'm sorry."

"No. Don't be." When he looked, she only seemed tired. "You're not wrong to ask me that."

"That isn't the same as being right."

"No, it isn't." She took a breath. "I let the story get smaller because I was afraid of what would happen if it got larger. That's the whole of it. I'm not proud of it. I would probably do it again, with you in the next room."

Kade thought of a boy at Northline, making everything small before it could matter. He had spent all evening hunting for a family resemblance, and he did not enjoy finding it there.

"I'll call you after Sen."

"Please."

Sera glanced back as the band went dark. "Still planning to argue your way in?"

"Yes."

"Good. You sound normal."

He did not get the chance. His tablet chimed before they were off the bridge, with a priority header.

DIRECTOR SEN. COME TO MY OFFICE. BRING NAVARRO AND VEY.

It did not say why, and it did not mention Sera. The message had arrived a little under forty minutes after the bell. He held it out so that they could all read it.

"I'm still coming," said Sera. Nobody argued.

Taren came out of a side corridor thirty meters ahead, shaking rain off his jacket. He saw the four of them and slowed down. "Everything all right?"

Kade nearly kept walking. But Taren's eyes had gone, once, to the tablet in Kade's hand, with the priority stripe still lit across the top of it. He was too far away to read it. Probably.

"Classified family archaeology," Kade said.

"Sounds healthy."

"You have no idea."

"Usually true." Taren's band buzzed against his wrist, and he glanced down. For about as long as a blink the easy face was gone, and what was under it looked like someone doing a sum he did not like. Then the face came back. "You're all headed to Sen, then?"

Nobody had said that. Kade stopped. Taren stopped as well, with the smile still on.

"How did you know?"

Taren nodded past them at the administration building. "Where else would four people walk in the rain looking as though somebody had classified breakfast?"

It was a good answer, and it had come fast. Kade held his eye a second longer than was friendly, and put this one on the pile with the rest. The pile no longer fit where he kept it.

"Should I be worried?" Taren said.

"Probably."

Kade walked on. Mara looked back once, and he saw her do it. Neither of them said anything.

He stopped at the glass doors of the administration building with his hand on the bar.

"What?" said Mara.

He had a name in a sealed record whose provenance had held. He had a twelfth line in a graph that moved with the other eleven, and a bay with sound anchors and a dead conduit, dated to the same two years. His mother had been given the number twelve before he was born, by a man waking out of a nightmare. None of the three had known about the others. And a woman in a blazer had told a child *yes* with a clear conscience.

"I've spent four months climbing this place's ladder," he said.

Mara waited.

He looked up through the wet glass at the Spire. "It turns out the most important rank in my family is the one they deleted."

In a concrete stairwell Sen had once given him an index, a name against every hole, and he had promised to stand in front of each one. It had not crossed his mind that one of the holes would be shaped like his father.

He was not going to ask her whether she had known Aaron Mercer. He had three sources for that. He was going to ask who had decided the world would count to eleven. He was going to ask what twelve people had done in a room that was worth the subtraction.

He pulled the door open and held it for the others.

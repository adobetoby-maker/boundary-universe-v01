# Chapter 2 — Zero Protocol

By three o'clock, four different adults had told Kade Mercer that he was not being detained, and every one of them had said it from somewhere between him and a door.

He was keeping count because counting was what he did when a room stopped making sense. Vice Principal Russell's office had twenty-two floor tiles between the filing cabinet and the door, one window with two slats missing from the blind, one exit, and one phone that was not currently his, because it was under Russell's hand. Darius had folded himself into the second chair with his knees somewhere near his ears. Ms. Alvarez stood against the wall with her arms crossed, staying somewhere she had not been invited.

Through the gap Kade could see the lot. Two black government vehicles sat in the spaces marked for district maintenance. A third had pulled in twenty minutes ago with no markings at all, not even a plate frame.

Darius tilted his head at it. "That one's the secret one."

"It's the same car."

"No logo. Costs more than my building."

"Then be embarrassed with heated seats."

Russell stopped walking. He had been at it since one o'clock, six steps to the door, turn, six steps to the cabinet; Kade had timed the circuit twice. "Could the two of you try, for one minute, to understand that this is serious?"

Darius looked at Kade. Kade looked at Darius.

"No," they said together.

Russell shut his eyes. Ms. Alvarez's mouth did something at one corner and then stopped doing it.

The jokes were for Darius. Kade kept a private version of the afternoon, and it went like this: at some point before one o'clock he had dragged a line through a steel ball because a test told him to, and not long after, every testing station in the country had gone dark. Nobody had said those two things were connected. Nobody had needed to. The proctor had stepped away from him like he was a stove left on. When the national network came back nineteen minutes later, everyone in Multipurpose Two had gotten their results, all ten of them, and Kade's page had turned into a gray rectangle with four words in it. Result withheld. Authorized review.

Zero had been funny for about eight seconds. Authorized review sounded like a bill.

He knew the shape of these rooms. Somebody came in with a folder, somebody explained what he had done in language that made it sound worse, and he went home with a new adjective. The only unfamiliar part today was the scale. He had never broken anything the whole country could see.

His phone buzzed under Russell's hand. Kade reached for it. The hand did not move.

"It's mine."

"You have had forty-seven messages in twenty minutes."

The phone buzzed. "Forty-eight."

"Which is my point exactly."

Kade reached again, and Russell slid it six inches farther off. Russell had never in his life been physically quick. Today he was.

"Mr. Mercer. Until district legal tells me otherwise, you do not post, you do not answer questions, and you do not talk to anybody who says they are from a news organization."

"Does my mother count as a news organization?"

Russell went still. Ms. Alvarez uncrossed her arms.

That was worse than the cars. "What."

"We have been trying to reach her," Russell said.

"She's at work."

"We know."

"You called the hospital."

"Yes."

Kade was on his feet without having decided to stand. Nobody moved toward him. Nobody had to. The humor went out of the room like air out of a tire, and Darius sat up straight.

"You called her floor. She's emergency, Russell. You don't call the emergency department for a nurse unless somebody is bleeding."

"We needed a parent or guardian on site."

"You could have texted me. I would have texted her."

"We did text you."

Kade looked at his phone, face down under Russell's palm, and sat back down.

"Fine."

Russell slid it across. There were three messages from Elena Mercer. The first said call me, in capitals. The second was his name and the word now. The third had come two minutes after: she was leaving work, and if this was because he had punched someone, she was going to put whoever it was back together herself and then start on him.

He typed: no punching. Weird test thing. Sorry.

Three dots came up at once. That sentence did not improve my afternoon.

He put the phone in his pocket before Russell could think about it.

Russell resumed his circuit. Kade let him do two laps and then said, "Did the district tell you what happened?"

"No."

"The testing service?"

"No."

"Anybody?"

Russell looked at Ms. Alvarez, and she looked back, and something was agreed between them that Kade could not read. Russell sighed.

"At twelve forty-eight, the district received an instruction to secure the testing room, preserve all hardware, and keep you on campus pending an authorized review."

"Secure me, or the hardware?"

"The wording was not specific."

"That's reassuring."

"Then the superintendent called. Then district counsel. Then a federal office whose name I had never heard before today."

Kade filed the timeline. Ten minutes after the screens went white, someone in a building he would never see had already known his name and written instructions about him. That was not how discipline worked. Discipline was slow. It needed a meeting and a form, and it never arrived in a car with no plate frame.

The office door opened.

The man who came through it was not Kade's idea of a government secret. Fifty or so, wide through the shoulders, silver starting at the temples, a face that looked less dangerous than short on sleep. Dark suit, no tie. A narrow hard case under one arm. Behind him came a woman in a charcoal uniform with nothing sewn on it, no patch, no bar, no name. Thirty-five, maybe. Dark hair pulled back tight. Her eyes went around the room once, corners first, the way Kade's did, and stopped on him.

"Vice Principal Russell?"

"Yes."

"David Renn, International Resonance Authority. This is Lieutenant Commander Nia Vale, Federal Resonance Security."

Darius, under his breath: "Called it."

"She said her rank out loud."

Vale looked at them both, and Darius sat up straight.

Renn set the case on Russell's desk. "We need the room."

"These students are in my care."

"One student," Vale said. "Mr. Bell can go."

Darius pointed at his own chest. "Mr. Bell?"

"Don't get attached," Kade said.

Darius stood up slowly. At the door he looked back, and for once there was no joke on his face waiting to go.

"You good?"

Kade had the word ready. Fine. He had been fine through three schools and two transfers. He looked out the window at the car with no logo, then at the case on the desk, which had a latch that wanted a key, and found he did not want to lie to Darius in front of these people.

"No idea."

Darius nodded, as if that were a better answer than the usual one. "Text me."

He went.

"Ms. Alvarez can stay," Renn said. "You as well, Mr. Russell. We'll wait for Mrs. Mercer before we open anything protected."

Kade sat back. "How protected?"

Renn considered him. "That depends on how much of today you remember."

The pressure came back behind Kade's eyes as if the question had called it. The gray field. The selection handle floating free of the objects. The word, white on black.

"All of it."

Renn's eyes went to Vale's and came back. A quarter of a second, controlled. Enough. Kade saw it.

"You were hoping I wouldn't."

Renn took Darius's chair. "I was hoping the examination did what it was designed to do."

"Which was what?"

"Stop."

"That's a bad design for a test."

"Sometimes stopping is the most important thing a machine can do."

Something prickled between Kade's shoulder blades. Here was a machine somebody had built to quit, and it had quit on him.

Ms. Alvarez said, "If there is a risk to this student, say so now."

"There is no immediate risk that we know of."

She looked at Renn the way she looked at a badly written question. "That sentence has three holes in it."

Renn opened the case. A tablet. Two paper folders. And a key, a real one, brass, with teeth, the kind of thing Kade had seen on Mr. Boyd's supply-closet ring and nowhere else. Carrying one to unlock information meant theater or paranoia, and Renn did not look like a theatrical man.

Renn touched the key to the edge of the tablet, and the screen woke under a red band of text. Zero Protocol.

The name did something to the room. A reaction was one thing; a thing with a name had been designed in advance. Somebody had sat in a room years ago and imagined him.

"So it has a name."

"Yes," Vale said.

"What is it?"

"A failsafe."

"Against what?"

Neither of them answered.

Renn folded his hands on the desk. "The Aptitude Ascension Examination does not issue a score of zero."

"I watched it do that."

"You watched three digits appear. They were not a score. They were a routing code."

"Routing to you."

"To a restricted review process."

Kade looked at the red band again. "What makes it trigger?"

Renn's face went smooth and neutral in the exact way adults' faces went when they were deciding whether a seventeen-year-old counted as someone the truth was for. Kade hated that face.

Vale saw it too. "An examination path you were never supposed to reach," she said. Renn turned his head toward her, and she kept going. "Your test entered a diagnostic branch that has not been in the public examination specification for decades."

"Then why was it still in there?"

"That is one of the questions we are asking."

"What if I answered wrong?"

Vale's eyes sharpened. "You didn't."

Nobody breathed for a second. Renn said, "Nia."

"He's owed that much."

Kade leaned forward. "Then what did I answer?"

Vale said nothing.

"What did I answer?"

The door opened behind him, and his mother said, "Kade Mercer," in a voice that could have carried to the parking lot.

---

There were moments in a person's life when he wanted his mother, and there were moments when his full name in her voice made him consider changing countries, and this was both.

Elena Mercer stood in the doorway in navy scrubs under a gray coat she had clearly put on while moving. Her hair, usually knotted tight at the base of her neck for a shift, had come loose in three directions. Her St. Vincent badge still hung from the coat pocket, turned around backward.

She looked at him first. Head. Hands. Front of the hoodie. No blood. Then the two strangers, then Russell, and then, briefly and without expression, the red band on the tablet.

"I was told there was a federal incident involving my son."

Kade lifted one hand. "No punching."

"I got that message."

"It seemed like the important part."

"It was the only part that helped."

She crossed the office, put a hand on either side of his face, and turned it to the window light. He let her. Resisting only extended it, and her hands were cold from the car, which was somehow steadying.

"You're all right?"

"Yes."

"Headache?"

He hesitated a half second too long. Her eyes narrowed.

"A little."

"How little."

"Normal little."

"You do not have a normal-little category. You have none, and you have the kind where you end up over the sink."

Vale almost smiled. Elena saw it and turned. "And you are?"

Introductions happened again. Elena listened to all of them without interrupting once, and that was how Kade knew she was angry, because when she was merely irritated she asked questions while people were still talking.

Renn explained. The shutdown, the withheld result, Zero Protocol. He was good at it. He built sentences that were true and careful and, laid end to end, contained almost nothing a person could carry out of the room.

When Renn finished, Elena said, "You have described a procedure. You have not told me why my son is inside it."

Renn paused. Elena waited. Kade glanced at Ms. Alvarez and got the smallest nod. Yes. That is where you got it.

"We believe Kade may have an atypical Resonance profile."

Something in Kade went very still. His mother did not.

"How atypical?"

"We don't know."

"Dangerous?"

"There is no evidence of that."

"To him?"

"No evidence."

"To other people?"

"No evidence."

"Then explain the three government vehicles in front of his school."

Renn looked at Vale. Vale answered. "Because the last time Zero Protocol generated a valid route, the program it belonged to was classified."

Kade stopped breathing for a beat. Elena's face sharpened.

"The last time."

Renn closed his eyes briefly. Vale did not look sorry.

"You said decades," Kade said. "How many?"

Vale looked straight at him. "Three."

Thirty-one years of Resonance, the loop on the hallway screen said, every testing day. Three decades. The arithmetic happened before he could stop it, and he did not like where it put the number: right at the beginning of everything.

Renn must have seen his face. "Do not construct conclusions from incomplete information."

"That's basically a hobby."

"Kade."

He looked at his mother. She had gone pale around the mouth, and it was not the pallor of a long shift. "Sorry."

Renn slid one of the paper folders across the desk. On its cover was a silver crest Kade knew at once. Everybody would have. It sat in the corner of every broadcast the whole country stopped for.

Asterion Academy.

Asterion was what happened when a university, a military base, a professional sports league, and a national obsession had a very expensive child and gave it an island off California. He had watched the matches. Everyone had. He knew the names of graduates while pretending not to.

Northline did not send students to Asterion. Northline struggled to send students back to regular school.

He did not touch the folder. "That's not funny."

"Nobody is joking," Renn said.

"It's a mistake."

"Asterion has invoked a classified admissions authority."

"I didn't apply."

"You did not need to."

"I'm not Resonant."

"We no longer know that."

"I scored zero."

"You did not score zero."

That sentence was becoming irritating enough to hang in the gym. He pushed the folder back across the desk with two fingers.

"No."

"Mr. Mercer," Renn said, "I don't think you understand the offer."

"I understand a national test breaks, the government shows up, nobody answers one question, and then somebody hands me a folder for the most selective school on the planet. I understand that fine."

"It is not a standard admissions offer."

"Good. Then it's normal."

"Kade," Elena said.

He stood. "I'm going home."

Vale moved, but away from the door, one step to the side that left the exit clear. That was so unlike every other version of this room he had ever sat in that he stopped where he was.

"You are permitted to go home," Renn said.

"Generous."

"You are also required to report to Asterion for evaluation within seventy-two hours."

Kade turned back. "Required."

"A valid Zero Protocol route triggers federal Resonance safety statutes."

"So this isn't an offer."

"No."

Of course. Of course the first invitation Kade Mercer ever got to somewhere impossible arrived with compulsory attendance stapled to the front. He looked at Elena, and Elena had opened the folder and was reading, and her face gave away nothing, which was a skill she had learned in rooms with worse news than this one.

"Can we go?"

She looked at Renn. "He leaves with me."

"Yes."

"No surveillance inside my home."

"Agreed," Vale said.

"No press contact routed through your offices."

"Agreed."

"No non-emergency medical testing without his informed consent and mine, while he is a minor."

Renn hesitated. Elena's expression changed by half a degree. The hesitation had been noted, and it would be kept.

"Non-emergency procedures require consent," Renn said.

"Put it in writing."

He did, on the inside cover of the second folder, while Elena read over his shoulder.

While the ink dried, she took Kade into the hall. Not far; the office door stayed open behind them. Classes had let out twenty minutes ago and nobody had gone home.

"Tell me exactly what happened in that room."

"I told Russell."

"I am not Russell."

"Strong opener."

"Kade."

He leaned back against the lockers and told her. Not the first six sections, which had been arithmetic in costume. The seventh. The steel ball on its cable and the line he was not supposed to draw. The handle coming loose. The word. The gray field with nothing on it, and the boundary he had drawn around nothing. The pressure behind his eyes. The three zeros, and the proctor stepping back.

Elena listened the way she listened to patients she thought were leaving out the part that mattered. When he finished, she said, "Did you lose time?"

"What?"

"You said the room was almost empty when you looked up."

"I didn't notice people leaving."

"That isn't what I asked."

He thought about it honestly. The timer reading eighty-seven minutes when he had been sure of longer. Nine chairs pushed back and not one of them heard.

"I don't know."

"Vision changes? Numbness, hands or face?"

"No. No."

"Nausea?"

"Only when the government showed up."

She did not smile. "Kade."

"No."

Her shoulders came down a fraction. Then she looked past him, through the open office door, to the tablet on the desk with the red band still glowing on it, and something went across her face and was put away.

He had been watching her the whole time. "You've heard of it."

Her eyes came back to him. "No."

Too fast. He had grown up on her pauses. She took a breath before she told a waiting room how long the wait would be, and she had not taken one now. He stored it, and she watched him store it, and she rubbed her forehead with the heel of her hand.

"I have heard people use the word zero around Resonance research before. That is not the same thing as this."

"When?"

"Years ago."

"How many years?"

"Kade."

"Everybody keeps saying my name instead of a number."

"I know."

The anger drained a little, because she looked tired. Not shift tired. Older than that.

Then Russell called them back in, and the door closed on the next question.

---

Twenty minutes later he walked out of Northline beside his mother with a folder under his arm. The three vehicles still sat in the maintenance spaces, more drama than a school wedged between a tire warehouse and a self-storage yard had ever asked for.

Darius was waiting across the lot. He raised both eyebrows.

Kade held up the folder so the crest caught the light.

Darius's mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"Asterion?"

"I'm being educationally kidnapped."

"He is going for an evaluation," Elena said without slowing down.

Darius looked at him with an expression Kade could not read, which was rare, and then grinned. "Try not to get expelled before dinner."

"Ambitious."

Darius put his fist out. Kade bumped it.

"Text me."

"I will."

"And if they give you powers—"

"I know. Posture."

Darius laughed. Then the grin went away, and he said, "You coming back?"

Kade blinked. "It's three days."

"Wasn't what I asked."

The answer he wanted to give was easy. Sure. Obviously. Right after the richest school on Earth decides whether I'm medically disappointing. Darius had watched a lot of people at Northline say they were coming back.

"Yeah," Kade said.

Darius nodded once, like a man closing a hood. "Good."

They drove six blocks before Elena spoke.

"You were going to refuse."

Kade watched the tire warehouse go past. "I did refuse."

"No. You performed refusal."

He looked at her. "What's the difference?"

"The difference is you decided you hated it before you had to admit you wanted it."

"That sounds rehearsed."

"I have had seventeen years to rehearse." She kept both hands on the wheel, ten and two, the way she drove when she was too tired to trust herself. "Do you think I don't know what those schools did to you?"

He said nothing.

"You think I didn't read every report? Sit in every meeting? Listen to people tell me that if you would only apply yourself, only cooperate, only stop asking why?"

"I caused some of that."

"Yes."

He looked at her, surprised, and she glanced over.

"You absolutely caused some of it."

"Thanks."

"You're welcome. You can be difficult when you are scared."

"I'm not scared."

"There."

"What."

"That."

He frowned.

"You threw the word out before you checked whether it fit. You do that with every word that might."

He looked out the window. Long shadows, lit shop windows, sprinklers ticking over lawns the summer had already killed. He had lived his whole life on these blocks and knew which intersection flooded after fifteen minutes of rain. Asterion was a place from the broadcasts. This was where the water pooled.

Elena drove another block, and when she spoke again it was softer.

"You have spent years demanding that somebody give you a real chance."

His throat did something. "I never demanded Asterion."

"No. You demanded to be measured by what you can actually do, instead of by how well you sit still while somebody explains a thing you already understand."

He could not argue with that without lying.

The light ahead went red. She stopped and looked at him fully.

"Do not get scared now that somebody has finally asked you the right question."

He thought of the instruction on the screen. Identify the boundary of the active system. Of the line he had drawn through steel because Ms. Alvarez had told him to answer it anyway. Of the proctor stepping back.

"Mom."

"Yes."

"What if the question wasn't supposed to have an answer?"

The light went green. Elena looked ahead and let the car roll.

"Then they should not have asked you."

He smiled despite himself, and slightly resented that she could still do that.

---

She drove past the turn for home. He let one intersection go by before mentioning it.

"House was back there."

"I know where we live."

"Good. I've had enough surprises for one day."

She turned into the lot of a discount store wedged between a pharmacy and a pet-supply shop that had been empty since spring. Half the red letters in the sign had burned out, so the whole building announced itself as Value Ar.

Kade looked at the folder on his knees. "No."

"That word is doing a lot of work today."

"It's three days. I'm being evaluated, not presented at court."

"The packing list says formal academy wear."

"It also says recreation equipment. I could bring a deck of cards and a tie."

Elena parked. "You own neither."

She got out and went through the automatic doors without looking back, which was unfair. She knew he would follow before he let her shop for him alone.

He carried the folder in.

The store smelled like cardboard and detergent and fabric that had crossed an ocean folded into a cube. Elena took a cart with one front wheel that objected to forward motion.

"One collared shirt," she said. "One pair of decent pants. Toiletries that did not come out of the hospital travel bin."

She pulled a black shirt off a rack and handed it to him. He looked at the tag and hung it back up.

"What."

"Twenty-eight dollars."

"That is not a reason."

"It's several reasons written as a number."

She put it in the cart anyway. "We can afford a shirt."

"For three days?"

"Clothing continues to exist after evaluations."

He opened the folder to the equipment page while she pushed toward men's trousers, and read while he walked. The list took up a full column. Two formal academy sets. Three training sets. Indoor shoes, outdoor shoes, weather shell, approved field underlayer, personal medical supplies, and an optional practice uniform priced higher than a month of their electric bill.

The list was on page three. He turned past it to the terms. Section fourteen, residential housing. Fifteen, damage liability. Sixteen started with mandatory equipment, wandered into academy trademarks for two paragraphs, and ended in a sentence set in type small enough to count as hiding.

He stopped beside a display of belts.

"They provide it."

Elena turned.

He held the page out. "Required clothing and safety equipment during provisional jurisdiction remain the property and financial responsibility of the academy."

She read it. Then she flipped back to the list. Then she read the sentence again.

"Provisional jurisdiction," Kade said. "Friendly."

"They put that on page eleven."

"And the shopping list on page three."

Elena's mouth went flat. For the first time since Renn had opened the case, Kade felt something like pity for an institution, because it was about to meet his mother.

She took the trousers out of the cart. The shirt stayed.

"They can issue what they require. You are still taking one thing that is yours."

"Can the thing that's mine come from clearance?"

"Obviously."

They found a dark gray one for eleven dollars with sleeves a little long, which meant it fit him everywhere else.

At the luggage wall she lifted down a small rolling suitcase in hard blue plastic. He recoiled.

"Absolutely not."

"The zipper on your duffel opens under emotional stress."

"It has character."

"It has a tooth missing."

She put the suitcase in the cart. He lifted it out.

"I have a bag."

"You have a grocery-store promotion older than the basil plant you keep forgetting to water."

"It has experience."

Elena put both hands on the cart handle and waited. He hated it when she waited. Silence made weak answers see-through.

"What is wrong with the suitcase?"

"It has wheels."

"That is generally the point."

"It looks like luggage."

She waited some more.

He set the thing down on the floor between them, and the real objection stood there on four bright wheels.

"It looks like I expect to stay."

Her face softened, which was worse than losing the argument.

"Preparing is not consenting."

"Going is mandatory."

"Going to the evaluation is mandatory. Medical procedures are not. Enrollment is not. Letting them turn a question mark into ownership of you is not." She tapped the folder. "Renn signed because we made him. Paper does not make people honest. It gives dishonesty somewhere to stand so you can point at it later."

"That sounds like Ms. Alvarez."

"She texted me while you were packing up the test tablet."

"That's a conspiracy."

"It's a community."

"You do not have to prove you can leave by refusing everything you might need if you stay."

It landed close enough to the conversation in the car to sting, and far enough from it that he could not call it repetition. He looked at the suitcase for a while. Then he put it in the cart.

"We're buying it because the old zipper is structurally compromised."

"Of course."

"I'm still taking the duffel."

"Of course."

Every display television near the registers showed the same channel. A red map of the national testing outage. Then shaky phone footage of a hallway Kade knew better than his own kitchen: Northline's faded blue lockers, the door to Multipurpose Two, Russell going past the camera with both arms spread, trying to herd students back. The caption under it read Mystery Failure Halts National Aptitude Exam.

No name. Not yet.

The cashier followed his eyes to the screen, then down to the crest on the folder under his arm, and her face did a small recognition.

Elena moved the folder into the cart and laid the shirt over it. Not fast. Not dramatic. The motion of someone closing a curtain.

The cashier scanned the suitcase. "Busy day over there."

Northline had never been on national news for anything good. On television the building looked smaller and cheaper, a place the rest of the country could use for ten seconds of mystery and forget before the weather.

"Apparently," he said.

The cashier waited for more. Elena put her card in. Kade gave her nothing.

Outside, the heat had finally started to break. Elena loaded the suitcase into the trunk. Kade stood holding the new shirt on its plastic hanger, and through the store windows the red map flashed.

"I do want to know," he said.

She shut the trunk.

"What I answered. Why it picked me. Why you knew the word."

She did not defend the last part.

"Those are reasons to go," she said. "They are not promises to stay."

He opened the passenger door. For tonight, the distance between those two things was wide enough to stand in.

---

At home, the folder sat on the kitchen table like an accusation.

The house was small enough that the table had several jobs: meals, mail, Elena's continuing-education modules, Kade's half-finished electronics. Tonight every other job had been evicted.

Elena made soup, because she believed soup addressed categories of problem Kade considered unrelated to soup. He sat across from the folder and read the first page, which said Mandatory Evaluation in oversized letters, and then six pages of transport, housing, liability, and privacy that ended in something called provisional institutional jurisdiction, which he read three times.

"Sounds friendly."

She set a bowl in front of him. "Eat."

He did.

Halfway through, his phone buzzed. Darius, in capitals, asking whether he was famous yet. He typed back that Russell had confiscated the paparazzi. Then Tessa: her cousin said zero was impossible. Then a number he did not know, saying, is this Kade Mercer, I'm with Channel, and he blocked it before it got to the number. Another unknown number arrived thirty seconds later.

Elena held out her hand.

"No."

"Kade."

"I can block a number."

"That is not why."

He handed it over.

"Authoritarian."

"Put it on my evaluation."

After dinner she went to the hall closet and brought down the black storage box from the top shelf, and he stopped in the hallway, because he knew the box.

His father's things. There were not many. A watch that did not run. Two photographs, the one that had lived in the front room his whole life and the one that had not. A small mechanical pencil. An employee badge from an industrial systems company.

"Why are you getting that?"

She froze with it in her hands, and for one second he thought she might put it back. Then she carried it to the table and set it beside the folder.

"I don't know."

"That's not reassuring."

"No."

He sat. She opened the lid. The top photograph showed Aaron Mercer at twenty-three or twenty-four, standing beside a workbench, smiling at somebody out of frame. Dark hair. Narrow face. Kade had been told for years that he had Aaron's eyes, and had never found a use for it.

"You think this is about Dad."

Her hand tightened on the print. "I think I heard words today that I have not heard in a very long time."

"Zero."

"Yes."

"What else?"

She looked at him. "I don't know what is connected."

"That's different from no."

"Yes."

He sat back. The answer had cost her something. He could see it in the way she held the photograph by its edges, like a chart she did not want to hand to a family. So he did not push, because for once he could tell the difference between someone hiding a thing and someone who had not yet found a way to say it.

She put the photograph back.

"Your father worked in industrial systems."

"I know."

"That is true."

He looked at her. The sentence had been built too carefully, every word load-bearing, like a wall put up to hold exactly one thing off the floor. She closed the box. The folder stayed open beside it, and for a while the two of them sat at the table with two histories on it and did not touch either one.

At seventeen minutes to nine, his phone, on Elena's side of the table, lit with an academy message. She turned it so he could read.

Transport confirmed. Departure fifteen thirty, two days from now. Destination, Asterion Island. Status: mandatory evaluation, Zero Protocol.

He read it three times. Then he went to his room.

His closet had more empty hangers than clothes. Asterion's packing guide recommended formal academy wear, athletic clothing, personal electronics, and items supporting a successful transition to residential learning. Until tonight, his contribution to that list had been a hoodie with a repaired cuff and a duffel his mother had owned since before he was born. The new shirt hung from the closet door. The blue suitcase sat zipped on the floor by the bed, looking far too committed.

He pulled the duffel out from under the bed and started with socks.

Then he stopped, a pair in his hand, and sat on the edge of the mattress.

The strange thing was not that the government wanted him evaluated. It was not Asterion, or the key, or his mother's hand tight on a photograph. It was that somewhere underneath the fear and the irritation and the suspicion, something else had come up through the floor while he was not watching, and he had not asked it to.

He wanted to know what happened next.

He had spent years pretending not to care about outcomes. It was cheaper. That evening, alone in his room with an empty bag on the bed, he found out that wanting an answer was a great deal more frightening than any zero.

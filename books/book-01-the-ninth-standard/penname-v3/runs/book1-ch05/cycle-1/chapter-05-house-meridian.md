# Chapter 5 — House Meridian

*Ten. Moving day. If they give you a window, don't stand in it looking like you're pricing the glass.*

The gate had refused him two nights ago. He had stood at it with this band on this wrist and the reader had made one flat sound, ASSIGNED RESIDENTS ONLY, and he had gone around by the outside stair in the dark like something being delivered. This morning the reader gave two short warm notes, the glass said MERCER, K. — RESIDENT and cleared, and the iron gate that had stood open both times went on standing open.

A line in a table had changed. His feet noticed anyway.

Enrollment Officer Cole had walked him from Arrival C as far as the bridge and left him with one sentence about the place. "The bell rings when Meridian wins a House Trial." Kade had asked how often. Cole had looked at the bell exactly long enough not to answer, and gone.

Three towers stood around the court, joined by walkways every third floor, blue banners on the rails faded to the color of a work shirt. On the fourth floor of the near tower a bedsheet had been strung between two windows and painted in letters a meter high, WELCOME BACK, MEDIOCRITY, and under it, smaller, in another hand and another color, SPEAK FOR YOURSELF, 612.

The bell hung in the middle of all of it. Bronze, enormous, under a black steel frame three stories tall, its lip worn pale where hands had reached it from places no hand should have been able to reach.

Something was in the frame with it.

A drone the size of a dinner plate had wedged itself between two struts a meter under the bronze, and it was still trying. Its rotors spun up, and it leaned, and it hit the steel with a small hard sound, and settled, and spun up again, like somebody knocking to be let in.

A dozen students with push brooms were sweeping the flagstones under it and not looking up, with the discipline of people who had been told not to. One boy with a tablet was looking up. A woman with a scar through one eyebrow stood beside him holding an empty sweep bin, saying, in a voice that did not need raising, "Navarro. If that touches the bell, I'm not the one you'll be explaining it to."

Kade stopped at the edge of the flagstones with the duffel on his shoulder.

The woman did not turn around. "Bag by the planter. Broom's on the rail."

"I'm—"

"Mercer. Eight fourteen. Broom."

Somebody had put in for him by name before his number posted, and ninety seconds inside the House he was being handed a broom. He could not decide which of those was the insult. He took the broom, because the alternative was standing in a courtyard holding a grocery bag while twelve people swept around him, and he had done that version of a morning before.

He swept toward the bell, because that was where the problem was.

The boy with the tablet was talking to the drone. Not into the tablet. To the drone, low, the way people talk to a car that will not start. "Lift left. *Left.* Your left. Why is your—" The rotors spun. The drone leaned into the strut and struck it and settled.

"It's leaning the wrong way," Kade said.

"It is leaning exactly the way I'm telling it to."

"Then you're telling it wrong."

The boy looked down from the frame. Kade watched the file open behind his eyes, the one everybody on the island seemed to carry, and set his weight for it.

"You're Mercer."

"I've got a broom, if that helps."

"Eight thousand nine hundred eleven." He touched his own chest with the tablet. "Elias Navarro. Eli. There are one thousand five hundred seventy-one people under me on that board and I count them every morning like teeth." The drone struck the strut. Eli flinched with it. "The correction pulse should have it level in a quarter second. It has never once failed on the bench."

"Cut the motors."

"It'll fall."

"It's in a cage." Kade pointed the broom handle at the struts. "It can't go anywhere but down. Every time it tries to get away from the steel, it goes into the steel. Stop letting it try."

Eli's face did a thing where pride and arithmetic fought and arithmetic won by a very small margin. He touched the tablet. The rotors died, and the drone dropped straight out of the frame, and the woman with the bin stepped under it without any hurry at all and caught it, a small thump against the plastic, and looked at Eli over the rim.

"Bay Two stays closed until you can show the fourth-years that nothing else in it is capable of flight."

"That's their line."

"I'm quoting."

"Sera," said a new voice from the court's north edge, "your floor is in the bell."

The girl crossing the flagstones wore training black instead of academy blue and moved the way Dr. Park had moved in the round room, nothing spent that had not been budgeted. Tall. Dark hair tied back off her neck. Kade knew the face from a panel of glass in a plaza, from the part of the ladder nobody scrolled to because they already knew what was there. Vey, Mara. Year one. Seven.

"My floor is a floor," Sera said. "Navarro is a person on it. Different problems."

Mara's eyes went across the bin, the tablet, the broom, and stopped on Kade, and he could see her doing sums. "Mercer."

"Vey."

"Zero point zero zero."

"You've been reading."

"It's on a wall. I walk past walls." She did not soften it into a question and he found he preferred that to the pity shaped like curiosity he had eaten beside at Arrival. "This floor's roster closed four weeks ago. I helped close it."

"Then somebody reopened it."

"Somebody did." She let it sit, and he understood she was telling him that she did not know who, and that she was not used to not knowing, and that she was not going to ask him in a courtyard. "Eight fourteen."

"So I'm told."

"Then you'll be at dinner." She was already moving. "It starts at seven. It does not start at seven."

"She's seven," Eli said, cradling the drone.

"I know what she is."

"No. I mean she counts things. She just counted you. Watch what she does with the number."

---

Sera took the back stair up, not the elevator, and she took one handle of the duffel without asking.

His hand tightened on the other. She looked at it, once, straight on, the way you look at a reflex you have watched begin in other people, and he made the hand stop.

"Everybody walks up the laundry stair on their first day," she said. "So their feet know it when the alarm does. Nobody remembers it's there, which is what makes it fast."

"You give people the way out before you give them the room."

"Everybody should know the fastest way out of anywhere." She said it as though it were about buildings. He did not believe her.

The eighth-floor corridor curved with the tower around the court. Every fourth door stood open. An argument about load tolerances came out of one, music out of another. On the wall by the stairs blue tape divided a board into a grid, names down the side, jobs across the top. KITCHEN CLOSE. BAY RESET. COURTYARD SWEEP. NIGHT CHECK. His name was already in it, blue ink, DINING CLOSE, THURSDAY, with NAVARRO in the box under his.

"I've been in the building twelve minutes."

"Fourteen. The floor needed a Thursday." She tapped the grid. "Housing does maintenance. We do the messes we make before they turn into maintenance."

"You counted the doors off the courtyard before you looked at the bell," she said, further down the curve, as though it were the next item on the list.

"Habit."

"Mine too. Three placements. They kept sending me back to regular school because I tested fine, and I kept coming back because testing fine isn't the same as fitting." A pause with nothing in it that wanted filling. "Which was yours?"

"Northline."

"I know."

"It's not on the board."

"You just told me."

"Seventeen people asked housing to switch into eight fourteen after your name posted," she said. "I'm the one who told housing to lock it."

"Why?"

"Because Navarro asked for a roommate in July and got told nobody was coming, and he's had a bare bed for a month, and I don't trade people." She set her end of the bag down outside the door. The seam by the handle had stretched white where the stitching pulled. "People are going to tell you that you skipped a line."

"I did."

"Yes. The person behind that door has been last on more lists than you have, and he'll tell you a line is just somewhere to stand." She pushed the bag an inch toward him with her boot. "You owe this floor Thursday. Nobody's asking for your biography."

She started away.

"Vale." She looked back. "What's your rank?"

"Not the thing that carried your bag." And she went.

He stood in the corridor with the duffel against his shin and resented, precisely, how useful she had been. Useful people were a cost. You started expecting them.

---

Room eight fourteen held two beds, two desks, a window onto the court, and about forty percent more Eli Navarro than it had been built for. A sensor rig stood on one desk with wires up out of it like hair. The drone lay on the far desk in a dozen pieces, and above the far bed a wire model of the same drone turned slowly in the air, whole.

Eli pointed at the bare bed in case Kade had been considering the floor.

He unpacked in the order he always did. Socks first. Three shirts and jeans into one drawer. The academy shoes went onto the shelf still in their box, and Eli watched that go by and had the sense to say nothing. The eleven-dollar shirt, tag on, went over the back of the chair where it could be seen. The photograph of him and his mother on the front step went on the desk, facing the bed.

Then he turned the bag over to look at the handle, because Sera had said it was going to tear and he wanted to know how long he had.

Something slid inside the lining and ticked against the desk.

There was a flat zip pocket sewn into the inside of the duffel, meant for a key, and in seventeen years he had never once put anything in it. He opened it. A mechanical pencil, silver, the kind you refilled. Small tooth marks in the barrel near the tip.

He knew it from a box on a top shelf, from one afternoon at eleven when he had taken everything out and put it back in the same order. It had been in that box two nights ago, on the kitchen table, beside a silver folder. He had not packed it. The bag had been on that table after he went to bed, and his mother had told him about a note, and had not told him about this.

He set it on the desk because he could not decide where it went.

Eli came past for a screwdriver, saw it, and picked it up, the way he picked up everything, an object as a way into a conversation. He turned it. His thumb found the tooth marks and stopped on them.

"It was my father's," Kade said.

Eli put it down. He put it down exactly where it had been, as if the desk had a mark for it, and said, "Sorry," and went back to his own side of the room with the screwdriver, and did not ask.

Kade waited for it. He had heard the question begin in a lot of rooms, and he knew the pause that came before it, the small breath. Eli fitted a rotor arm and did not take the breath. The not-asking sat in the room like something left without a card, and it was the first thing anybody on the island had chosen not to know about him.

He put the pencil in the drawer.

---

"What did it look like from the ground?" Eli said, some time later, with the drone half assembled and the wire model pulled open over the room into a web of colored lines. "I've got the log. I want the other angle."

"Every time it tried to lean away from the steel, it leaned in."

"It can't. The controller reads the tilt and lifts the low side. It's the simplest loop in the machine."

"Which side is low?"

"The—" Eli stopped with his hand in the air.

Kade had not needed a field for it. He would have said so to anyone who asked. It was a cargo tram on a whiteboard. It was a plank with a number painted on the end. A rule that had been true a moment ago and was still being obeyed.

"It decides which rotor is low," he said, tracing it over the lines. "Then it turns. The decision stays stuck to the rotor. It's fixing where it used to be."

Eli rewound the log with two fingers and let it crawl. The frame tilted. The controller picked its pair. The body rotated through level, and for thirty-two milliseconds, the counter said, the machine went on treating an old relationship as a current one.

"Oh," Eli said, very quietly.

He changed one line. He ran it. The wire drone tipped, caught itself, and hung.

He ran it again. It hung.

He turned around with the face of a man who has been robbed by a friend. "Your baseline says you have no field perception."

"It's certified. I put my thumb on it."

"You found a rotating-reference error from the ground. With a broom."

"I don't know what that is."

"You just *did* it."

"Then name it so a person can hear it."

Eli looked at the hovering wire model for a while. Then he pulled a chair toward it and began talking, fast, in a vocabulary Kade caught perhaps half of. When Kade did not catch it he said so, and Eli backed up. He did not get louder. He did not say it the same way twice. He went back three steps and came at it from another side until it landed, and Kade, who had spent eight years in rooms where being lost was treated as a choice, kept bracing for the impatience and kept not getting it.

By the time the real drone could lift off the desk and hold itself level over Eli's palm, Kade's side of the room had stopped looking like somewhere a person was about to leave.

*Eleven. Mom says there's a pencil. She says don't be strange about it. I'm relaying. I don't know what it means and I've decided not to.*

---

At six fifty-eight Eli looked at the wall clock and stopped in the middle of a sentence about torque.

"We're late."

"Dinner's at seven."

"Yes."

Doors were opening all down the curve. At the elevator a fourth-year holding a full car took in the eight of them waiting and said, "Stairs," and the doors shut, and everybody moved. Seven flights. His knee from yesterday's plank had an opinion by the third landing and he kept it to himself, and learned going down that Meridian dinner was voluntary, that the good tables went by order of arrival, and that upperclassmen took stairs the way other people took fences.

The hall was the long glass building. Inside, it was loud and warm and smelled of bread and something roasting, and a screen hung on the far wall so large it seemed to have its own weather. They got the last two seats at the north table one second before a group from the west tower reached the same bench, and the west tower group weighed it and went toward the windows with the discipline of people used to being scored. Sera was two places down, already eating, and did not look up and did not need to.

A spoon rang against a glass at the front. Nothing happened. Under the standings display a broad fourth-year looked toward the courtyard doors, toward the bell, and several voices said "Do not," together, and he raised both palms and touched a panel on the wall instead. The speakers gave one deep bronze note. Every conversation stopped.

Then the whole room booed.

He waited it out. "The real bell remains reserved for real victory."

"Fourteen months!"

"Fourteen months and nine days," somebody corrected, and the precision had the weight of a grievance counted daily.

The fourth-year's wrist was lit. Six hundred twelve. Kade thought of the bedsheet.

"Three notices. One. Training Bay Two is closed until Navarro proves nothing else in it flies."

Eli sank an inch. "The frame was separate research," he told his plate.

"Two. Standings update after Friday assessments. We remain twelfth." A groan. He pointed at a paper sign taped under the screen, TOP HALF ADJACENT, in marker. "Or that, depending how you weigh courage against arithmetic." Applause. Meridian, Kade was learning, applauded its own jokes on a schedule.

"Three. New arrivals stand when called. Name, rank, and one thing this House can ask of you."

Two returning students went first. One offered two years of structural field training and got a decent hand. One offered a key to a music practice room in another building and got a better one.

"Kade Mercer."

The hall did not go quiet. It went interested, which was louder.

He sat one second too long, and a boot found the leg of his chair from two places down and nudged it, and he stood. The standings display refreshed as if it had been waiting for him. MERCER, KADE — 10,482.

"Kade Mercer. Ten thousand four hundred eighty-two." He put a hand on the back of the chair. "I'd like it noted I'm the only person in this room whose rank can't get worse."

Nobody laughed.

"It can," the fourth-year said. "We've done it."

That got the laugh, and it was not his, and he stood inside it and minded less than he had expected to. Three days of adults asking him to test, consent, hold still, trust. This was a room asking him for something too, and it should have felt like one more claim on him, and it did not, and it took him a second to see why. Everyone else had stood up first. The question had been put to all of them before it was put to him.

"One thing," the fourth-year said.

"I don't know yet."

The hall held.

"He can see where a thing thinks it is," Eli said, at ordinary volume, not to Kade. To the room.

"That isn't a sentence, Navarro."

"It will be."

Six hundred twelve looked past them through the glass, to where a drone hung at eye level outside the dining hall doors, having followed Eli down and stopped at the threshold under House safety rules like a dog that knew about carpets.

"Provisional," he said. "Like the rest of him. Sit."

Kade sat. Three rows over, Mara Vey turned back to her table without any expression at all, and he told himself that was nothing, and counted it.

Then dinner happened, and he learned that everyone in Meridian knew everyone's rank and that pretending not to was worse manners than knowing. A first-year named Anton wanted to know if Zero Protocol came with classified training. A girl named Nessa wanted to know if the agents at his house had been armed. Somebody wanted to know what a Northline was, and he answered the ones he could and turned the rest, and was, for a while, a person at a table being asked things.

Then Nessa said, "Is Zero hereditary? Does it run in families?"

"No."

It was out before his fork was. Too fast, the way a door goes in wind. Nessa shrugged and turned to Anton.

Sera, two places down, had not looked up. She looked at her plate one second longer than the plate required, and went on eating, and he knew that was not the end of it.

"This bread," Eli announced, "has been engineered by someone who hates jaws," and the table, which had views on the bread, took it, and Kade breathed. Eli did not look at him for credit.

---

A cheer went up at the far end of the hall.

The screen had switched to an arena feed. Two upperclassmen stood inside a white circle on a gray floor, and the names came up. Dai, a woman, year four. Tomas, a man, year four. Under both, the same two words. STANDARD SIX.

The hall settled. Even Mara turned.

He had watched Standard Six bouts on a cracked gym screen since he was six, with a voice telling you what to admire. This was the House's own feed, one wide camera and no commentary.

The signal.

Dai moved first and Kade lost her. One instant at the line, the next six meters in with the floor cracking behind her where she had left it. Tomas raised one open hand and her momentum did not stop. It turned. She went sideways, rotating, boots on the wall for less than a second, and the wall bowed where she had been. Something had gone into it. Something had come out somewhere else.

He leaned in. The hall went away.

Dai struck. Tomas took the strike and split it, and three floor panels fractured at once in three different places as the force went looking for somewhere to be. And Kade saw it. Not the field. He could not see the field and never had. He saw where things went, force entering one system and leaving by another, the same question he had put to a chamber wall and a hood string and a plank. Where does this thing end, and who decided?

The cold thumb pressed once at the base of his skull, exact, and let go.

His fingers were at the back of his neck under the collar before he had decided to put them there.

"You see something."

Mara was standing beside the table. He had not watched her cross the room.

"Everybody sees the match."

"Nobody else is sitting like that." Eli had stopped eating. Sera was watching him and not the screen.

On the feed Dai came in again and Tomas gave ground, and the force went through the floor and into a brace at the foot of the barrier that flexed, just enough, and bled the transfer out. He could almost have drawn the path and could not have said with what.

"They keep pretending the fighter is the system," he said.

Mara's face changed. "Meaning what?"

The hall came back around him. Trays, noise, the screen. "Nothing."

"That word's carrying a lot," Sera said, to her plate. "You said it exactly the way you said *no* a minute ago."

He had nothing for that. Eli looked delighted. "She is going to be very good for you."

On the screen the match ended with Dai pinned to the barrier by her own acceleration, redirected, and the hall stood up shouting.

The argument began before she was off the floor. Anton said she had overloaded the second strike. Nessa said she had used that output in the regional final. Eli pulled a napkin over and drew a square for the arena and three marks where the floor had gone and said it was not stronger, it was cheaper, the east brace had taken most of it, and Anton said that was not an explanation.

"Because he prepared the route." Mara had not sat down. She took the pen out of Eli's hand without asking and drew a line from Tomas's mark to the wall. "First exchange. He tests the floor here."

"He missed," Anton said.

"He touched the floor." A second line. "Next time she crossed he gave up the center and steered her toward the same support." The third line ended at the brace. She gave Eli his pen back.

"She thought he was losing ground," Kade said.

"He was choosing where the ground ended."

It pulled his head around. She had said it in plain tactical language, the kind anyone at the table could have used, and she was looking at the napkin and not at him, and he could not tell if that was the point.

It spread down the table from there. Somebody found the arena schematics. Somebody found Tomas's last five matches and started comparing where he gave ground. Nessa threw out two of Eli's assumptions because he had ignored Dai's recovery time, and Eli, after a fight, gave them up. Anton changed his position and did not pretend he had always held the new one. Kade asked something, and nobody told him it was basic. They answered, and disagreed about the answer, and then asked him what he had seen, as if that were a piece too.

At Northline, group work had meant one person doing the assignment and four negotiating credit. Here six people could look at the same thirty seconds and lay six different pieces of it on a napkin. He was the least informed person at the table. He had been that at a lot of tables, and it had never once felt like this, like a thing with a door in it.

Worse, there were people he wanted to go through the door with.

His phone buzzed against his leg. A campus account he did not know.

*TAREN HOLT — #34. Mercer. Meridian roof, after. Bring the face you make when someone tells you something for free.*

A second one behind it.

*The face is correct. Keep it. Come anyway.*

He showed Eli. Eli whistled, softly. "Taren."

"Is that a diagnosis?"

"More of a forecast."

Sera read it over his shoulder without apology. "He likes people. People tell him things. He tells other people different things. Usually useful ones." She reached across and took a roll off Eli's plate. "Not everything with teeth is bad for you."

Eli looked at the plate. "That was mine."

"Was."

---

At twenty hundred he stood on the landing of the laundry stair with the phone at his ear, because he wanted to know the stair before he needed it.

"Room," his mother said, at the kitchen table, with the folder.

"Eight fourteen. Roommate builds drones and puts them in places drones shouldn't be. There's a woman on the floor who told me the fastest way out of the building before she told me where dinner was."

"I like her."

"You would."

"I would. I'm saying it." A pen. "And the other thing."

"Once. Watching a fight on a screen. No field in the room. Just a screen and me thinking about where the force went." He made himself finish it properly. "Cold. Not hurt. Gone in a breath."

She wrote it down. "Cold goes at twenty hundred. Hurt goes right then."

"Same rule."

"Same rule."

"You put a pencil in my bag."

"I put a pencil in your bag."

"You didn't ask."

"You'd have said no."

"I'd have said no."

"It's been in a box for seventeen years. Somebody should write with it." Her voice did not change. He knew her well enough to know what that cost. "Go up to your roof. Don't sit on anything that wasn't built to be sat on."

---

The roof was a set of terraces around the upper mechanical floor, with greenhouse panels on the seaward side and enough benches to suggest someone had once expected students to come up and reflect. Meridian had answered with two heaters, a chess table bolted down against the wind, and a hammock slung at a height no safety office had approved. One of the bell's stay cables came up out of the dark to an anchor in the parapet.

Taren Holt sat on the parapet beside the anchor with thirty meters of night at his back. Dark curls. Academy jacket open in the wind. The kind of face that looked relaxed because making other people relax was how it got through rooms. Thirty-four had been near the top of the first-year ladder on the plaza glass, in the part Kade had scrolled past.

"You came."

"You said free."

"I said *for* free. Different preposition." He held out a can and kept it out until Kade took it. Carbonated citrus, with enough warning text on the side to count as medicine. "Short version. There's a pool on you."

"I know. A boy at Arrival lost money."

"On which side?"

"Mine."

"Then he's smarter than the forum." Taren pointed with his own can at the island going down into the dark below them. "Half of this place thinks you stole a room. The other half thinks Zero means you're secretly terrifying."

"Those people haven't watched me try to move a puck."

"Facts walk. Rumor sprints." He drank. "And there's a third group, small, that just wants to see what you do. I'm in that one."

"That's the one I trust least."

"You should." He said it with real approval. "Campus rule, Mercer. Everybody on this island is selling something. The ones who tell you they aren't are selling that."

"What are you selling?"

"Context." He nodded down toward the residential ring, where lights had just come on in a small arena at its foot. "For instance."

A crowd had gathered at the observation rail before the lights finished warming. Two first-years walked into a white circle eight meters across, painted on a flat gray floor with nothing around it. No walls. Nowhere to send anything. Nowhere to hide a mistake. The board above it lit one line.

CROSS, JULIAN — 1.

Kade stood up straighter without meaning to. "Rank one."

"Rank one."

The other name was sixty-one. Julian took the inland side of the circle, toward the Spire. Sixty-one took the sea side, rolled his shoulders once, and stood.

The signal. Neither of them moved.

Five seconds. Kade looked at Taren. "Thrilling."

"Wait."

At seven, sixty-one stepped left. Julian moved right, not away but across, cutting the line, and sixty-one stopped, and they reset without having touched.

At eleven, sixty-one drove in. The first step was fast enough to blur, but Julian was off the center line before it landed, and sixty-one turned the drive into a hook, and Julian dropped a shoulder, and the hook skimmed his jacket instead of his chest. The arm was right there, extended, open. He could have had it.

He stepped out instead, and went back to the inland half of the circle.

"Why give that up?" Kade said.

Taren did not answer.

Below, sixty-one smiled.

At nineteen he came again, same first step, different finish. He cut across Julian's retreat and put a pulse through the floor, low, a pale line flashing between their feet, and Julian's lead boot skidded on it. Julian let the bad step take him, dropped a hand to the floor, turned on it, and sixty-one, who had braced for resistance, found none, and his pulse spent itself past Julian's hip into nothing.

Julian came up inside his reach. He did not strike. He touched sixty-one's elbow, moved it perhaps three centimeters, and stepped out again.

Twenty-four seconds.

"He's had him twice."

"Has he?"

Sixty-one followed him toward the inland edge. He had the center now and had not paid for it, and the rail had begun shouting for him to close. Julian's heel stopped half a meter from the line. From the parapet, with a can going warm in his hand, Kade thought he looked caught.

The first exchange had shown which way sixty-one went when Julian crossed him. The second had shown what he did when a retreat stood open. Now the retreat was gone.

At thirty-one, Julian moved first. One short step toward the center.

Sixty-one answered before it finished. Right foot down hard, shoulder turned, committed, the angle that had cut off the escape last time, because last time there had been an escape to cut off.

There was not. Julian stopped.

The pulse broke on empty floor. Sixty-one's arm came up to catch a body that had never come into reach, and Julian took the arm, turned his hips, and walked sixty-one's own weight across the line.

The clock stopped at thirty-eight seconds.

No blast. No impossible speed. Three pieces of information, each bought at a price, and one decision made after the answer was certain.

Kade found that he was frowning.

Taren was watching him and not the arena. "What."

"I expected it to look harder."

"It does, from inside." He counted on his fingers. "Output counts. Control counts. Record counts. Everybody in the top hundred can do something hard. He's first because after somebody wrecks his plan he keeps doing the correct hard thing, more times in a row than anyone else in the building. Fewer bad decisions. That's the whole secret."

It annoyed Kade at once, which he had learned usually meant it mattered.

At Northline, changing the plan had meant the plan had failed and someone was about to assign blame. He had gotten good at improvising because improvising was the one thing nobody could grade. Down there a boy had improvised three times in thirty-eight seconds, and every one of them had been part of the plan.

Julian went to his opponent and pulled him up. No arm in the air. No look at the rail. An instructor came onto the floor with a slate and pointed at something on the replay, and Julian bent his head to it and listened like someone who had lost.

Kade watched longer than he meant to.

"Sleep tonight," Taren said. "Your combat assessment's tomorrow."

Kade turned. "Nobody's told me when it is."

"I'm telling you."

"How do you know?"

"House schedule access."

"I don't have House schedule access."

"You got here this morning."

"That isn't an explanation."

Taren grinned. "Context."

Rank one had needed thirty-eight seconds. The combat line on Kade's band said UNRATED, and tomorrow somebody with a slate would try to turn that into a number too.

Three days ago the thought would have had him at the transit desk with a departure time. He waited for that to happen. It did not. Something in him had leaned forward instead, toward the circle, the way you lean toward a problem that moves.

Taren lifted his can. "To last place."

"That's a terrible toast."

Kade touched his can to it.

Below, Julian Cross stepped back into the circle for another round. Above them the bell hung in its frame in the dark, and did not ring, and had not for fourteen months and nine days, and Kade understood that he would know that number for as long as he lived here. It was not his grievance yet. But he had started counting, and eight floors down there was a pencil in a drawer that had spent seventeen years in a box, and his mother had decided it was time somebody wrote with it.

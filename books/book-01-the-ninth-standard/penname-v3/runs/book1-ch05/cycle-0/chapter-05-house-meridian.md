# Chapter 5 — House Meridian

*Ten. Moving day. Don't carry the bag like there's a body in it.*

The gate knew him.

He had stood at it two nights ago with the same band on the same wrist, and the reader had made its one sorry sound and told him ASSIGNED RESIDENTS ONLY, and he had gone around by the outside stair in the dark like a delivery. This morning the reader saw the band and made a different noise, two short warm notes, and the iron gate that had been standing open both times went on standing open, and MERCER, K. — RESIDENT scrolled once across the glass and cleared.

Nothing had changed but a line in a table somewhere. His feet felt it anyway.

Enrollment Officer Cole had walked him across from Arrival C with the tablet under his arm and no display lit at his wrist, and in the whole walk had said one thing about the place Kade was being given to.

"The bell rings when Meridian wins a House Trial."

Kade had looked up at it. Bronze, enormous, hung under a black steel frame three stories tall, the lip of it worn pale where generations of hands had touched it from places no hand should have been able to reach. "How often?"

Cole had kept walking. Kade had taken that for the answer and filed it.

Now Cole was gone back toward Central with his one pace of following still in him, and Kade stood alone in a courtyard with a grocery-store duffel on his shoulder and nobody to be the correct amount of unimpressed for.

Three towers stood around the court, joined by walkways every third floor, with blue banners hung from the rails, some new and some so faded the blue had gone the color of a work shirt. The lower levels were glass: common rooms, a dining hall long enough to be the building he had walked behind in the dark, training bays with their shutters half up, and students in all of it, moving, a hundred of them visible from where he stood and none of them looking at him yet.

On the fourth floor of the nearest tower somebody had strung a bedsheet between two windows and painted on it in letters a meter high:

WELCOME BACK, MEDIOCRITY.

Under it, in a different hand and a different paint, smaller and angrier:

SPEAK FOR YOURSELF, 612.

He laughed. It got out before he could get in front of it, one syllable, alone in the court, and he heard it come back off the bell.

He liked the place. He had liked it from the gate. That was a problem he would deal with later, when he had a door to close.

The doors of the nearest tower opened and a boy came through them carrying three tablets under one arm, a tool case in that hand, half a pastry in the other, and, pinned against his chest by his chin, most of a drone. It was not assembled. It was a drone the way a spilled drawer is a desk.

He did not see Kade. He saw the pastry.

They met at the corner of the planter. The tablets survived because the arm was already clamped around them. The tool case swung. The drone left his chest in a dozen pieces, going up, and Kade's hand went out on its own, the way it had gone out for a tool on a tilting floor yesterday, and closed on a rotor with one blade missing.

Everything else hit the stone.

The boy looked down at the pastry, which he still had.

"Excellent," he said.

Kade held up the rotor. "Your priorities are in a strange order."

The boy looked at the rotor. Then at the duffel. Then at Kade's face, and Kade watched it happen, the small change, the file opening behind somebody's eyes, and set his weight for it.

"Oh," the boy said. "You're Mercer."

"Yes."

"Ten thousand four hundred eighty-two."

"It's on a wall. It's on several walls."

"I'm eight thousand nine hundred eleven." He said it the way people announce a birthday. He waited, and when Kade did nothing with it, he pointed at his own chest with the pastry. "Elias Navarro. Eli. As of this morning I am better than one thousand five hundred seventy-one people."

"That's one way to read it."

"It's the only way worth the arithmetic." He crouched and began collecting parts into the front of his jacket. Kade put the bag down and crouched with him, because there were a lot of parts and four hands beat two.

"What happened to it?"

"Nothing."

Kade turned the rotor so the missing blade showed.

"That was research."

"Into?"

"Whether the safety limiters on the House flight bays are set too conservative." Eli took the rotor from him and looked at it with real tenderness. "They are not."

Kade laughed again. Twice in a minute. He was going to have to watch that.

"Navarro." A voice from the walkway above, level, not raised, arriving with the confidence that it would be heard. "Tell me you did not put another machine into the east stairwell."

Eli's face did something complicated and then went still. "Be calm," he said to Kade, low, in the voice of someone giving advice he could not take.

The girl coming down the last flight wore training black instead of academy blue, and she came down it the way Dr. Park had crossed the round room, nothing wasted and nothing hurried, every step already paid for. Tall. Dark hair tied back off her neck. He knew the face from a panel of glass in a plaza, from the part of the ladder nobody scrolled to because they already knew what was there.

Vey, Mara. Year one. Seven.

Her eyes went across the debris, to Eli, to the duffel, to Kade, and stopped, and he could see her doing sums.

"Mercer."

"Vey."

One eyebrow. "You looked me up."

"There was a very large board. You were near the top of it. It was hard not to."

"I'm eight thousand nine hundred eleven," Eli said, from the ground.

"We know, Eli." She had not looked at him. "Your baseline produced no measurable output."

It was not a question and she did not soften it into one, and he found he preferred it to the pity shaped like curiosity he had eaten beside last night. "Word travels."

"It's a school built around measurement. Anything measured travels."

"Sounds exhausting."

"It's supposed to be." She let that sit. "What House?"

"This one. Apparently."

Something moved in her face. Not much. A fraction of a degree and gone, and he would not have caught it on anyone he had not been watching. "Meridian's first-year roster was finalized four weeks ago."

"Then somebody reopened it." He had been told, in an elevator, that somebody had put in for him by name before the number posted. He had not been told who. He had been on the island long enough to know he was not going to be told standing in a courtyard. "Take it up with whoever. They love me."

"I doubt that."

"So do I."

For one second something near her mouth considered being amusement and voted against it. She turned to Eli. "Clean the stairwell."

"It was one landing."

"Before Vale sees it."

Eli went a shade paler, gathered the last of the drone into his jacket, and went in through the doors at something just short of a run.

Kade shouldered the bag. "Vale."

"You'll meet her."

"That sounded like a warning."

"It was information." She was already walking. "You seem to have trouble telling them apart."

He followed her in, because she had not said not to, and because it was the direction his room was in, and because he wanted to see whether she would look back to check that he had. She did not.

---

Meridian's lobby was smaller than the arrival residence's and about four times as used. Competition banners covered the long wall, old ones, with names and years written straight onto the cloth in marker by the people who had won them and, lower down, by people who wished they had. The table by the door had been repaired so many times that none of its legs matched. A sofa wore a patch on one arm shaped like a continent. The cork board offered tutoring, sparring partners, a string quartet in need of a viola, three running clubs, debate, and something called UNAUTHORIZED ASTRONOMY, across which somebody had stapled a strip: OFFICIALLY AUTHORIZED THIS YEAR. TRY TO BE NORMAL ABOUT IT.

Above the board a display held the House standings. Twenty Houses. Meridian at twelfth. Under the screen somebody had taped a sheet of paper.

TOP HALF ADJACENT.

"Last year was difficult," Mara said, reading it with him.

"I respect the messaging."

"Meridian is unusually comfortable with failure." He could not tell whether she was reporting it or objecting to it.

"Maybe that's why they sent for me. Cultural fit."

She smiled. It was there and then it was not, a lit window as a train goes past, and he found he had counted it, and disliked that he had.

The woman coming down the central stair was older than the students around her by a year or two and moved like it, like somebody who knew which steps in a building creaked. Short dark hair. A narrow white scar across one eyebrow. Ink going up under the sleeves of a plain gray shirt, and no rank anywhere on her, no lit wrist, no lanyard. The lobby did not go quiet. It got a little more orderly, the way a room does when the person who counts the exits walks into it.

"Sera," Mara said.

Sera Vale looked at Kade one second longer than was polite, and then at the duffel.

"You're the transfer."

"Provisional."

"You're carrying your whole life in a grocery bag with one handle re-sewn."

His hand closed on the strap. He had a sentence for that, and it was not a kind one, and before he could use it she took the other handle.

"This is going to tear."

"I can carry it."

"I didn't say you couldn't." She took half the weight anyway, and he felt the bag go light and felt himself start to pull it back, a reflex as old as the bag, and then she looked at him once, straight on, as if she had watched the reflex begin, and he stopped.

She did not comment on it. That was worse, and better.

"Eight fourteen," she said. "Navarro's your roommate."

"Is that a punishment?"

"For which of you?"

"Open question."

Mara had already gone the other way without a goodbye, which he was beginning to understand was not rudeness but economy.

In the elevator Sera pressed eight and said, to the doors, "You went to alternative school."

He looked at her. "That's not on the board."

"No."

"Then how."

"Because I did."

The doors opened. He did not move for half a second. She walked out, and he caught up.

"Which one?"

"Three. They kept sending me back."

"Back where?"

"Regular. They kept deciding I was ready."

"Were you?"

"No." Without heat. A fact about weather.

The eighth-floor corridor curved with the tower around the court. Every fourth door stood open. Music came out of one, an argument about load tolerances out of another, and from somewhere the smell of burned sugar, which Sera noted with her nose and chose not to pursue. On the wall by the stairs, blue tape divided a board into a grid, names down the side, jobs across the top. KITCHEN CLOSE. TRAINING BAY RESET. COURTYARD SWEEP. NIGHT CHECK.

His name was already on it. Blue ink, somebody's handwriting, under DINING CLOSE, THURSDAY, with NAVARRO in the box beneath.

"I've been in the building twelve minutes."

"The floor needed a Thursday."

"So it's labor."

"It's living near people." She tapped the board. "Housing staff do maintenance. We do the messes we make before they turn into maintenance. If Eli tells you a scorch mark is cosmetic, make him clean it anyway."

"Noted."

"Quiet room, west end. Real quiet, not Meridian quiet. Training bays lock at twenty-two hundred unless a fourth-year signs for supervision. Laundry's behind that door. The stair behind the laundry is the fast way to the courtyard when the alarm goes. Faster than the main stair, because nobody remembers it's there." She said the last as though it were the most important item on the list, and to him it was.

He looked down the curve at the green marker over the far door. "You give people the way out before you give them dinner."

"Everybody should know the fastest way out of anywhere."

He did not have to explain why that landed. She had said it to him as if she already knew.

She set her end of the bag down outside eight fourteen. The seam by the handle had stretched white where the stitching pulled. Another flight and it would have gone.

"Why here?" he said. "For you."

Her face did something complicated and then settled, the way a scale settles. "Because being bad at one system turned out not to be the same as being bad at everything. Took me three schools to get that sentence. You can have it free."

He looked at the door, because it was easier.

"People are going to call you lucky," she said.

"I am lucky."

"Yes. That's not the same as owing them shame for it." She waited until he looked at her. "They'll say you skipped a line. They'll say somebody made a political decision. They'll say Zero bought you a room other people spent years earning."

"That's reasonable."

"No. It's predictable. Different word." She pushed the bag an inch toward him with her boot. "You don't owe anybody shame for arriving by a different road. You owe them what everybody on this floor owes. Thursday."

She started away down the curve.

"Vale."

She looked back.

"What's your rank?"

Her mouth tilted, not quite a smile. "High enough not to tell you." And she went.

He stood in the corridor with the bag against his shin and disliked, precisely and thoroughly, how useful she had been. Useful people were a cost. You started expecting them.

---

Room eight fourteen held two beds, two desks, a window onto the court, and about forty percent more Eli Navarro than it had been designed for.

Every surface had something on it. A half-built sensor rig on one desk with wires standing up out of it like hair. Two training gauntlets on the floor, opened, plates laid out in order. Above the far bed a projection turned slowly in the air, a wire model of a drone, the same drone, whole. The wall panel beside the window was off its clips and leaning against the bed, and Eli was inside the wall to the shoulders.

"Good news," said the wall.

"I doubt it."

"Our room circuit can take forty percent more draw than housing says it can."

"That's a sentence with bad news inside it."

"It means we can run a private simulation core."

"Why is the wall open?"

"I was confirming." He came out with dust in his hair and a meter in one hand, saw the bag, and pointed at the bare bed with the meter, in case Kade had been considering the wall.

Kade put the bag on the bed and did not open it yet.

"I should tell you," Eli said, "that after your assignment posted, seventeen people asked housing to switch into this room. Seventeen. Then housing locked the form."

"Why?"

Eli looked at him as if he had asked why water was wet. "Because you're the most interesting person in the incoming class."

"I'm last. It's certified. I put my thumb on it."

"Those are unrelated facts." He went back into the wall.

Kade unzipped the bag and started with socks, because that was the order, and Eli, it turned out, treated every object that came out of it as an opening.

"You own one charger."

"I own one tablet."

"Brave." The academy's formal shoes came out in their box, unopened, and went onto the shelf in the box, and Eli watched that and had the sense to say nothing. Three shirts. Jeans. The training sets in their packaging. The eleven-dollar shirt on its hanger, tag still on, went on the back of the desk chair by itself, where it could be seen.

Out of the side pocket, the photograph. Him and his mother on the front step two summers ago, her hand on the back of his neck.

Eli looked at it from across the room, which was as close as he came. "Your mom?"

"Yes."

"She looks like she's about to take your pulse."

"She was. She's a nurse. It isn't a look, it's a reflex."

Eli waited. It was a small wait with room in it for another question, and Kade could feel the shape of the one that would fit: the other side of the step, the person who was not in the frame. Eli did not ask it. He turned back to the wall panel and started fitting it onto its clips, and the not-asking sat in the room like a gift somebody had left without a card.

Kade set the photograph on the desk, facing the bed.

The projection over Eli's bed flashed red.

ATTITUDE CORRECTION FAILED.

The wire drone rolled onto its side, clipped a rendered stair rail, and came apart into twelve neat pieces that fell the way real pieces never do.

Eli looked at it with both hands still on the wall panel. "That's inaccurate."

Kade looked from the simulation to the jacket on the floor, still full of actual parts. "It looks peer-reviewed."

"The correction pulse should fire before the roll passes eleven degrees." Eli left the wall and pulled the model open with both hands, and it spread across the room into a web of colored lines, forces, angles, numbers changing beside each joint. "It fires here. See?"

"No."

Eli turned around, surprised.

"You said *see* like the colors were going to turn into information on their own."

"Right. Sorry. Yes." He collapsed half of it and built it back slower. One line for where the drone wanted to be. One for the rotor that was supposed to put it there. A number at each rotor, going up and down. "Sensors read the tilt. The controller adds lift on the low side. Frame levels. Simple."

He ran it. The drone tilted. The controller fired. The drone rolled harder and hit the rail.

Kade watched it three times, and it was not Resonance he was watching, and he would have said so to anyone who asked. It was the thing he had done to a cargo tram on a whiteboard, and to a plank with a number stenciled on it. A rule, applied. A rule that had been true a moment ago.

"Your low side moves," he said.

"Obviously."

"Does the controller know?"

Eli opened his mouth and did not put anything in it.

Kade traced it in the air over the lines. "It decides this rotor is the low one. Then the frame turns. But the decision stays stuck to the rotor. It's fixing where the drone used to be."

Eli stared at the model. He rewound three frames with a flick of two fingers and let it crawl. The body tilted. The controller chose its pair. The body rotated past level, and for thirty-two milliseconds, the counter said, the system went on treating an old relationship as a current one.

"Oh," Eli said, quietly.

He changed one line and ran it. The drone tipped, caught itself, and hung in the air.

Neither of them said anything. Eli ran it again. It hung.

He turned around with the face of a man who has been robbed by a friend. "Your baseline says you've got no field perception."

"It does."

"You found a rotating-reference error in under a minute."

"Your drone was steering by where it used to be."

"That is what a rotating-reference error *is*."

"Then name things so a person can hear them."

Eli looked at the hovering wire drone for a while. "I spent four hours on that."

"I'm sorry for your loss."

"No. No, this is good. This is very good. It's also humiliating, but the ratio's favorable." He dragged a chair toward the projection, hooked its leg on a gauntlet, and nearly went over, and Kade had the gauntlet off the floor before the second foot could find it.

"Where does this live?"

Eli pointed at a shelf with three things already on it. Kade waited. Eli moved the three things.

They did the floor after that, both of them, because there was no crossing it otherwise. The panel went back on its clips. The gauntlets went on the cleared shelf. The sensor rig moved eight inches so a person could set down a tablet, and while they worked Eli talked through every change he wanted in the controller, fast, in a vocabulary Kade caught perhaps half of. When he did not catch it he said so, and Eli backed up. He did not get louder. He did not explain it the same way twice. He went back three steps and came at it from a different side until it landed, and Kade, who had spent eight years in rooms where being lost was treated as a choice, kept bracing for the impatience and kept not getting it.

By the time the real drone, back together on the desk, could lift off and hold itself level over Eli's open palm, Kade's clothes were in one drawer and his side of the room had stopped looking like a room somebody was about to leave.

He looked at the drone hanging there. Then at Eli, who was talking to it.

*First friend here*, some part of him offered, and he took the thought back at once as premature and unsupported by evidence, and noted that he had thought it anyway.

*Eleven. You've made a friend and you're annoyed about it. I can tell from here.*

---

At six fifty-eight Eli looked at the clock on the wall and stopped in the middle of a sentence about torque.

"We're late."

"Dinner's at seven."

"Yes." He was already at the door.

Kade followed, because the alternative was admitting he wanted to know why, and the corridor answered before he could ask: doors opening all along the curve, people coming out of them, and at the elevator a fourth-year holding a car that was already full, taking in the eight of them waiting, and saying, "Stairs."

The doors shut. Everybody moved.

Seven flights. His knee from yesterday's plank had an opinion by the third landing and he kept it to himself, and learned on the way down that Meridian dinner was voluntary, that the good tables went by order of arrival, and that upperclassmen took stairs the way other people took fences. A boy two flights ahead vaulted the last five steps, landed beside a wall clock, and checked it with a frown, as if the jump had been forced on him by a schedule.

The hall was the long glass building he had walked behind in the dark. Inside it was loud and bright and smelled of bread and something roasting, long tables, a serving line down one side, and a screen high on the far wall so large it seemed to have its own weather.

"North table," Eli said, breathing hard.

Half its seats had jackets on them. "Why?"

"Potatoes come out of the kitchen door at that end. They reach it first. After that it's thermodynamics."

"You crashed a drone testing safety limits, and *this* is where you bring strategy."

"Food produces measurable outcomes."

They took the last two seats a second before a group from the west tower reached the same bench, and the west tower group looked at them, weighed it, and redirected toward the windows with the disciplined acceptance of people used to being scored. Nobody argued. Kade set his tray down and found Sera two places along, already eating, who did not look up and did not need to.

A spoon rang against a glass at the front of the hall. Nothing happened. It rang again.

"That never works," Eli said.

Under the standings display a broad-shouldered fourth-year looked toward the courtyard doors, toward the bell, and several voices said "Do not," at once, and he raised both hands and touched a panel on the wall instead. The speakers gave one deep bronze note, a recording, and every conversation in the hall stopped dead.

Then the whole room booed.

He waited it out with his palms up. "The real bell remains reserved for real victory."

"Fourteen months!"

"Fourteen months and nine days," somebody corrected, and the precision had the weight of a grievance counted daily.

The fourth-year's wrist was lit; from the north table Kade could just read it. Year four. Six hundred twelve. He thought of the bedsheet. *Speak for yourself.*

"Three notices. One. Training Bay Two is closed until Navarro demonstrates that nothing else in it is capable of flight."

Eli sank an inch. "The stairwell was separate research," he told his plate.

"Two. House standings update after Friday assessments. We remain twelfth." A groan went down the tables. He pointed at the paper sign under the screen. "Or top-half adjacent, depending how you weigh courage against arithmetic." That got applause. Meridian, Kade was learning, applauded its own jokes on a schedule.

"Three. New arrivals. You stand when I call you. Name, rank, and one thing this House can ask of you."

Kade looked at Eli. "You left this out."

"I was busy being useful."

"Debatable."

Two names first, returning students who had changed Houses. The first offered two years of structural field training and got a decent hand. The second offered a key to a music practice room in another building and got a better one.

"Kade Mercer."

The hall did not go quiet. It went interested, which was louder.

He sat one second too long. Then a boot found the leg of his chair from two seats down and nudged it, not hard, and he stood.

The standings display refreshed as if it had been waiting for him. MERCER, KADE — 10,482. Nobody in the room had needed reminding, and now none of them could pretend they had not been.

"Kade Mercer," he said.

"We got that part," from the back.

"Good. Meridian's literacy runs ahead of its ranking." It got a laugh, and the laugh loosened something in the hall, and he was ashamed of how badly he had needed it.

"Rank ten thousand four hundred eighty-two."

"For now," Eli said, at ordinary volume, not to Kade. To the room.

Heads turned toward Eli, who did not seem to notice he had said anything and went on eating, having declared faith by accident and apparently intending to live with it.

The third part waited. One thing the House could ask of him.

Three days. Every adult on the island had asked him to test, comply, consent, hold still, wait, or trust. Every student had wanted the classified version. The academy wanted a category. This should have sounded like one more claim on him, and it did not, and it took him a moment standing there to find out why: every other person in the room had stood up and answered it first. The music room. Two years of structural work. The question had been put to all of them before it was put to him.

"I don't know yet," he said.

The hall held.

He put a hand on the back of his chair. "But if your drone starts steering by where it used to be, apparently I can help with that."

Eli made a small wounded noise.

Six-twelve looked past them, out through the glass, to where a drone hung at eye level beyond the dining hall doors, having followed Eli down and stopped at the threshold under House safety rules, like a dog that knew about carpets.

"Accepted," he said.

Kade sat. Three rows over, Mara Vey, who had turned in her seat to watch, gave him a nod so small it could have been a correction to her posture. He told himself it did not count. He counted it.

Then dinner started, and Kade learned that everybody in Meridian knew everybody's rank, and that pretending not to was worse manners than knowing. The House could call the ladder reductive and mean it and still say a stranger's number before passing the bread. A first-year named Anton wanted to know if Zero Protocol came with classified powers. A girl named Nessa wanted to know if the agents at his house had been armed. Somebody wanted to know if the national test had crashed because he had done something to it, and somebody wanted to know what a Northline was, and he answered the ones he could and turned the rest, and was, for a while, simply a person at a table being asked things.

Then someone down the table he had not met said, "Your dad Resonant too?"

"No."

It was out before the fork reached his mouth. Too fast, the way a door goes in wind. Nobody heard it. The boy who had asked shrugged and moved on.

Sera, two places down, heard it. She did not look at him. She looked at her plate for one second longer than the plate required and went on eating, and he knew that was not the end of it.

"These potatoes," Eli announced to the table, "have an unacceptable structural consistency," and the table, which had views on the potatoes, took the bait, and Kade breathed. Eli did not glance at him for credit. He went on about starch as if it were the only subject in the world.

A cheer went up at the far end of the hall.

The big screen had switched to an arena feed. Two upperclassmen stood inside a white circle on a gray floor, and the names came up. Dai, a woman, year four. A man called Tomas, year four. Under both, the same two words. STANDARD SIX.

The hall settled. Even Mara turned in her seat.

He had watched Standard Six bouts since he was six years old, on a wall screen in a district gym with a crack across it. Those came with a voice telling you what to admire. This was the House's own feed, no commentary, no cut angles, one wide camera and two people who had been trained to treat physics as a negotiation.

The signal.

Dai moved first and Kade lost her. One instant at the line, the next six meters in and the floor cracking behind her where she had left it. Tomas raised one hand, open, and her momentum did not stop. It turned. She went sideways, rotating, boots on the wall for less than a second, and pushed off, and the wall bowed where she had been. Something had gone into it. Something had come out somewhere else.

He leaned in. The hall went away.

Dai struck. Tomas took the strike and split it, and three floor panels fractured at once in three different places as the force went looking for somewhere to be. And Kade saw it. Not the field. He could not see the field and never had. He saw where things went. Force entering one system and leaving by another. The same question he had put to a chamber wall in an examination room not a week ago, and to a hood string on a plane. Where does this thing end, and who decided?

The cold thumb pressed once at the base of his skull, exact, and let go.

He put two fingers to the back of his neck under the collar without deciding to.

"You see something."

Mara was standing beside the table. He had not watched her cross the room.

"What?"

"In the match. Something."

"Everyone sees the match."

"Not sitting like that." Eli had stopped eating. Sera was watching him, and not the screen.

On the feed Dai came in again and Tomas gave ground, and the force went through the floor and then into a brace at the foot of the barrier that flexed, just enough, and bled the transfer out. He could almost have drawn the path, and could not have said with what.

"They keep pretending the fighter is the system," he said.

Mara's face changed. "Meaning what?"

The hall came back around him. Trays, noise, the screen. "Nothing."

"Careful," Sera said.

He looked at her. She pointed her fork at him.

"You use that word when you mean you noticed something and don't want anybody asking."

He had nothing for that. Eli looked delighted. "Oh, she is going to be very good for you."

"Eat your structurally unacceptable potatoes."

On the screen the match ended with Dai pinned to the barrier by her own acceleration, redirected, and the hall stood up shouting.

The argument began before she was off the floor.

"She overloaded the second strike," Anton said.

Nessa shook her head. "She used that output in the regional final."

"Then his partition was stronger."

"His ceiling hasn't moved in a year."

Three people talked at once. Eli pulled a paper napkin over and drew a square for the arena and three marks where the floor had gone. "Not stronger. Cheaper. The east brace took most of it."

"That's not an explanation," Anton said.

"Because he prepared the route." Mara had not sat down. She reached over Eli's shoulder, took the pen out of his hand, and drew a line from Tomas's starting mark to the wall. "First exchange. He tests the floor here."

"He missed."

"He touched the floor." A second line. "Next time she crossed, he gave up the center and steered her toward the same support." The third line ended at the brace. She gave Eli his pen back.

Kade ran it in his head. Tomas backing. Dai driving him. The wall waiting behind them both.

"She thought he was losing ground," he said.

"He was choosing where the ground ended."

It pulled his head around. She had said it in plain tactical language, the kind anyone at the table could have used. Probably. He was not sure of that, and she was looking at the napkin and not at him.

"So the last redirect only works on that side."

"At that cost. Yes."

"If she'd changed the angle?"

"He'd need a different plan."

"If the brace failed?"

"Barrier sensors trip before structural failure," Eli said. "Probably."

"Remove the last word," said Sera.

"Barrier sensors trip before structural failure."

"Is that true?"

"Statistically."

"Navarro."

"I'd have to check the maintenance record."

"Better."

It spread down the table from there. Somebody pulled up the arena schematics. Somebody else found Tomas's last five matches and started comparing where he gave ground. Nessa threw out two of Eli's assumptions because he had ignored Dai's recovery time, and Eli, after a fight, gave them up. Anton changed his position and did not pretend he had always held the new one. Kade listened, and asked something, and nobody told him it was basic. They answered, and disagreed about the answer, and then turned around and asked him what he had seen, as if that were also a piece.

At Northline, group work had meant one person doing the assignment and four negotiating credit. Here six people could look at the same thirty seconds, come back with six different pieces of it, and lay them on a napkin.

He was the least informed person at the table. He had been that at a lot of tables, and it had never once felt like this, like a thing with a door in it. It meant there was something to learn.

Worse: there were people he wanted to learn it with.

His phone buzzed against his leg. A campus account he did not know.

*TAREN HOLT — #34. You're Mercer. Meridian roof after dinner. I'll explain why half the school already hates you and the other half wants to buy you lunch.*

A second one behind it.

*Do not accept the lunch. There is always a catch.*

He showed Eli. Eli whistled, softly. "Taren."

"Is that a diagnosis?"

"More like a forecast."

"Dangerous?"

"Depends if you're standing outside."

Sera read it over his shoulder without apology. "Don't tell him anything you wouldn't want repeated."

"That sounded like a warning."

"He likes people. People tell him things. He tells other people different things. Usually useful ones." She reached across and took a potato off Eli's plate. "Not everything dangerous is bad for you."

Eli looked at the plate. "Those were mine."

"Past tense."

---

At twenty hundred he stood on the landing of the laundry stair with the phone at his ear, because Sera had said it was the fastest way down and he wanted to know it before he needed it, and because his mother had said every day, and call, not text.

"Room," she said. She was at the kitchen table. He could hear the folder.

"Eight fourteen. Roommate builds drones and crashes them. On purpose, he says."

"Do you like him."

"It's been nine hours."

"That isn't what I asked."

"Yes."

"Good. What else."

He gave her the floor. The duty grid with his name on it before he had arrived. A woman with a scar through her eyebrow who had told him the fastest way out of the building before she told him when dinner was.

"I like her," Elena said, at once.

"You'd say that."

"I would. I'm saying it." A pause with a pen in it. "And the other thing."

"Once. Watching a fight on a screen. There wasn't a field in the room. Just a screen, and I was thinking about where the force went." He made himself finish it the way he had in the round room. "Cold. Not hurt. Gone in a breath."

"A screen."

"A screen."

She wrote it down. He could hear her write it down. "Cold goes at twenty hundred. Hurt goes right then. Same rule."

"Same rule."

"Go up to your roof," she said. "Don't sit on anything that wasn't built to be sat on."

---

The roof was not a roof so much as a set of terraces around the upper mechanical floor, with greenhouse panels on the seaward side, a weather mast on the other, and enough benches to suggest somebody had once expected students to come up here and reflect. Meridian had answered with two portable heaters, a chess table bolted down against the wind, and a hammock slung between a pipe and a railing at a height no safety office had approved. The bell hung in its frame in the court below, and one of the frame's stay cables came up out of the dark to an anchor in the parapet.

Taren Holt sat on the parapet beside the anchor with thirty meters of night at his back.

Kade knew him too. Thirty-four. It had been near the top of the first-year ladder on the glass in the plaza, a name in the part he had scrolled past. Dark curls. Academy jacket open in the wind. The smile of someone who looked relaxed because making other people relax was how he got through rooms.

"You came."

"You threatened me with information."

"Works on everybody."

Kade stopped two meters off. "Half the school hates me."

"I rounded."

"Up or down?"

"Depends which forum." Taren held out a can. Kade looked at it.

"No lunch."

"Good. You learn fast."

"It's not lunch."

"Still free."

He took it. Carbonated citrus, with enough warning text on the side to count as medicine.

Taren pointed with his own can at the island going down into the dark below them, towers and bridges and the lit ring of the transit line. "Short version. Three kinds of people here already care about you."

"I was happier five seconds ago."

"One. People who think you stole a place."

"Reasonable."

"No. Predictable."

Kade turned and looked at him.

Taren grinned. "Sera?"

"Unfortunately."

"Two. People who think Zero means you're secretly terrifying."

"Those people haven't watched me try to move a puck."

"Facts walk. Rumor sprints. Three. People who think you're interesting."

"That's the group I trust least."

"You should." He drank. "It's the one I'm in."

"You said three kinds. Which are you?"

The smile adjusted. It did not go anywhere. It was re-aimed. "Useful."

"That wasn't one of the options."

"I don't like frameworks with edges." He said it like a line he had tried on somebody before.

"Did you rehearse that?"

"A little."

Which was an answer near the question and not to it. Kade thought of a fork pointed at him across a table. "What do you want?"

"Nothing yet."

"That's a bad sentence."

"I want to see what you do. You're either the strangest clerical mistake this school has made in a decade or the best first-year story it's had, and I'd like to know which before everybody else does."

"That sounds like gambling."

"I don't gamble. I'm thirty-four. I prefer games I can lean on." He looked out toward the training arm of the island. "Campus rule, Mercer. Everybody here is selling something."

"You too."

"Especially me."

"Selling what?"

"Context." Lights came on below, in a small arena at the foot of the residential ring, and Taren pointed at it with the can. "For instance."

A crowd had gathered at the observation rail before the lights finished warming. Two first-years walked into a circle, white, eight meters across, painted on a flat gray floor with nothing around it. No walls. Nowhere to send anything. Nowhere to hide a mistake. On the board above it one line lit.

CROSS, JULIAN — 1.

Kade stood up straighter without meaning to.

"Rank one," he said.

"Rank one," Taren agreed.

The other name was sixty-one. Julian took the inland side of the circle, toward the Spire. Sixty-one took the sea side, rolled his shoulders once, and stood.

The signal.

Neither of them moved.

Five seconds. Kade looked at Taren. "Thrilling."

"Wait."

At seven, sixty-one stepped left. Julian moved right, not away but across, cutting the line, and sixty-one stopped, and they reset without having touched.

At eleven, sixty-one drove in. The first step was fast enough to blur, but Julian was off the center line before it landed, and sixty-one turned the drive into a hook, and Julian dropped a shoulder, and the hook skimmed his jacket instead of finding his chest. The arm was right there, extended, open. He could have taken it.

He did not. He stepped out and went back to the inland half of the circle.

"Why give that up?" Kade said.

Taren did not answer.

Below, sixty-one smiled.

At nineteen he came again. Same first step. Different finish. This time he cut across Julian's line of retreat and put a pulse through the floor, low, a pale line flashing between their feet, and Julian's lead boot skidded on it. The rail reacted. Julian let the bad step take him, dropped a hand to the floor, and turned on it, and sixty-one, who had braced for resistance, found none, and his pulse spent itself past Julian's hip into nothing.

Julian came up inside his reach.

He did not strike. He touched sixty-one's elbow, moved it perhaps three centimeters, and stepped out again.

Twenty-four seconds.

"He's had him twice," Kade said.

"Has he?"

"You are going to be unbearable about this."

"Briefly."

Sixty-one followed him toward the inland edge. He had the center now, and had not paid for it. He had put the top-ranked first-year on the back foot twice, and the crowd at the rail had begun shouting for him to close. Julian's heel stopped half a meter from the line. He looked caught. From the parapet, with a can going warm in his hand, Kade thought he looked caught.

The first exchange had shown which way sixty-one went when Julian crossed him. The second had shown what he did when a retreat stood open. Now the retreat was gone.

At thirty-one, Julian moved first. One short step toward the center.

Sixty-one answered before it finished. Right foot down hard, shoulder turned, committed, the angle that had cut off the escape last time, because last time there had been an escape to cut off.

There was not. Julian stopped.

The pulse broke on empty floor. Sixty-one's arm came up to catch a body that had never come into reach, and Julian took the arm, turned his hips, and walked sixty-one's own weight across the line.

The clock stopped at thirty-eight seconds.

No blast. No impossible speed. Three pieces of information, each collected at a price, and one decision made after the answer was certain.

Kade found that he was frowning.

Taren was watching him and not the arena. "What."

"I expected it to look harder."

"It does, if you're in there."

"Helpful."

"People misunderstand the ladder. They think the top is where the biggest output lives."

"It's not?"

"Sometimes. Julian's not first because he can do the most dramatic thing." Taren counted it off on his fingers. "Output counts. Control counts. Record counts. But everybody in the top hundred can do something hard. He keeps doing the correct hard thing after somebody has wrecked his first plan. Fewer bad decisions than anyone else in the building."

It annoyed Kade at once, which he had learned usually meant it mattered.

At Northline, changing the plan had meant the plan had failed and somebody was about to assign blame. He had gotten good at improvising because improvising was the one thing nobody could grade. Down there a boy had improvised three times in thirty-eight seconds, and every time it had been part of the plan.

Julian went to his opponent and pulled him up. No arm in the air. No look at the rail. An instructor came onto the floor with a slate and pointed at something on the replay, and Julian bent his head to it and listened like someone who had lost.

Kade watched longer than he meant to.

"Your first combat assessment's tomorrow," Taren said.

Kade turned. "How do you know that?"

"House schedule access."

"I don't have House schedule access."

"You got here this morning."

"That isn't an explanation."

Taren grinned. "Context."

Kade looked back down at the circle. Rank one had needed thirty-eight seconds. The combat line on Kade's band said UNRATED, and tomorrow somebody with a slate would try to turn that into a number too, the way they had turned everything else about him into one.

Three days ago the thought would have had him at the transit desk with his band and a departure time. He waited for that to happen. It did not. Something in him had leaned forward instead, toward the circle, the way you lean toward a problem that moves.

Taren lifted his can. "To last place."

"That's a terrible toast."

"Only if you plan on staying there."

Kade touched his can to it.

Below, Julian Cross stepped back into the circle for another round. Above them the bell hung in its frame in the dark, and did not ring, and had not for fourteen months and nine days, and Kade understood that he would know that number for as long as he lived here, and that he had already started counting.

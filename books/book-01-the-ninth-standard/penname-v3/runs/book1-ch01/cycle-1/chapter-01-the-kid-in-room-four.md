# Chapter 1 — The Kid in Room Four

The metal detector at Northline Alternative had not worked since March, and Kade Mercer was the only person who seemed to mind.

Not because he wanted to be searched. He walked through the dead gray arch every morning with his district card in his fist, and every morning he looked at the loose cord coiled on the floor behind it and thought the same thing: a machine people believed was working was more dangerous than one they knew was broken. Nobody at Northline believed it was working. That was almost the same as safe.

Mr. Boyd sat behind the security desk with a cup of coffee and a roll of electrical tape, waving students through by name. He knew all forty-two. There were only six classrooms to keep track of, and Boyd had been in the building longer than the paint.

"Mercer. Card."

Kade held it up without stopping. Boyd was already looking past him at the door, where a fourteen-year-old named Miguel was wheeling in a bicycle with the rear brake cable hanging loose against the tire. Miguel's family did not own a car. Boyd told him to bring it around the desk.

Kade had seen the cable before Boyd had. It was the kind of thing he saw without wanting to: the frayed strand where the housing ended, the slack that meant the far end had let go. He kept walking, because if he stopped he would start telling Boyd how to route the new cable.

Northline Alternative lived in an old county records building between a tire warehouse and a self-storage yard. Somebody had painted the outside blue three years ago. The desert had since taken the south wall down to the color of a bandage left on too long. The district website called it a personalized transitional learning campus. Students called it Northline, and parents called it whatever they had to call it when a relative asked where their kid went to school now.

Some of the forty-two had been expelled. Some had been arrested. Some had stopped showing up to regular school until the district stopped pretending they would come back. There were night jobs and probation officers and panic disorders and babies and complicated houses. Two social workers shared one office. The vending machine in the multipurpose hallway sometimes let go of three bags of pretzels for the price of one, for reasons nobody had ever found, and Darius Bell believed it did this out of respect.

Kade had none of the obvious reasons for being there. Vice Principal Russell had told him, more than once, that this was part of his problem. What he had instead was a file: three suspensions, two transfers, one semester of failed classes he could have passed with his eyes shut, and enough teacher comments to build a whole person out of administrative adjectives. Bright but inconsistent. Argumentative. Does not utilize available opportunities. His favorite was from ninth grade: Kade often appears disengaged even when he understands the material. As though understanding a thing ought to have made pretending to enjoy it easier.

He had read the file once, cover to cover, while Russell was out of the office taking a call. Russell had left it open on the desk, which in Kade's view turned unauthorized access into a failure of information security. Ms. Alvarez had disagreed, for eleven minutes.

Darius came through the arch last, hood up, two inches taller than Kade and built like the district had accidentally enrolled a linebacker in remedial geometry. He said, "Don't talk to me till ten," which was what he said every day, and went past Boyd without showing a card, and Boyd let him.

---

Room Four had a heater that shuddered when it started and a clock above the door that stopped every time the heater did, so nobody trusted the clock. Kade did not need it. He had Darius.

*A cargo tram with a mass of eighteen thousand kilograms accelerates from rest to twelve meters per second over eight seconds. What average force is required?*

He had the number before he finished reading. Mass times the change in speed, over the time. Twenty-seven thousand newtons. The arithmetic was not the problem. The problem was the last word. Required by what? A tram on steel rails paid friction on every meter. A tram on a magnetic guideway paid something else, and the software had not said which kind of tram it was, or whether eighteen thousand kilograms meant the cargo or the cargo plus the thing hauling it. It had drawn a cartoon tram with cartoon crates in the bed and wanted him to pretend the crates were welded down.

He opened the notes field under the answer box and typed *friction*. Then he looked at the word for a moment and deleted it, letter by letter, and typed twenty-seven thousand where the software wanted it.

Green check. A cartoon rocket ship rose off the bottom of the screen, looped, and threw off sparks. Kade had been at Northline for forty-three school days and had hated the rocket ship on every one of them.

Across the aisle, a chair scraped. Ten nineteen by the bad clock.

"Bathroom," Darius said.

Ms. Alvarez did not lift her eyes from the district tablet. "You went fourteen minutes ago."

"Different bathroom."

"There is one bathroom."

He was already moving. Four strides to the door, and his hand on the push bar.

Kade turned his tablet around so the cartoon tram faced the aisle.

"What happens to the boxes?"

Darius stopped. "What boxes."

"Eighteen-thousand-kilo tram. Nothing to twelve meters a second in eight seconds. Software says the answer's twenty-seven thousand newtons." Kade tapped the stacked crates. "What do these do?"

"Why."

"Because the question doesn't say. It's got all these numbers in it and it doesn't say."

Darius looked at the screen, and Kade watched him decide that answering was faster than not answering. "They come through the front."

"Why."

"Because that's what stuff does. You ever stand up on a bus when the driver stomps it? Everything in the aisle ends up on the driver." He took his hand off the bar and came back one stride, which was as far as his pride would allow. "You hit speed that fast with a loose load, the load's the thing moving. The tram's just what it hits."

"Mr. Bell," Ms. Alvarez said, still not looking up, "sit down and say that again, louder."

Darius did not sit. But he did not leave either, and Kade took the opening while it was there.

"You're at four," he said, quiet enough that it stayed between them. "Out of five. Number five, Russell bumps you back to review."

"Don't."

"Review's Friday. Your mom traded a shift for Friday. You hit five today, he pushes it a week, and she gave up a shift for nothing and has to give up another one."

Darius's jaw moved. He had spent most of yesterday pretending not to care whether his mother could get the time off, which was the loudest way he knew how to care about anything.

"Walk out at lunch," Kade said. "Nobody logs lunch. Call it a protest."

Darius looked toward the front of the room, where Ms. Alvarez was studying her tablet with the total concentration of a woman who had heard nothing. Then he came down the aisle and dropped into his chair hard enough that the desk legs complained.

"Still hate you."

"That's allowed."

Somebody in the back laughed. Darius was sixteen. He had come to Northline from Canyon Ridge High after he put another student through the glass panel beside a classroom door; the other kid had needed eleven stitches, and Darius had been suspended and had never gone back. A file and a person were two different documents, and people who had only read the file tended to get Darius wrong.

"Since Mr. Bell has decided to remain enrolled," Ms. Alvarez said, "we're going to use him."

"I was enrolled the whole time."

"Your chair was."

She touched her screen, and the tram bloomed across the wall display, the crates now the size of real ones. Beneath the problem she typed a second line, and the wall printed it large. WHAT INFORMATION IS MISSING.

Kade kept his thumb still on the edge of his tablet. He knew where this was going and who it was going to. Ms. Alvarez looked at a room the way another person would take inventory, and the inventory always ended at him.

"Mercer. You typed something under your answer and erased it. Say it out loud."

"The software says I'm right."

"The software fires a rocket for arithmetic. I'm asking you."

The heater kicked on. The clock stuck.

"Friction," he said.

She wrote it on the wall.

"What else?"

"Track grade. Drive efficiency. Whether twelve meters a second is measured against the track. Whether the load shifts." It was coming faster than he wanted. That was the trouble with a list. Once it started it had its own momentum, and he could hear his voice going the way it went when he stopped guarding it. "Wheel condition. Wind, if it's outside. Peak force against average. Whether the tram drives itself or gets pulled. Whether eighteen thousand includes the tram."

She wrote LOAD SECUREMENT last, and under it, in smaller letters, BELL. Darius sat a little straighter and pretended he had not.

"Mr. Bell says loose cargo comes through the front," Ms. Alvarez said. "Mr. Mercer says the problem doesn't tell us whether the cargo is loose. Let's see what the problem is hiding." She rolled the AV cart out from beside her desk, lifted the dead projector off it, and stacked in its place six of the old district algebra books nobody had opened since the tablets arrived. "Mr. Bell. Rest to top speed, as fast as you can manage. Stop at the tape."

There was a strip of yellow tape across the floor by the door, left over from a fire drill. Darius looked at the cart.

"This is a trick."

"It's physics. Physics is a trick."

He stood, took the handle, and shoved. The cart went the length of the aisle with its bad wheel chattering, and he stopped it at the tape with both arms and his whole weight, and the six algebra books kept going. They went off the front of the cart in a fan and hit the floor one after another, loud as doors slamming.

Nobody laughed for a second. Then everybody did.

Darius looked at the books on the floor with something Kade had not seen on his face in a classroom before, which was interest. "That's the bus."

"That is twenty-seven thousand newtons, nowhere in the problem," said Ms. Alvarez. "Pick those up." She drew a box on the wall around the number. "This is the answer the question asks for. Under the simplification the question states, it's correct, and nobody is going to take it away from you." She drew a larger box around the list. "This is what keeps somebody from loading a real tram badly and blaming the math when it goes through the front."

Someone by the window said, "Which one's going to be on the test?"

"The small one."

"So what's the big one for?"

Ms. Alvarez capped her stylus. "Answering the question and understanding the tram are two different jobs. The district only tests the first one. I'd rather you leave this building able to do both."

Kade looked at the two boxes. Schools were not supposed to say that part out loud.

She assigned the next problem. A water tank, an outlet valve, a flow rate that assumed water behaved politely. Kade entered the number they wanted, and the rocket rose, and before it finished its loop he opened the notes field and typed what the question had not said. No tank height. No outlet diameter. Nothing about whether the flow held steady as the level dropped.

He looked at the words. He did not delete them.

Darius leaned across the gap. "Who are you writing to?"

"I'm writing down what they left out."

Darius considered the water-stained ceiling tile above the heater. "If it's Northline water, the pipe's clogged."

Kade added PIPE CONDITION. Darius nodded like that settled it, and Kade sat with the little list under his answer and found that for once he was in no hurry to close the problem.

---

At ten thirty-one the speaker above the door clicked, and every head came up, because Northline's speakers worked only when nobody wanted them to.

"Attention, students." Russell's voice, tinny and official. "All seventeen-year-old students scheduled for the National Aptitude Ascension Examination will report to Multipurpose Two at eleven hundred hours. Bring your district identification card. Testing begins promptly at eleven fifteen."

Darius groaned.

"You're sixteen," Kade said.

"I'm groaning for you."

The speaker clicked off. Ms. Alvarez looked at Kade over the top of her tablet, and she was not smiling, which was unusual enough that he looked at her properly.

"If a question in there is badly written," she said, "answer it anyway. Complain afterward."

"That's not how I work."

"I'm aware. Work differently for two hours."

He shrugged, because shrugging was free.

The chime at her station sounded. "Ten-minute break before testing. Stay on campus. Nobody set anything on fire."

Darius caught Kade's sleeve on the way out. "Come watch me get chosen. The machine knows about the test. That's the whole point of today."

Kade let himself be towed.

The multipurpose hallway had one working wall screen, and on testing day the district ran the same loop on it that it ran everywhere, with the sound off. A woman in a gray Conduit rig stood at the base of a highway overpass with one hand raised while the span above her, cracked clean through, held long enough for the last car to clear. A seawall in a storm, and the line of figures standing on it. Then the arena, the part everybody waited for, the crowd and the score ticker, and the words along the bottom of the frame. THIRTY-ONE YEARS OF RESONANCE. YOUR TURN.

Thirty-one years since the world had rearranged itself around the small fraction of human beings who could do things the rest could only watch. The Conduit on the woman at the overpass cost more than Northline's building, metal detector included. He did not expect to be one of them. A great many institutions had explained to him over the years exactly what he was capable of, and the reviews were mixed.

The vending machine sat in its alcove under the screen. Darius pushed his hood back and put his palm flat against the side panel, twice, in the same worn place.

"One bag," Darius told the machine. "That's all I'm asking."

"You talk to it like it can hear you."

"It can hear respect."

Two credits. B-seven. The coil turned, and one bag of pretzels dropped, and Darius's whole face fell in on itself.

"It hates me."

The coil kept turning. A second bag fell. Then a third.

Darius pointed at Kade. "Bear witness."

"I take back everything I've ever said about you."

Tessa arrived from nowhere and had a bag out of the tray before Darius could close his hand.

Kade did not take one. His stomach had started doing something he declined to name.

Tessa tore her bag open and looked up at the arena on the screen. "What do you think you'll get?"

"On the test? Somewhere between zero and whatever the top is."

"My cousin got seven twelve last year."

"Is that good?"

She stared at him until he gave up. Everybody knew the thresholds. Above seven hundred, scholarship offices called your house. Eight hundred brought recruiters. Nine hundred got you on the local news, and the highest score anyone had ever recorded was supposedly nine sixty-eight, though people argued online about whether the scoring had changed since then.

Tessa lowered her voice like the machine was listening. "My aunt says anything over six fifty and they test you for Conduit compatibility."

"Your aunt also says the moon landing was filmed in Nevada."

"That's my other aunt." She threw a pretzel at him. "My dad got five ninety-eight, and he still brings it up."

Darius looked at Kade. "What about your dad?"

The question was ordinary. That was why it went in clean.

"He died when I was a baby."

Darius's face did a fast, private recalculation. Not pity. Kade would have known what to do with pity.

"Oh."

"Industrial accident."

"You know what he scored?"

"No."

He had never wondered. Aaron Mercer existed in the Mercer house as one photograph, a watch that had stopped, and a short list of stories his mother told carefully enough that Kade had quit asking for new ones years ago. A national test score had never been part of the list. Standing under the district's loop on the morning of his own test, the gap felt briefly strange, like a stair that was not there.

Then Darius held out the last bag, and Kade took a pretzel, and the subject was closed. That was the useful thing about food. Mostly it was handing you the exit.

Tessa looked at the hall clock. "You're going to be late."

Ten fifty-four. Kade gave the bag back.

"Try not to get expelled while I'm in there."

"No promises."

---

He went back to Room Four for his hoodie, and Ms. Alvarez was still at her desk with the wall display still showing two boxes.

"Kade."

He turned with the hoodie half on. "What."

"You left your notes on the water problem this time."

"Forgot to delete them."

"No, you didn't."

He got his other arm through the sleeve. She was leaning against the desk with her arms folded, looking at him the way she had looked at the room before she said his name, like the inventory had ended.

"You know what I think your problem is?"

"Is this the short version or the ranked list?"

"You decide something doesn't matter before you find out whether you're good at it."

He kept his smile where it was. "Efficient."

"No."

One word, and it landed harder than it had any right to. She nodded at the hall.

"Go take the test."

He turned to go.

"And Kade."

He looked back.

"Try."

He had a joke ready. He did not use it, and he could not have said why.

---

Multipurpose Two had been the county archive room once, and it had kept the archive's chill. The shelving was gone. In its place stood twenty testing stations walled off with folding privacy panels that never quite met at the corners, and a floor vent by the far wall that breathed cold air across the room in a slow, steady rhythm. Eleven Northline students took assigned seats while two district proctors checked cards against a list.

Kade had Station Seven. The tablet was bigger than the ones in Room Four, and beside it lay a black sensor band, thin enough to look harmless.

The proctor who stopped at his shoulder was a narrow man with a lanyard and a face that had spent a long career not being impressed by teenagers. "Temple contacts here and here. Rear contacts behind the ears. Don't take it off during a section unless you're told to."

"I've worn one."

"Then you'll be the easy part of my day."

He seated the band. The contacts were cool against his temples, and something under the skin registered them the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. The screen woke and told him who he was. NATIONAL APTITUDE ASCENSION EXAMINATION. Candidate Mercer, Kade A. Age seventeen. Identity verified. A button that said ACCEPT, and a countdown.

Five. Four. He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the desk, where somebody had carved half a name and given up. Three. Two. Try. One.

For the first hour the test was the small box, and he was good at the small box. Pattern recognition. Language. Mathematics. He moved fast, because he had always been good at tests when he could be bothered to finish them, and today he could be bothered, though he would not have admitted that under questioning. Most of it was arithmetic in a costume.

Then the fourth section loaded, and it was the big box, and the test stopped feeling like school.

A suspension bridge oscillated in a simulated crosswind, and he had to choose where to add damping. Easy. A municipal water network ruptured, and he had to isolate the failure while holding pressure to the hospital district, and he thought of his mother's floor at St. Vincent, of what a pressure drop meant at a scrub sink in the middle of a bad night, and did it properly. A transport grid lost three substations in the middle of an evacuation and gave him ninety seconds to reroute power and traffic at once.

He forgot to be bored. That annoyed him a little. He leaned toward the screen without noticing he had.

Three emergency routes converged on a bridge rated below the projected load, and the obvious move was to reroute traffic. Kade left the traffic alone. He shut down two commercial substations instead, blacking out several blocks to free enough capacity to run the rail evacuation line faster, and the simulation paused and gave him two lines to think about.

CIVILIAN IMPACT PENALTY, MINUS EIGHT POINT THREE PERCENT.

SURVIVAL PROJECTION, PLUS FOURTEEN POINT SEVEN PERCENT.

Somewhere in the dark blocks there were people on home oxygen and groceries going warm, and the test had put a number on them, and the number was smaller than the other number. He did not know whether that made him right. He knew it was the choice he would make again, and he noticed that the test had not told him which answer it wanted. It had only told him what his answer cost.

"Thanks," he told the screen. "That clears it up." He accepted the score.

Reaction timing came and went. Then a section called adaptive reasoning hid two sensors in a pressure network, changed a vehicle's mass after he committed, and docked him for choosing an assumption too early, and that one made him smile, because it was the first question all day that had admitted the problem might be the problem.

Then the seventh section loaded, and it had no title, and no box at all.

*Identify the boundary of the active system.*

A diagram. A steel sphere hung from a cable inside a transparent chamber. The chamber sat on a platform. The platform sat on a floor grid. Colored arrows showed where force went. Beyond the one line of instruction, nothing. No answer field. No notes field to argue in.

He tapped the sphere, and the tablet chimed and said INCOMPLETE. The chamber. INCOMPLETE. The platform. INCOMPLETE.

He sat back. The contacts at his temples had gone from cool to cold.

The drawing wanted him to believe the lines around the objects meant something. Sphere, cable, chamber, platform, floor, each in its own neat outline like the algebra books stacked on the cart. But the cable hung from the chamber's ceiling, and the chamber sat its weight on the platform, and the platform pushed into the floor, and the floor into a foundation the diagram had not bothered to draw, and if the sphere moved, every one of them would know about it the way the tape line had known about the books.

"That's not a system," he said under his breath. "That's a list."

He dragged a fingertip around the sphere, the cable, the chamber, and the platform together, and the selection glowed, and the tablet said INCOMPLETE. He stretched the line to take in the floor grid.

The screen flickered. Once.

He drew the same boundary again, and the screen flickered again, and this time the sphere, which had hung dead still since the section began, swung a few degrees on its cable and slowed, as though it had been a simulation all along and had only now been told to run. Not random. His pulse changed, and he felt it change.

He tried to push the boundary out past the floor grid. The interface would not let him. Then he saw it. The selection handle at the edge of the diagram had moved. Before, it snapped to the objects. Now it floated between them, attached to nothing.

He touched it, and the boundary went where he took it.

The room had gone quiet in a way he could not account for, as if the privacy panels had thickened. He drew a line diagonally through the platform, and the tablet accepted it. Up through the frame, keeping one support and leaving the other. Accepted. Across the chamber wall. Through the cable. Accepted, and accepted. He stopped once with his finger above the sphere, because the next line went through solid steel, where no line had any business being, and Ms. Alvarez said, from a long way off, answer it anyway. He drew the line around half the sphere.

The screen went black.

For one full second he saw nothing but his own face in the glass, the band a dark stripe across his forehead. Then a single word in white capitals, and nothing else.

REDEFINE.

"What," he said, too loud.

The word was gone. A new simulation loaded with no section header and one instruction. *Minimize occupant impulse.* A vehicle struck a barrier. He selected the crumple zones and got eighty-three point one percent. He reset and included the barrier. Ninety-one point four. He included a length of roadway. Ninety-six even. Then, on a hunch he could not have defended to anyone, he drew the boundary so the vehicle's rear axle sat outside the active transfer network, on the far side of the line, and the screen hesitated, and for half a second the numbers dissolved into static.

Ninety-nine point nine eight.

He stared at it. Behind his eyes, something had begun to press. Not pain. Not yet. More like the tight, bright feeling at the end of a night he had stayed up too far into, when the room started looking sharper than it was.

The next problem loaded before he touched anything. A pressure vessel: prevent rupture. He moved the boundary. A power grid: preserve critical load. He moved the boundary. A falling structure, a heat exchange, a charge across a gap, a fluid in a pipe with no diameter, a collision. He stopped reading the titles. They came faster, and at first he answered them, and then he was answering before they finished loading, and the interface seemed to be doing the same thing back. Every problem was one hidden question in different clothes. Where does this stop being one thing and start being another? And every time, his answer got simpler.

A thing ended where you quit counting it. Nowhere else.

The pressure behind his eyes sharpened to a point. He blinked hard. A timer in the corner read eighty-seven minutes and thirteen seconds, which could not be right, because the standard block was ninety and he had the strong, sourceless certainty that he had been in this chair longer than that. He looked for the proctor. The man was no longer at his list. He was standing very still, a few meters off. Not bored-still. Watching-still.

Kade looked back. The timer was gone.

A new problem. No diagram. A blank gray field and one line.

*Define the active system.*

Nothing else. No objects, no arrows, no lines to argue with. Nowhere to write down what the question had left out, because the question had left out everything.

His first instinct was to select the whole screen. He stopped his hand above the glass. What active system? The tablet? The station? The room? Him? He looked at his own hand, at the cable running from the tablet into the floor, at the proctor, at the band pressing its cold contacts to his skin. Something colder than the contacts walked down his spine and kept going.

He put two fingers on the gray and drew a boundary around nothing.

The tablet vibrated once, like a phone in a pocket.

SECTION COMPLETE.

Then the ordinary interface came back, and at the top of the screen one word turned over and over. SCORING.

He looked around. Multipurpose Two was nearly empty. One girl at the far end zipping her bag. One proctor by the door, the other three meters behind Station Seven, and Kade could not have said when the other nine students had stood up and gone. He had not heard a single chair.

"How long was I in that section?"

The near proctor looked at the wall clock, and then at him. "The section runs until it's finished."

Kade took the band off. His fingers were not quite steady doing it, and he made them be. A bead of sweat had gotten loose from his hairline and was making for his ear. He wiped it on his sleeve and looked at the turning word and realized, with real irritation, that he wanted the number before he had a joke ready for it.

Then a worse thought. If the number was good, he wanted to tell Elena. If it was terrible, he wanted to tell Darius first, so Darius could make it funny before anyone else got to it. The test had become real enough to have an order of operations for who got the truth.

That meant he cared. Annoying.

The door opened and Darius put his head in.

"You alive?"

"Provisionally."

"Food's gone. Alvarez sent me. She says you don't leave people in government testing rooms. It's a rule she has."

"She has a lot of rules."

Darius came the rest of the way in and stopped beside Station Seven and looked at the turning word. "What'd you get?"

"It's thinking about it."

"You think you're Resonant?"

"No."

"That was fast."

"I've had seventeen years to think about it."

"Nobody could live with you if you had powers."

"That's fair."

Darius laughed, and the turning word stopped.

The page was almost empty. No percentile, no section breakdown, no recommendations. Then three digits appeared in the center, alone, and stayed there.

000.

Darius went quiet.

Behind them the proctor took a sharp breath in through his nose. Kade heard it and set it aside for later.

Darius recovered first. "That's a lot of zero."

Kade nodded slowly. "It's consistent. You've got to give it that."

"Three of them."

"They didn't want me to miss one."

Darius barked a laugh, and Kade smiled, because that was what you did. You got to the joke first. If you were fast enough, nobody had to ask whether it hurt, and you never had to find out.

Three schools. Three suspensions. Forty-three days at Northline. And the machine the whole country used to find out what its seventeen-year-olds were worth had looked at him for two hours and found nothing to report.

The embarrassment came, not hard, just familiar. A little heat at the back of the neck. The old instinct came in right behind it, easy as breathing. Doesn't matter. He could hear himself saying it to Ms. Alvarez already. Stupid test. Bad software. He could make the whole morning irrelevant before he had to decide whether he had cared about it.

He looked at the three zeros.

You decide something doesn't matter before you find out whether you're good at it.

His smile went away. For once he had tried. That was the part he could not get around. He had left the notes in the field, and he had leaned toward the screen, and he had drawn a line through a steel ball because a test asked him to, and the number was still zero.

Behind him the proctor said, very quietly, "Don't touch the station."

Kade turned. "What?"

The vent stopped.

He noticed that first, before anything, because the far wall had been breathing at him for two hours and the breath just quit. Then the lights went. Not a flicker, not a dimming. Out, all at once. Every tablet in Multipurpose Two went black in the same instant, and Darius's laugh stopped like a door closing. For two full seconds the room was dark as a closet.

Then every testing screen in the room lit white with one line of black text.

SESSION TERMINATED.

The proctor's wrist terminal began to vibrate. Then the other proctor's. Then Darius's phone, and Kade's against his hip, all at once.

The wall display rebooted on its own. A district network map came up, Northline a single point on it, and a red indicator flashed over the point. Then another. Then five. The map zoomed out on its own, city, county, state, and Kade stopped breathing for a second, because the red was spreading across the western states the way infection spreads through a wound.

"What is that?" Darius said. Nobody answered him.

The map widened to the whole country. Testing centers went out one after another, and the labels under them did not say offline. They said terminated.

Kade's phone buzzed again. A government notice filled the screen. National Aptitude Service Interruption. Results temporarily unavailable.

He looked back at Station Seven.

It was the only screen in the room not showing SESSION TERMINATED. It still held his score. Three zeros, steady, white on black, as if they had not heard the news.

The proctor saw it too. Kade watched the color go out of the man's face, because watching people was easy when they were working that hard not to react. The proctor took one step backward. It was not the tablet he was stepping away from.

His wrist terminal buzzed again, and this time he answered at once.

"Northline testing center." A pause. "Yes." A longer one. His eyes came to Kade and stayed. "No. The candidate's still here. He's still at the station."

Kade's stomach went tight.

The proctor turned half away, as if privacy still applied to anything. "Mercer. Kade Mercer."

Silence on the line. Then the voice on the other end said something Kade could not hear, and the proctor's face changed. Kade had watched a lot of adults hear his name over the years. He knew what recognition looked like. He had never once seen it followed by fear.

"Yes, sir," the proctor said, and ended the call.

Nobody spoke. Kade reached for the joke, and there was nothing there.

Out in the corridor, doors were opening and teachers were calling students back into rooms. Somewhere near the front office Russell was arguing at full volume with someone about district emergency procedures, and losing. Inside Multipurpose Two, Station Seven glowed on.

Kade had spent most of his life being handed numbers that were supposed to measure him. Grades. Attendance. Suspensions. Risk. Potential. Not one of them had ever frightened the people doing the measuring.

He looked at the three zeros and, for the first time that day, let himself wonder what a machine had to see before it wrote down nothing.

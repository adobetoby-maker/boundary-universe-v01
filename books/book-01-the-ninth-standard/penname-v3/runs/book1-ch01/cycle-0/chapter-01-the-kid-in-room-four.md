# Chapter 1 — The Kid in Room Four

Kade Mercer had the answer forty seconds before he typed it. He spent the forty seconds arguing with the question.

*A cargo tram with a mass of eighteen thousand kilograms accelerates from rest to twelve meters per second over eight seconds. What average force is required?*

The arithmetic was nothing. Mass times the change in speed, divided by the time. Twenty-seven thousand newtons, a number so tidy it practically apologized for itself. The trouble was the last word. Required by what? A tram on steel rails paid a friction tax on every meter it moved. A tram on a magnetic guideway paid a different tax, in field geometry, and the district's software had not said which kind of tram it was. It had not said whether twelve meters per second was measured against the track or against the ground, or whether the track itself was moving, or whether eighteen thousand kilograms meant the cargo or the cargo plus the vehicle hauling it. It had drawn a cartoon tram with cartoon crates stacked in the bed and asked him to pretend the crates were welded down.

He typed the number the software wanted.

A green check. Then the animation, a small gold trophy that spun once and threw off sparkles. Kade had been at Northline Alternative for forty-three school days and had hated the trophy on every one of them.

Across the aisle, a chair scraped.

He did not look up. He did not need to check the clock, either, because Darius Bell was the clock. Every morning at ten seventeen, give or take the heater, Darius reached the end of what he was willing to learn and stood.

"Where are you going, Mr. Bell?"

Ms. Alvarez did not lift her eyes from the district tablet in her hands. Her coffee sat at her elbow, going cold the way it did every morning.

"Checking on the machine."

"The vending machine is not on your schedule."

"It's not a schedule thing. It's a relationship."

"Then nurture it at lunch."

Darius was already moving. Kade watched him go with the part of his attention that could not stop counting, the part that had counted the strides from Darius's desk to the door on the first day and never needed to again. Four strides. Hand on the push bar. And Kade, who had told himself at least twice this week that Darius Bell's decisions were Darius Bell's business, heard himself say, "Four."

Darius stopped. "Four what?"

"Exits. Unexcused. Your contract says five, and then Russell bumps you back to review."

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything. I said a number."

"You've got the face on."

"Which face?"

"The one where you've already worked out what I'm going to do and you're bored about it."

"I'm bored about everything," Kade said. "You're not special."

Somebody in the back laughed. Darius did not. He was sixteen, two inches taller than Kade, with the shoulders of a kid who had been mistaken for a grown man since he was twelve. He had come to Northline from Canyon Ridge High after he put another student through the glass panel beside a classroom door. The other kid had walked away with eleven stitches. Darius had walked away with a transfer. He had never told Kade the whole story, and Kade had never asked, which was one of the reasons they got along. Kade had learned at Northline that a file and a person were two different documents, and that people who had only read the file tended to get Darius wrong.

"Review's Friday," Kade said, quieter. "Your mom traded a shift for Friday."

That landed. Kade knew about the shift because Darius had spent most of yesterday afternoon pretending not to care whether his mother could get the time off, which was the loudest way Darius knew how to care about anything.

"You hit five today, Russell pushes the meeting a week," Kade went on. "She loses the shift for nothing and has to trade another one. Walk out at lunch instead. Nobody logs lunch. Call it a protest."

Darius looked toward the front of the room. Ms. Alvarez was studying her tablet with the total concentration of a woman who had not heard a word.

His hand came off the push bar. He came back down the aisle and dropped into his chair hard enough to make the desk legs complain.

"Still hate you," he said.

"Noted."

Ms. Alvarez lifted her coffee, found it cold, and drank it anyway. "Since Mr. Bell has chosen to remain enrolled," she said, "we're going to use him."

"I was enrolled the whole time."

"Your chair was."

She touched her screen, and the tram problem bloomed across the wall display, the cartoon tram now the size of a real golf cart. Three people groaned. In the back row, the kid who stocked warehouse shelves until two every morning opened one eye, judged the situation, and closed it again. The two girls by the window, who did every problem together and refused to be separated, pulled their desks closer as if the tram might try something. Kade looked down at his own tablet, where the trophy had finished its lap and vanished, and felt the small, familiar dread of a teacher about to make an easy thing complicated.

"Who got twenty-seven thousand newtons?"

Most of the hands in the room went up.

"Who got something else?"

Darius raised his hand halfway. "I got a red box."

"A red box is not a unit of force."

"It's what I got."

She waited. Darius sighed and turned his tablet toward her. He had done the multiplication right and the division right and then entered the answer as twenty-seven hundred.

"You're missing a zero," Kade said.

"I'm missing a lot of things."

"Fix the number first," Ms. Alvarez said. "The institution will still be here to blame afterward."

While Darius fixed it, she typed a second line beneath the problem, and the wall display printed it large. *What information is missing?*

The hands came down.

Kade kept his thumb still on the edge of his tablet. He knew where this was going, and he knew who it was going to. Ms. Alvarez looked at a room like she was taking inventory, and the inventory always ended at him.

"Mercer."

"The software says I'm right."

"The software also throws confetti for arithmetic. I'm asking you."

The heater under the windows kicked on with a shudder. Above the door, the clock's second hand stuck, considered its options, and went on. The clock and the heater shared a circuit and a grudge; Kade had stopped trusting either of them in his first week.

"Friction," he said.

She wrote it on the display.

"What else?"

"Track grade. Drive efficiency. Whether twelve meters per second is relative to the track. Whether the cargo shifts under acceleration." The words were coming faster than he wanted them to. That was the trouble with a list. Once it started, it had its own momentum. "Wheel condition. Wind, if it's outdoors. Peak force versus average force. Whether the tram drives itself or gets pulled. Whether the eighteen thousand includes the tram."

The list grew down the wall one line at a time in her neat block capitals. Kade wished she would write smaller.

Darius squinted at the cartoon. "That thing on wheels?"

"It has something pretending to be wheels."

"Then the load's going to move." Darius pointed at the stacked crates. "You hit speed too fast, all that comes forward. You ever stand on a bus when the driver stomps it? Everything in the aisle ends up at the front. Boxes don't care about your equation."

Kade turned in his seat to look at him.

Darius shrugged, as if he had said something about the weather.

Ms. Alvarez wrote LOAD SECUREMENT at the bottom of the list. Darius sat a little straighter and pretended he had not.

Kade looked back at the display. The problem had annoyed him because it ignored everything that could make the answer useful, and Darius had found one of those things without touching the equation at all. He had found it by standing on a bus.

Ms. Alvarez drew a box around twenty-seven thousand.

"This is the answer the question asks for. Under the simplification the question states, it's correct. Nobody is going to take it away from you." Then she drew a bigger box around the list. "This is what keeps somebody from loading a real tram badly and blaming the math when it fails."

One of the girls by the window said, "Which box is on the test?"

"The small one."

"Then why do we need the big one?"

Ms. Alvarez capped her stylus. "Because the small box gets you through the test, and the big box is the part where nobody gets hurt. The district pays me to teach the first one. I would rather you leave this building with both."

Kade stared at the two boxes. Schools were not supposed to say that part out loud. He filed it with the short list of things Ms. Alvarez had said that he had not yet found a way to argue with.

Darius tapped the missing-information list on his own screen. "So he did the whole big box in his head and then typed the small one like the rest of us."

"Yes," Ms. Alvarez said.

"That's the most Mercer thing I ever heard."

"It is occasionally exhausting to watch."

"I'm sitting right here," Kade said.

"Your chair is," said Darius.

Ms. Alvarez assigned the next problem. A water tank, an outlet valve, a flow rate that assumed water behaved politely. Kade entered the number they wanted, and then, before the trophy could finish its lap, he opened the notes field under the answer and typed what the question had not said. No tank height. No outlet diameter. Nothing about whether the flow held steady as the level dropped.

He did not look toward the front of the room. Ms. Alvarez did not say anything. He could feel her not saying it.

Darius leaned across the gap between their desks. "Who are you writing to?"

"Nobody."

"You're writing a complaint to nobody."

"I'm writing down what they left out."

Darius considered the water-stained ceiling tile above the heater. "If it's Northline water, the pipe's clogged."

Kade added PIPE CONDITION.

Darius nodded like the matter was settled and went back to his own screen. Kade looked at the little list under his answer. For once, he was in no hurry to close the problem.

---

Northline Alternative lived in an old county records building between a tire warehouse and a self-storage yard. Somebody had painted it blue three years ago. The desert had since worked the south wall down to the gray of an old bandage. The district website called it a personalized transitional learning campus. Students called it Northline. Parents called it whatever they had to call it when relatives asked where their kid went to school now.

Forty-two students, six classrooms. Some had been expelled. Some had been arrested. Some had simply stopped showing up to regular school until the district stopped pretending they would come back. There were panic disorders and probation officers, night jobs and pregnancies, complicated families, addictions, and combinations Kade had quit trying to sort. Northline had no mascot. It had a metal detector at the front door, two social workers who shared one office, a security officer who spent most afternoons fixing bicycles, and a vending machine that sometimes released three bags of pretzels for the price of one. Nobody knew why. Darius believed the machine respected him.

Kade had none of the obvious reasons for being there, which was, according to Vice Principal Russell, part of his problem.

By seventeen he had collected three suspensions, two school transfers, one semester of failed classes he could have passed in his sleep, and enough teacher comments to build a personality out of administrative adjectives. Bright but inconsistent. Argumentative. Does not utilize available opportunities. His favorite was from ninth grade: Kade often appears disengaged even when he understands the material. As if understanding it should have made pretending to enjoy it easier.

He had read the whole file once, cover to cover, while Russell was out of the office making a phone call. He had not hacked anything. Russell had left the folder open on the desk, which in Kade's opinion turned unauthorized access into a failure of information security. Ms. Alvarez had disagreed. They had argued about it for eleven minutes. Kade had enjoyed the argument. She had not. Probably.

---

At ten thirty-one the classroom speaker clicked, and every head in Room Four came up, because Northline's speakers worked only when nobody wanted them to.

"Attention, students," said Russell's voice. "All seventeen-year-old students scheduled for the National Aptitude Ascension Examination will report to Multipurpose Two at eleven hundred hours. Bring your district identification card. Testing begins promptly at eleven fifteen."

Darius groaned.

"You're sixteen," Kade said.

"I'm groaning for you."

"That's thoughtful."

"I've been working on it."

The speaker clicked off. Ms. Alvarez set down her tablet. "Mercer. Do you have your ID?"

"Somewhere."

"Make it a pocket."

He patted the front of his hoodie and felt the card's stiff corner. "Present."

"A miracle."

"You've been managing your expectations. It's healthy."

A couple of people laughed. Ms. Alvarez did not, which was unusual enough that Kade looked at her properly.

"You nervous?" he asked.

"For you?"

"For the integrity of national testing."

"No." She picked up her coffee and did not drink from it. "I am concerned you will decide the instructions on question twelve are philosophically inconsistent and decline to answer it."

"That happened once."

"Twice."

"The second set really was inconsistent."

The corner of her mouth moved. He counted it as a win.

"Just take the test," she said.

He shrugged, because shrugging was free.

The Aptitude Ascension Examination was older than he was. Every seventeen-year-old in the country sat some version of it, and the official explanation was that it measured academic reasoning, spatial cognition, reaction speed, systems intuition, stress adaptation, and a dozen other qualities useful to universities, technical apprenticeships, military programs, and Resonance screening.

Resonance screening was the part people cared about.

Thirty-one years after Resonance entered the world, civilization had rearranged itself around the small fraction of human beings who could do things the rest could only watch. They held up failing bridges while crews got clear. They caught aircraft that were coming down wrong and put them down right. They built in orbit and stood on seawalls during storms, and they fought in arenas in front of hundreds of millions of people who had paid to see it, carrying Conduits that cost more than Northline's whole building, metal detector included. Every year a few teenagers walked into a testing room as ordinary kids and walked out with a number that changed their lives.

Kade did not expect to be one of them. A great many institutions had explained to him over the years exactly what he was capable of. The reviews were mixed.

A chime sounded at the teacher's station. Ms. Alvarez read the message and looked around the room.

"Ten-minute break before testing. Stay on campus. Nobody set anything on fire."

Darius raised his hand. "Clarification."

"No."

"You didn't hear the question."

"I've heard all your questions."

Chairs scraped. Northline breaks ran on rules nobody had written down. Students with jobs checked their schedules. Students with probation officers checked their messages. People who wanted to be alone went behind the old records annex, where the cameras covered the door and not the wall. Everyone else drifted toward the vending machine.

Darius caught Kade's sleeve. "Come watch me get chosen."

"I have a test."

"In twenty minutes."

"Exactly."

"The machine knows about the test. That's the whole point."

Kade allowed himself to be towed into the hall.

Mr. Boyd sat behind the security desk with the front wheel of a bicycle in his lap and a brake cable between his teeth. The bike belonged to Miguel, a fourteen-year-old whose family had no car and whose rear brake had quit on the ride in that morning. Boyd took the cable out of his mouth as they passed.

"Testing day."

Kade nodded.

"Don't start a war with the government before lunch."

"No promises."

Boyd went back to the cable. Northline had more adults doing things outside their job descriptions than any school Kade had attended. Sometimes that meant nobody knew who was responsible for anything. Sometimes it meant a security officer fixed a kid's brakes so the kid could get home. Kade had not decided which of those was the honest version of the place.

The vending machine hummed in its alcove by the multipurpose hallway. Darius approached it with the gravity of a man entering a chapel.

"One bag," he told it. "That's all I'm asking."

"You talk to it like it can hear you."

"It can hear respect."

He fed in two credits and pressed B-seven. The coil turned. One bag of pretzels dropped, and Darius's whole face fell in on itself.

"It hates me," he said.

The coil kept turning. A second bag fell. Then a third.

Darius pointed at Kade without taking his eyes off the machine. "Bear witness."

"I take back everything I've ever said about you."

Tessa arrived from nowhere and took one of the bags before Darius could close his hand over it. She had a talent for being near things when they happened.

"Hey," Darius said. "Those are mine."

"It gave three. Three is a share."

"It gave three to me."

"The machine believes in redistribution."

"The machine believes in me."

"You don't know what redistribution means."

"I know I have fewer pretzels."

Kade did not take one. His stomach had started doing something he declined to call nerves.

Tessa tore her bag open. "What do you think you'll get?"

"On the test? Somewhere between zero and whatever the maximum is."

"That's confident."

"I like to leave room for outcomes."

"My cousin got seven twelve last year."

"Is that good?"

She stared at him until he gave up. Everybody knew the thresholds. Above seven hundred, scholarship offices called your house. Eight hundred brought recruiters. Nine hundred got you on the local news. The highest score ever recorded was supposedly nine sixty-eight, though people argued online about whether the scoring had changed since then. Northline students did not, as a rule, contribute to that argument.

Tessa lowered her voice like the machine might be listening. "My aunt says anything over six fifty and they test you for Conduit compatibility."

"Your aunt also says the moon landing was filmed in Nevada."

"That's my other aunt."

"You have a complicated family."

She threw a pretzel at him. He caught it, which surprised them both.

Darius, chewing: "What'd your mom get?"

Kade frowned. "How would I know?"

"Parents tell you that stuff."

"Your mother tells you her standardized test scores?"

"She tells me everything I do is genetic when she's mad."

"My dad got five ninety-eight," Tessa said, "and he still brings it up."

"That explains your family better than the moon thing."

Darius looked back at Kade. "What about your dad?"

The question was ordinary. That was why it went in so clean.

Kade shrugged. "He died when I was a baby."

Darius's face did a fast, private recalculation. It was not pity. Kade would have known what to do with pity.

"Oh."

"Industrial accident."

"You know what he scored?"

"No."

He had never wondered. Aaron Mercer existed in the Mercer house as two photographs, a watch that had stopped, and a short list of stories his mother told carefully enough that Kade had quit asking for new ones years ago. A national test score had never been part of the story. Standing outside Multipurpose Two on the morning of his own test, the absence felt briefly strange, like a stair that was not there.

Then Darius held out the last bag, and Kade took a pretzel, and the subject was closed. That was the useful thing about food. People thought they were sharing a snack. Mostly they were handing you the exit.

Tessa checked the hall clock. "You're going to be late."

Ten fifty-four. Kade gave the bag back to Darius.

"Try not to get expelled while I'm in there."

"No promises."

---

Ms. Alvarez stopped him at the classroom door when he went back for his hoodie. The others were already filing past toward Multipurpose Two.

"Kade."

He turned. She was leaning against the teacher's desk with her arms folded and her coffee forgotten behind her.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"People don't usually say my name like that for nothing."

She studied him a second longer than was comfortable. "You know what I think your problem is?"

"Is this the short version or the ranked list?"

"You decide something doesn't matter before you find out whether you're good at it."

He kept his smile where it was. "Efficient."

"No."

Just the one word, and it landed harder than it should have. She nodded toward the hall.

"Go take the test."

He turned to go.

"And Kade."

He looked back.

"Try."

He had a joke ready. He did not use it, and he could not have said why.

---

Multipurpose Two had been the county archive room once. The shelving was gone, and in its place stood twenty testing stations walled off with folding privacy panels, the kind that never quite met at the corners. Eleven Northline students took their assigned seats while two district proctors checked ID cards against a list.

Kade had Station Seven. The testing tablet was bigger than the ones in Room Four, and beside it lay a black sensor band, thin enough to look harmless and expensive enough that he decided not to ask what happened if you dropped one.

A proctor stopped at his shoulder, a narrow man with a lanyard and a face that had spent a long time not being impressed by teenagers.

"Temple contacts here and here," he said, tapping the band. "Rear contacts behind the ears. Do not remove it during a section unless instructed."

"I've worn one."

"Then this will be the easy part of my day."

Kade looked up at him. The proctor looked back with no expression at all. Kade decided, provisionally, that he liked him.

He seated the band. The contacts were cool against his temples, and something under the skin registered them the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. The screen woke.

NATIONAL APTITUDE ASCENSION EXAMINATION. Candidate: Mercer, Kade A. Age: seventeen. Identity verified.

Below that, legal text. Kade scrolled. He scrolled some more. The text kept going, like it had been written by people paid by the paragraph.

"Does anyone read this?"

The proctor did not look up from his list. "Nobody in the history of the program."

"Comforting."

"It was written to be scrolled."

Kade pressed ACCEPT. A countdown appeared. Five. Four. Three. He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the desk, where somebody had carved half a name and given up. Two. Ms. Alvarez's voice arrived in his head without being invited. Try. One.

The first sections were ordinary. Pattern recognition, language, mathematics. Kade moved fast. He had always been good at tests when he could be bothered to finish them, and today he could be bothered, though he would not have admitted it under questioning. Sentence relationships. Shapes that rotated. Conditional logic. Probability. A few of the questions were hard enough to be interesting. Most were not.

Section four changed the rules. A suspension bridge oscillated in a simulated crosswind, and he had to choose where to add damping. Easy. A municipal water network ruptured, and he had to isolate the failure while holding pressure to the hospital district. He thought of his mother's floor at St. Vincent, of what a pressure drop would mean at a scrub sink in the middle of a bad night, and did it properly. A transport grid lost three substations in the middle of an evacuation and gave him ninety seconds to reroute power and traffic at once.

He forgot to be bored. That annoyed him a little.

The questions had stopped feeling like school. For once, a test was asking what he actually saw when he looked at a problem, not whether he could remember an equation. What fails first. What can be sacrificed. What looks important and is not. Where the load is really going. He leaned toward the screen without noticing that he had.

In one scenario, three emergency routes converged on a bridge rated below the projected load. The obvious move was to reroute the traffic. Kade left the traffic alone and shut down two commercial substations instead, blacking out several blocks to free enough grid capacity to run the rail evacuation line faster. The simulation paused. Two lines appeared.

CIVILIAN IMPACT PENALTY: MINUS 8.3 PERCENT.

SURVIVAL PROJECTION: PLUS 14.7 PERCENT.

He looked at them for a while. Somewhere in the dark blocks there were people on home oxygen and people with groceries going warm, and the test had put a number on them, and the number was smaller than the other number. He did not know whether that made him right. He knew it was the choice he would make again.

"Thanks," he told the screen. "That clears it up."

He accepted the score.

Section five was reaction timing. Section six was called adaptive reasoning, and it was where the test got strange. Questions stopped giving him everything at once. A collision problem changed the mass of one vehicle after he committed to an answer. A pressure network hid two of its sensors. A structural problem handed him three plausible assumptions and docked him for picking any of them too early.

That last one made him smile. Finally. A test that admitted the problem might be the problem.

Then section seven loaded, and it had no title.

*Identify the boundary of the active system.*

A diagram. A steel sphere hung from a cable inside a transparent chamber. The chamber sat on a platform. The platform sat on a floor grid. Colored arrows showed force vectors, and beyond the instruction, nothing. No question. No answer field.

He waited. Nothing happened.

He tapped the sphere. The tablet chimed. *Incomplete.* He tapped the chamber. *Incomplete.* The platform. *Incomplete.*

Kade sat back. The contacts at his temples felt colder than they had.

The drawing wanted him to believe the lines around the objects meant something. Sphere, cable, chamber, platform, floor. Clean boxes. But the cable hung from the chamber's ceiling, and the chamber sat its weight on the platform, and the platform pushed into the floor, and the floor into a foundation the diagram had not bothered to draw, and if the sphere moved, every one of them would feel it.

"That's not a system," he said under his breath. "That's a list."

The proctor glanced over. Kade did not look up.

He dragged a fingertip around the sphere, the cable, the chamber, the platform. The selection glowed. *Incomplete.* He stretched it to take in the floor grid.

The screen flickered. Once.

He took his finger away. The diagram settled. He drew the same boundary again, and the screen flickered again, and this time the sphere, which had hung dead still since the section began, swung a few degrees on its cable and slowed, as if it had been a simulation all along and had only now been told to run. That was not random. His pulse changed, and he felt it change.

He tried to push the boundary out past the floor grid. The interface would not let him. He tried again. Nothing.

Then he saw it. The selection handle at the edge of the diagram had moved. Before, it snapped to the objects. Now it floated between them, attached to nothing.

He touched it. The boundary went where he took it. Anywhere.

Kade sat up straight. The room had gone quiet in a way he could not account for.

He drew a line diagonally through the platform, and the screen accepted it. Up through the frame, keeping one support and leaving the other, and the screen accepted that. Across the chamber wall. Through the cable. Accepted. Accepted.

He drew a line around half the sphere.

The screen went black.

For one full second he saw nothing but his own face in the glass, the band a dark line across his forehead. Then a single word appeared in white capitals, and nothing else.

REDEFINE.

"What?" he said. Too loud.

The word vanished. A new simulation loaded without a section header, with one instruction. *Minimize occupant impulse.* A vehicle struck a barrier. Kade selected the crumple zones, and the readout gave him eighty-three point one percent. He reset and included the barrier. Ninety-one point four. He included a length of roadway. Ninety-six even. Then, on a hunch he could not have defended, he drew the boundary so that the vehicle's rear axle sat outside the active transfer network, on the far side of the line.

The screen hesitated. For half a second the numbers dissolved into static.

Ninety-nine point nine eight.

Kade stared at it. Behind his eyes, something had begun to press. Not pain. Not yet. More like the tight, bright feeling at the end of a night he had stayed up too far into, when the room started looking sharper than it really was.

The next problem loaded before he touched anything. A pressure vessel: prevent rupture. He moved the boundary. A power grid: preserve critical load. Boundary. A falling structure: minimize transferred impulse. Boundary. Heat. Charge. Fluid. Collisions. The problems came faster, and at first he answered them, and then he started answering before they finished loading, and the interface seemed to be doing the same thing back to him. Every problem asked the same hidden question wearing different clothes. Where does this stop being one thing and start being another? And every time, his answer got simpler.

A thing ended where you quit counting it, and nowhere else.

The pressure behind his eyes sharpened into a point. He blinked hard. A timer in the corner read eighty-seven minutes and thirteen seconds, which could not be right. The standard block was ninety minutes, and he had the strong, sourceless feeling that he had been in this chair for longer than that. He looked toward the proctor. The man was standing very still. Not bored-still. Watching-still.

Kade looked back at the screen. The timer was gone.

A new problem. No diagram this time, only a blank gray field and one line of text.

*Define the active system.*

Nothing else appeared. No objects, no vectors, no lines to argue with.

"Seriously," he whispered.

His first instinct was to select the whole screen. He stopped his hand. What active system? The tablet? The station? The room? Him? He looked at his own hand resting on the desk. At the cable running from the tablet down into the floor. At the privacy panels and the proctor and the band pressing its cool contacts to his skin. Something cold walked down his spine and kept going.

He put two fingers on the gray and drew a boundary around nothing.

The tablet vibrated once, like a phone in a pocket.

SECTION COMPLETE.

He sat there. After a while the ordinary testing interface returned as if none of it had happened, and at the top of the screen one word turned over and over.

SCORING.

He looked around. Multipurpose Two was nearly empty. One girl at the far end was zipping her bag. One proctor stood by the door. The other stood three meters behind Station Seven with his hands at his sides, and Kade could not have said when either of them had moved, or when the other nine students had gotten up and gone. He had not noticed a single chair.

"How long was I in that section?" he asked.

The near proctor looked at the wall clock, then at him. "The section runs until it's finished."

"That sounds like a policy."

The man did not smile.

Kade took off the band. His skin was cool where the contacts had been, and a bead of sweat had gotten loose from his hairline and was making its way toward his ear. He wiped it with his sleeve, looked at the turning word, and realized, with real irritation, that he wanted the number before he had a joke ready for it.

He thought of Ms. Alvarez saying try. Of Tessa's cousin and seven twelve. Of his mother at St. Vincent, moving from one emergency to the next without a thought in her head about national aptitude scores. And of the question from the vending machine alcove, which had no business following him in here. What did your dad score. He still did not know. It was a stupid thing to think about now.

Then a more uncomfortable thought. If the number was good, he wanted to tell Elena. If it was terrible, he wanted to tell Darius first, so Darius could make it funny before anyone else got to it. The test had become real enough to have an order of operations for who got the truth.

That meant he cared. Annoying.

The door opened and Darius put his head in.

"You alive?"

"Provisionally."

"Food's gone."

"Of course it is."

"Alvarez sent me. She says leaving people in government testing rooms is frowned on."

"She's a tyrant."

Darius came the rest of the way in. The proctor by the door opened his mouth, and Darius pointed back over his shoulder without breaking stride. "She said." The proctor apparently knew Ms. Alvarez well enough to accept that as paperwork.

Darius stopped beside Station Seven. "What'd you get?"

"It's still scoring."

"You think you're Resonant?"

"No."

"That was fast."

"I've had seventeen years to think about it."

"Nobody could live with you if you had powers."

"That's fair."

"You'd catch a bullet and then critique where everybody was standing."

"Your posture first."

Darius laughed, and the turning word stopped.

Kade looked back at the screen. For a moment he thought the page had failed to load. It was almost empty. No percentile, no section breakdown, no recommendations. Then three digits appeared in the center, alone, and stayed there.

000.

Darius went quiet.

Behind them, the proctor took a sharp breath through his nose. Kade heard it and set it aside for later. That was interesting.

Darius recovered first. "Wow."

Kade nodded slowly.

"You have to admit it's clean."

"You got zero."

"I noticed."

"Like zero zero zero."

"It's three zeros. Somebody wanted to be thorough."

Darius barked a laugh, and Kade smiled, because that was what you did. You got to the joke first. If you were fast enough, nobody had to ask whether it hurt, and you never had to find out.

Three schools. Three suspensions. A semester he could have passed. Forty-three days at Northline. And the machine the whole country used to find out what its seventeen-year-olds were worth had looked at him for ninety minutes and found nothing to report.

He waited for the embarrassment, and it came, not hard, just familiar. A little pressure under the ribs. A little heat at the back of the neck. The old instinct came in right behind it, easy as breathing. Doesn't matter. He could hear himself saying it to Ms. Alvarez already. Stupid test. Bad software. Government scoring. He could make the whole morning irrelevant before he had to decide whether he had cared about it.

He looked at the three zeros.

You decide something doesn't matter before you find out whether you're good at it.

His smile went away. For once, he had tried. That was the part he could not get around. He had actually tried, and he was not going to be able to pretend otherwise, not to her and not to himself, and the number was still zero.

Behind him the proctor said, very quietly, "Don't touch the station."

Kade turned. "What?"

The lights went out.

Not a flicker, not a dimming. Out, all at once. The ventilation died mid-breath. Every tablet in Multipurpose Two went black in the same instant, and Darius's laugh stopped like a door closing. The emergency lights should have come on. They did not. For two full seconds the room was as dark as a closet, and somewhere in it Kade heard someone pull in a breath and hold it.

Then every testing screen in the room lit white at once. No logos, no controls. One line of black text.

SESSION TERMINATED.

The proctor said something sharp under his breath. His wrist terminal began to vibrate. Then the other proctor's. Then Darius's phone, and Kade's, buzzing against his hip. In the hallway someone shouted.

The wall display rebooted on its own. A district network map came up, Northline a single point on it, and one red indicator flashed over the point. Then another. Then five. The map zoomed out without anyone touching it, to the city, to the county, to the state. It paused. Then it pulled back again, and Kade stopped breathing for a second, because the red was spreading across the western states the way infection spreads through a wound, one center after another going dark.

The proctor crossed to the wall.

"What is that?" Darius said. Nobody answered him. His phone was already up at arm's length, the way it always was when something happened.

The map widened to the whole country. Testing centers went out one after another, and the labels under them did not say offline. They said terminated.

Kade's phone buzzed again. A government testing notice filled the screen. National Aptitude Service Interruption. Results temporarily unavailable.

He looked back at Station Seven.

It was the only screen in the room not showing SESSION TERMINATED. It still held his score. Three zeros, steady, white on black, as if they had not heard the news.

The proctor saw it too. Kade watched the color go out of the man's face, because watching people was easy when they were working that hard not to react. The proctor took one step backward. It was not the tablet he was stepping away from.

Darius saw it. "What?" he said.

The proctor looked toward the door, and his wrist terminal buzzed again, and this time he answered it at once.

"Northline testing center." A pause. "Yes." A longer one. His eyes came to Kade and stayed there. "No. The candidate's still here. He's still at the station."

Kade's stomach went tight.

The proctor turned half away, as if privacy still applied to anything. "Mercer. First name Kade."

Silence on the line. Then the voice on the other end said something Kade could not hear, and the proctor's face changed. Kade had watched a lot of adults hear his name over the years. He knew what recognition looked like. He had never once seen it followed by fear.

"Yes, sir," the proctor said, and ended the call.

Nobody spoke. Kade looked from the man to the screen. Three zeros. He reached for the joke, and there was nothing there.

Out in the hall, doors were opening up and down the corridor. Teachers called students back into rooms. Phones chimed over each other. Somewhere near the front office Russell was arguing at full volume with someone about district emergency procedures, and losing. Inside Multipurpose Two, Station Seven glowed on.

Kade had spent most of his life being handed numbers that were supposed to measure him. Grades. Attendance. Suspensions. Risk. Potential. Not one of them had ever scared the people doing the measuring.

He looked at the three zeros, and for the first time that day he did not assume they meant nothing.

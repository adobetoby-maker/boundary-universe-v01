# Chapter 22 — Midyear: Mercer v. Vey

The quarterfinal was set for ten past six, which gave House Meridian ninety-three minutes to make it worse.

Eli built a comparison model. Sera confiscated it. Taren wheeled a portable board into the prep room and opened a betting pool, learned from a passing upper-year that wagering was an expellable offense, and renamed it a prediction exercise without changing a single column.

There were four columns. MERCER SCORES FIRST. VEY SCORES FIRST. MEDICAL INTERRUPTION. STRUCTURAL DAMAGE. The last one had three times as many marks under it as any other.

Kade pointed. "What do those people win?"

"The satisfaction of being correct."

"You've invented gambling with nothing to gamble."

Taren capped his marker. "I've invented forecasting."

Sera crossed the room, wiped out MEDICAL INTERRUPTION with the side of her fist, and wrote BOTH CLEARED WITHOUT HOLD in its place. "Forecast something useful."

Taren put his mark under the new heading. Kade added his own, and then one tick under VEY SCORES FIRST, because Hale had spent the morning teaching him to say in advance what he expected.

"Disloyal," Eli said.

"Calibrated."

On the far wall the spectator feed scrolled through the people Meridian students had registered for remote access. Parents, mostly. Old coaches in time zones the arena clock listed in smaller type. Two names stopped him. ELENA MERCER, SAINT VINCENT MEDICAL. DARIUS BELL, NORTHLINE. Both had a green light beside them, which meant both had the feed open.

His mother had told him four times today that she was not watching. Apparently that was a flexible medical term.

His band buzzed before he could decide whether to call her on it. Darius. VEY LOOKS RELAXED. YOU LOOK LIKE YOU ATE A BATTERY.

THE CAMERA ADDS VOLTAGE, Kade sent back.

ALVAREZ SAYS KEEP YOUR LEFT FOOT UNDER YOU.

So Ms. Alvarez was watching too, and had not registered her name. He turned his wrist over before Northline could start coaching him by committee.

Priya had declined to mark the board at all. "Mara's going to take him apart," she said, around a mouthful of somebody's crackers, "and I don't need a chart to enjoy it."

"You're supposed to be my friend."

"I put you on the floor the first week I knew you."

"That was an assessment."

"It was a lovely assessment." She had gone out in the third round that afternoon, by a single point. The blue thread was still knotted on her jacket.

Anton, passing through with a crate of ice packs, predicted that Kade would score two. Nessa, from the darkest corner of the room with her glasses on, said one. Eli, having stolen his model back, said one point seven.

"That isn't how points work."

"It's how expected value works."

"I predict he survives," Sera said.

"Thank you."

"I'm setting goals we can reach."

Across the room Mara stretched in silence, and nobody offered her any advice. That was partly because rank seven needed less of it than Kade did. Mostly it was because Mara Vey had a way of making unrequested advice feel like a filing error.

Priya did carry her water bottle over when she finished, and set it down within reach on her right side and said nothing about it. Mara nodded once. Kade had lived in Meridian long enough to read the exchange. Help did not always come as advice. Sometimes it was a person remembering which shoulder had taken a hard contact in the last round and putting the bottle on the other side.

Mara had been icing that shoulder twenty minutes ago. The wrap was still lying on the bench. Now she rolled the joint once, testing it. No wince. Nothing caught.

Kade wrote LEFT SHOULDER on the back of his bracket sheet. Then he crossed it out. She would know he had seen it, and she would expect him to test it, and if she had left the wrap lying where he could see it she might very well want him to. He wrote a second line under the first and crossed that out as well.

Eli leaned over. "Your plan currently consists of two deletions."

"It's evolving."

"Toward blank paper."

"The purest strategy."

"Your pencil gets louder when you're confused," Mara said, without opening her eyes.

"That isn't physically possible."

"And yet."

The thread on the back of his right hand had worked loose against Bisset. He went to pull the knot tight with his teeth, and Sera caught his wrist first. "Hold still." She retied it the way she would have secured a splint, fast and flat, with the ends tucked under.

"House support is getting aggressive."

"You make ordinary care require escalation."

Across the room Mara checked the blue at the seal of her left glove. For the next hour they were opponents. Neither of them had taken Meridian off. His forearm, the one Bisset had worked on all through the fourth round, had gone the color of a plum, and he watched Mara's eyes open and find the bruise and move on, and knew it had been entered in a ledger somewhere.

He looked at her about three seconds longer than the bruise required. She turned her head.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Sera made a small noise in her throat. Kade looked at her.

"Careful," she said, which was his own word, handed back to him.

He left before the room became unmanageable.

---

Julian was in the warmup corridor outside the main arena, working slow footwork against nobody. He did not stop when he saw Kade.

"Vey."

"Apparently."

"Do you have a plan?"

"Several."

"Good."

"You sound as though you're responsible for that."

"I'm taking the credit." Forward on the angle, recover. It was the same sequence Kade did, with all the corners sanded off.

Kade leaned on the wall. "How do I beat her?"

"I'm not telling you."

"Excellent mentorship."

"If she beats you, she's on my side of the bracket. Anything I give you, I pay for at eight o'clock."

"Selfishness. Finally. I was starting to worry."

"Also, if I tell you how I'd fight her, you'll try to fight her like me, and you'll be bad at it." Julian reversed without crossing his feet. "Vey knows your habits better than anyone alive. Possibly excepting Navarro."

"Eli knows my data. Mara knows my habits."

"Exactly."

"That sounds worse."

"It is."

Kade waited. Julian sighed, and his feet stopped.

"One thing."

"I knew friendship would win."

"It didn't. I became irritated."

"Similar mechanism."

Julian looked straight at him. "Don't confuse having a plan with refusing to abandon one."

"You spent three months telling me to commit to plans."

"I did."

"And now—"

"Now I'm telling you a plan is a tool. It isn't a vow. You have a habit, Mercer, of turning whatever you learned last into a point of honor." He went back to the footwork. "Welcome to the next problem."

Kade hated him, a little. "Good luck tonight."

"You too."

Julian's head came round. "Don't say that as if you expect to meet me."

"That sounded threatened."

"Go and warm up."

---

The competitors' tunnel split into two lanes with a wall of glass between them, so that each fighter could watch the other while the technicians did the final scans. Kade rolled a shoulder. Mara checked a wrist seal. A technician left, and for a moment there was nobody else.

"Planning to go easy on me?" Kade said.

Mara looked at him through the glass. "No."

"Good."

"You?"

"Absolutely."

Her eyes narrowed. He smiled.

"Psychological warfare."

"Poorly executed."

Outside, the announcer had started on their introduction, and the story was evidently too good to be brief. Zero. Rank seven. Housemates. House Trial partners. His impossible climb. Her famous family. The phrase that carried down the tunnel was "one of the season's most unexpected training partnerships."

"Unexpected?"

"You were last."

"Still rude."

A rules official stopped between the lanes with a slate against her chest. "Quarterfinal conditions. First to four. Scoring is unchanged from the open rounds. A clean touch on a band, the opponent over the strip, or a controlled takedown. Any uncontrolled head contact is a fifteen-second medical assessment. The barrier is live. Contact with it scores nothing for anybody. Deliberate coupling to arena infrastructure is an automatic stop and a technical review." Her eyes came to rest on Kade for the last sentence.

"That rule's always been there," he said.

"It has never needed this much emphasis."

Mara faced straight ahead. He could tell she was enjoying herself, because one corner of her mouth had gone professionally neutral.

"You share a House and a training group," the official went on. "There will be no House corner. Between points you return to your own reset sector. No communication from teammates. Medical may stop the bout at its discretion."

The glass made the rule visible. Same room, separate lanes. All day the Open had been turning people he ate lunch with into lines on a bracket, and tonight the building would put them back at the same tables and expect nothing to have changed.

A technician closed a scoring band round his ribs and told him to breathe in. It tightened, found him, and let out a notch. Another tucked the ends of the blue thread flat under his hand strap so nothing could snag. Across the glass, someone was doing the same to the thread at Mara's glove.

"Questions?" said the official.

"If she damages my confidence, does that score as uncontrolled contact?"

"No."

"Medical assessment?"

"Also no."

"He was like this before the match," Mara said.

The official's mouth twitched. "Then it's a preexisting condition."

The outer doors unlocked, and the sound came in ahead of the light. Ten thousand people did not make one noise. There was the Meridian chant off the east block, and a flat wall of applause from the general stands, and single names breaking loose near the rail, and under all of it the barrier generators humming up through the soles of his shoes.

He laid the floor out in his head while he still had a head to do it with. One ring, the same ring as this morning, with the lit strip round its edge and two center marks. But the main arena was built for cameras. North and south there was a good four paces of dead floor between the strip and the barrier. East and west the barrier wall came in close, a single stride beyond the white, so that the ring had two narrow sides where a fighter with his back to the strip could feel the field on his shoulder blades. Three ways to score. And one way to lose to Mara, which was to believe the first geometry she showed him.

Mara took a step toward the doors and stopped. "Kade."

He looked at her.

"If it starts. The cold. The floor turning into—"

"I know."

"I'm serious."

"So am I."

She held his eyes for a second. She had looked at him like this once before, the day of Pike, when he had been a hazard she happened to be standing near. There were four months of shared work in it now, and a door she had stopped at because he asked.

"I won't use it to win," he said.

She nodded. Then they walked out into the light, by separate doors.

---

The lights over the quarterfinal floor were bright enough to erase everything beyond the barrier. The announcer went on for some time, and they both ignored him. The difference was that Mara looked comfortable doing it.

He studied her on her mark. Weight centered. Front foot light. Hands loose. Nothing obvious, which meant it was all obvious and he had not found it yet.

The horn went, and he attacked first, and that was the mistake.

She gave him exactly the opening he had expected to find: a narrow lane inside her right guard, the width of a shoulder. He took it. She was already moving to punish the adjustment she knew would come after it. He changed the angle. She had changed before he did. Her forearm met his shoulder, not hard, only enough to spoil the foot he was about to stand on, and she stepped across his line and laid two fingers on the band at his back as he went past her.

One to nothing. He had not marked the board for nothing.

The replay went up over the floor from three angles. From inside, the lane had looked barely wide enough to enter. From above it looked like an invitation painted on the floor. The overhead view drew their paths in pale lines, his bending in, hers running straight, and printed the difference between them. Fourteen centimeters. The crowd applauded the touch. Kade looked at the fourteen centimeters. She had not beaten his speed. She had taken away the place he meant to stand.

He walked to his sector. There was no Sera there to tell him to breathe and no Eli holding up a graph. He had what he had learned, and nothing else. Across the ring Mara bounced once on her toes, and the shoulder he had written down and crossed out moved as freely as the other.

She was not smiling now. That worried him more.

In the second exchange he waited, and so did she. He had spent three months getting more patient, and she had plainly budgeted for it. She came forward with an entry that was just slightly wrong, her lead hand a little low. He saw the flaw. He heard Julian, too: never the floor you're offered. He did nothing. She withdrew. No score.

But the clock had run, and nothing about it had been neutral. Without touching him she had moved him half a step toward the east side, where the barrier stood close. He took the center back. She let him have it, which was another offer.

"Disappointed?" he said.

"Not yet."

She drifted left. He drifted with her. For twenty seconds neither of them committed to anything, and the stands got restless, and Mara liked a restless crowd a good deal better than he did. He knew because her breathing did not change at all.

Then she came at his rear side, and he countered, and she turned the counter into the next problem. Her left hand went high, too shallow to score but high enough to demand a guard, and the guard it demanded was the purple forearm. He covered, slower than he would have that morning. She touched his elbow and turned the axis of the block. He dropped his weight before the pressure could turn the rest of him.

That was the first trap. Her right foot came in behind his lead leg. He pulled the leg out and rotated clear. That was the second. The noise in the stands climbed as though two right answers meant he was ahead.

Mara's eyes had not left his center. He understood, a beat too late to do anything with it, that she had spent the first two movements choosing his third. There was one safe place left to recover to, and she had built it, and his weight was already on its way there and too far forward when it arrived. She hooked his ankle and sent his shoulder the other way.

He hit the mat palm first, then shoulder. The floor field took the last fraction off the landing without making it pleasant. She let go of him before the official called the break and backed into her sector. It had been controlled and clean, and there was nothing in it for Dr. Park to frown at.

Two to nothing. He lay there for a second. The lights were offensively calm.

A hand came down into view. "Two, nothing."

"I can count."

"You sometimes approximate."

He took the hand and stood. The official waited for him to show her stable balance, and he planted both feet, and by then Mara's hand had already gone.

Now he had it. Mara was not fighting him. She was fighting the version of him who would exist three decisions from now, and she had met that person before. Julian had taught Kade to make plans. Mara had spent four months watching what those plans looked like once he started believing in them.

It was deeply unfair. It was also excellent.

The reset horn did not come. A scoring band had faulted in the quarterfinal next door, and the officials would not run two floors on one clean channel. NINETY SECOND HOLD, said the display over the ring, and began to count.

Kade crouched in his sector. Mara stayed standing in hers. They were not allowed corners, but the arena could not stop Meridian from being visible. Sera sat at the front rail with her hands clasped between her knees. Eli had put his tablet down, which Kade had seen him do perhaps twice. Priya held up two fingers, pointed them at her own eyes, and then at Mara. Watch her. Sera pulled the hand down before an official could count it as coaching.

Mara had seen it too. "Subtle."

"It's the House's defining quality."

He looked past her at the replay, which had frozen on his second recovery. His feet were right. His guard was right. He had done everything Julian had drilled into him, correctly, in order, and it had delivered him like a parcel.

Julian's lesson had been to go in with a plan, so that other people could rely on what he did next. Mara's answer was not that plans failed. It was that a plan could be read. Anything other people could rely on, she could rely on.

The count went under twenty. He could not fix it by being random. She would simply wait for randomness to beat itself, which it would, and quickly. He needed something narrower than that and harder. He needed a choice that was still worth something after she understood it.

He stood up at ten. He did not have it yet. He had the shape of where it would go.

The third exchange lasted most of a minute. He gave ground on purpose. She refused to follow it too far. He changed tempo, and she changed distance. He showed her the straight-back retreat, heel, heel, the habit she had corrected several hundred times and that Armitage had charged him two points for that very morning, and he left it hanging out in front of her like washing.

She did not take it. He nearly laughed. She knew that he knew that she knew, and the fight had become ridiculous.

But her attention was on the offer. He could see that. She was not taking it, and declining it was costing her something, the way declining Armitage's ankle had cost him a thought this morning. The feint had already done the only work it was ever going to do. It was a tool. It wasn't a vow.

So he dropped it in the middle, half a step into a retreat he never finished, and broke off the line at an angle neither of them had stood on all match. For the first time her base opened, a fraction, the width of a person recalculating. He stepped through it and touched the band at her ribs.

Two to one.

The east block came apart. He could hear Eli through all of it, which should not have been acoustically possible. Overhead, the replay drew his first path, stopped it dead halfway, and drew a second one leaving it at a slant. The system labeled the movement UNCLASSIFIED TRANSITION, which made one ordinary sidestep sound like a security incident.

Mara read the label on her way back to her mark. "Don't become proud of confusing the software."

"It's one of my measurable strengths."

"The software will adapt."

"You sound sure."

"I have evidence." She settled. And then, because she was Mara and accuracy was a kind of manners with her: "Better."

"That was praise."

"Don't make it unpleasant."

His pulse had come down by the time he reached his own mark. The point had changed more than the board. She could not build a whole exchange off his first commitment any longer and trust him to see it through. She would have to shorten the time between his choices until he had no room to change them. He knew that. She knew he knew.

The horn went, and she changed first, and she stopped giving him time.

It was not reckless. It was pressure, even and close, one decision after another arriving a little sooner than he wanted them, because she knew perfectly well what he turned into when he was hurried. The first contact drove into his guard. He gave two steps, found the center mark under his rear foot, and stopped giving. She did not try to break the brace. She took all her weight off it at once, so that his own resistance tipped him toward her, and he got his feet back under him only just ahead of her hand.

The second entry came low. He shut the lane with his knee, turned his hip, and his hand went, before any part of him had voted on it, for the shoulder that had been iced.

She let him reach. The arm turned under his hand like something oiled. The shoulder had never been the target. The target was the hand he had sent after it, and she drew that hand across his own body and opened up everything behind him, and he tore loose a moment before it became a hold. He had crossed the word out twice. His hand had not read the sheet.

He blocked. He turned another entry with plain mechanics. He took a controlled forearm on the bruise, which hurt more than he let his face say, and circled, and realized she had been steering the circle. His back was to the west side, the narrow side, the strip a step behind his heels and the barrier a stride behind that, close enough that he could feel it on the back of his neck.

And the arena offered itself.

It came the way it always came, politely, from the edge. The floor under both their stances. The anchors under the floor. The wall behind him and the lattice that tied the wall to everything else. It was all one connected thing, with paths laid through it, and the cold was starting its line up from the base of his skull toward the place behind his eyes. Her next step would put him over the strip. And the wall was right there, and it was available, and it was a different answer.

The crowd rose. He could feel what they were waiting for.

"Do it!" someone yelled, off to the right. And from the other side, higher: "Break it again!"

Two voices from two ends of the building, and neither of them knew what it cost. Neither of them would be standing on this floor if the barrier took force from the wrong place and handed it back up through Mara's spine.

He was angry, and the anger made it worse. The line brightened. He could move the problem, he knew that much. He did not know where every part of it would land. And Mara was inside the system. She was one of the things the path ran through. That weighed more than a score.

He let it go, and it did not go all at once. The wall stayed joined to the floor a moment longer. The anchors stayed lit behind his eyes. The path waited, as though a thing he could do became a thing he owed simply by staying possible.

He opened his right hand, the one with the thread on it, and named to himself what belonged inside the match. His body. Hers. The ring, the strip, the bands. Nothing underneath.

The cold drew back. The connections stopped asking. The arena went back to being objects: a floor, a wall, Mara, him.

She had seen it. She could not have seen what it was, but she had seen him decide. She did not pause to reward him for it. She came straight through the space his attention had left, turned him by the shoulder, and sat him down over the strip with a hand in the middle of his chest.

Three to one.

He sat up on the dead floor with the barrier humming at his back. Mara stood several steps off, inside the white.

"You could have used it," she said, low enough that the floor microphones might miss it.

He tipped his head toward the stands. "Are you trying to get us both detained?"

"No."

"Then I made the right call."

She nodded. Something crossed her face and was put away again. Respect, possibly. Relief, certainly. And something else under both, which he did not have a column for.

"You didn't wait for me," he said.

"Would you have wanted me to?"

"No." He got up. "I'd have been insulted for a month."

"Six weeks," Mara said, and went back to her mark.

Up in the medical booth Dr. Park was watching him with her slate in her hands. Director Sen stood behind her, a little back from the glass, the way a person stands who does not wish to appear to be attending. Neither of them signaled. He took that for permission.

One more point to her and it was done. He had one plan left from the prep room, written on the back of a bracket sheet, and he threw it out where he stood. There was nothing the matter with it. But she had been reading his prep-room plans since October.

He built one out of what had happened on this floor in the last nine minutes. She expected him to want the clever reversal now. He was down three to one to someone he badly wanted to impress, and she expected a plan with moving parts. So he would give her something stupidly simple.

He feinted high. She saw the opening under it, because of course she did, and sent the counter, and he rolled under it and came up at last on the line he had been after since the first horn. Her weight shifted. His hand closed on her forearm. He turned.

For one fine fraction of a second Mara Vey was exactly where he needed her.

Then she changed the future. She let go of the arm he had counted on her to protect. She stepped through the direction he meant to send her and not away from it, pinned his lead wrist against his own chest, and fed all of his turn across her hip.

He understood the counter completely while he was in the air, which was not a useful time to understand it.

He landed flat on his back, and the horn came down on top of him. WINNER, said the board, in letters a story high. VEY. FOUR TO ONE.

For a moment it was quiet inside his head. Then he laughed. He could not help it. It came up out of his bruised ribs and hurt on the way.

Mara stood over him. "What?"

"That was awful."

"You lost."

"I noticed."

"You sound happy about it."

"I nearly had the plan."

"You nearly had the wrong plan."

"That's less encouraging." He took her hand and came up. "Did you plan all of that?"

"Most of it."

"How far ahead?"

"Far enough."

"That's deeply irritating."

"I know."

They had not let go yet. They stood there for half a second, close, both breathing hard, her hair coming out of its tie, and it was not anything. It was not enough of anything to deserve the noise ten thousand people made about it.

They made it anyway. Mara stepped back as if the floor had been switched on.

Kade looked for Meridian, which was a mistake. Eli was making a face that Kade filed for future revenge. Sera looked delighted by his suffering generally. Taren was applauding with suspicious enthusiasm. Nessa, still in her dark glasses, had one finger up. *I said one.* Anton looked personally betrayed by his own forecast. And two sections along, gloved and waiting on his call, Julian gave him a single nod.

His band buzzed twice before he reached the tunnel. Darius. WHY DID THE WHOLE ARENA JUST GO OOOH. Then his mother. I SAW NOTHING. I WAS CHARTING.

He walked off the floor grinning and annoyed in about equal parts.

---

Park caught him before Julian could.

"Symptoms."

"None."

"You initiated Boundary perception."

He looked at her. "How do you know?"

"Your pupil response changed for about two seconds. Then it normalized."

"I stopped."

"Headache?"

"No."

"Tremor?"

He held out the right hand, thread and all. It was steady. She wrote something on her slate, and it was longer than a check mark.

"What?"

"Control may include choosing not to complete an event."

It had not occurred to him. All his life, not doing a thing had felt like the absence of an action. It was what he did in hallways. Park was calling it a skill, and she was going to score it.

"That goes in my file?"

"Everything goes in your file."

"Comforting."

She let him go. Julian was waiting twelve steps farther down the corridor, with his gloves on.

"You finally trusted a plan."

"And lost."

"You trusted one plan." Julian smiled, all teeth and goodwill. "Welcome to the next problem."

Then he went to his semifinal. Kade reflected that useful people were a plague, and that there seemed to be more of them every week.

---

Vey against Cross drew the biggest crowd of the night. Kade sat in the Meridian block between Sera and Eli, with a cold pack strapped on his forearm and Taren in the row behind.

It looked nothing like his match. Mara did not know Julian's habits the way she knew his. Julian did not know Mara's corrections the way he knew Kade's. They spent the first minute finding out.

Julian took the center and left her both sides. Against Kade that would have been a threat. Against Mara it was a question: which way did she go when nobody made her? She stepped east. He gave up perhaps three centimeters. On the next pass she stepped west, and he gave up the same three. Nobody touched anybody. They were measuring what each of them thought was expensive.

"This is horrifying," Eli whispered.

"Why?"

"They're both thinking."

"Something you might try," Sera said. "Quietly."

Mara attacked on the third pass. Julian had not made a mistake. He had made the same concession twice, and a repetition from Julian Cross was almost certainly bait. She went in exactly far enough to make him close it, and he closed it, and she had her answer and got out. Thirty seconds later he offered her the center again, and she used his closing step to turn him off his own mark and touched his shoulder.

Julian took the next two with a kind of force Kade could follow and could not yet produce. He made every contact costly to leave, until giving up position was cheaper than staying in it.

She tied it by ignoring his stance and going after his transitions. Each time Julian changed direction there was a sliver of time when the old plan no longer applied and the new one did not have his full weight yet, and she worked in those slivers.

Kade was leaning forward. "She's changed the problem."

"From what?" Eli said.

"From how do I move Julian. To when has Julian not finished moving himself."

Sera pointed at the floor without looking at him. "Quietly."

He sat back. He kept the idea.

Mara took the next point and led three to two into the last minute. Then Julian changed his stance, and Kade's stomach dropped, because he knew that change. It had been used on him in their seventh private session. It was a false concession of the center.

Mara saw the opening, and took only half of it. That was smart. Julian had planned for smart. The concession folded up behind her. It was not a trap with one hidden answer. It was a choice between two unfinished ones. If she took the whole center, he had the counter. If she took half, he kept enough of the west side to shut her way out. She tried to make a third choice out of nothing, and her heel crossed the strip by less than the width of the blue thread at her glove.

The barrier chimed. Three all. With eight seconds left, Julian took the last one.

CROSS, FOUR. VEY, THREE.

She stood in the ring for two seconds after the horn. Then she shook Julian's hand. There was no drama in it, and there was no smile either.

When she got back to the block she did not sit down. She held a hand out to Eli. "Last forty seconds."

Eli gave her the tablet at once. She watched her own loss twice, and then a third time.

"You're allowed to be annoyed first," Kade said.

"I am annoyed."

"You look academic."

"They coexist."

He found that attractive. He decided immediately not to look into why.

She froze the frame on Julian's stance shift and turned it toward him. "You knew this one."

"He's used it on me."

"You didn't tell me."

"You didn't ask."

She stared at him. He waited it out. Her mouth moved, nearly. "Fair." She ran it back a second. "Did he give you the full center?"

"Twice. The first time I rushed it. The second time I tried to show him I'd learned not to rush it."

"And?"

"He wasn't testing my patience. He was testing which lesson I wanted him to see me having learned."

She played it at quarter speed. Julian's rear foot settling. Her own heel going over the white. "I thought the half entry was mine," she said.

He knew what it cost her to say that. "It was yours."

"He planned for it."

"Him planning for your choice doesn't make it stop being your choice. You made him build an answer. I watched him build it. It took him three exchanges."

Her eyes went back to the frame. "That distinction is suspiciously generous from someone I threw five times."

"Four. One of them I sat down over the strip. And that's counting generously."

"The warmup corridor."

"That was weeks ago."

"Still five."

He took the tablet out of her hands and drew a line with his thumbnail down Julian's exit on the west side. "He kept this open through the first three exchanges. He could only close the half entry at the end because you'd spent the whole match teaching him he had to guard it."

She studied the line. Then she took the tablet back and drew another straight through his. "You're giving me too much credit."

"You gave me one point."

"You earned one point."

It stopped him. She did not soften it or turn it into a joke. She left it where she had put it.

The block was emptying round them, toward food and showers. Priya went off with Anton to find more ice. Eli stayed. The tablet was technically his, and he had no instinct at all for leaving two people alone.

Kade looked at the score in the corner of the frame. Four, three. One point did not make Mara the equal of Julian in all the ways the ladder cared about. It meant that tonight, against this problem, Julian had needed her own choices to beat her with.

It made his four to one look different. Mara had not shown him up as a person without a plan. She had found the exact place where he stopped examining what his plan let other people predict.

And she had asked him, just now, what Julian had done to him, and had taken the answer and used it. It came to him, with some surprise, that a rivalry did not need either side to wish the other smaller. He had not known that before tonight, either.

---

Julian won the final a little before ten. Rank one stayed rank one. He looked more tired than triumphant, and when they hung the medal on him, the big display cut to a remote feed, and there was his father.

Kade had never seen Lucian Cross before. He saw what the man's face did to Julian's spine. The crowd saw a proud son standing up straight. Kade had been thrown by that body for three months, and he saw it brace.

"Exactly what was expected," said Lucian Cross.

He had not said well done, or that he was proud. He had said it was expected.

The interviewer asked him what set his son apart from the other finalists.

"Ownership. Julian understands that the strongest person present owns the result. He doesn't wait for circumstance to become someone else's fault."

On the floor Julian's hand closed round the edge of the medal. The smile stayed where it was.

Kade had been hearing versions of that all term. If Julian could have prevented a failure, he counted the failure as his. Kade had half admired it. Coming from the father, it left no room in the building for anybody else to own anything, not even the winning.

The interviewer turned. "Julian, is that how you approached the final?"

For one beat the arena waited. Julian looked toward the competitors' seats. Mara was there with ice on her shoulder. The finalist he had just beaten stood at the rail, still breathing carefully round a split lip. Beyond them were coaches and medics and barrier technicians, and some two thousand first-years who had been knocked out since Monday.

"I approached it as a match against someone capable of beating me," Julian said. "Anything less would have been poor preparation."

The other finalist looked up. Lucian Cross's face did not change by a hair. Julian had not contradicted him. He had simply handed a share of the result back across the floor, in public, with perfect manners.

The crowd heard sportsmanship and cheered it. Kade heard an argument, conducted by the only one of its two parties who was not allowed to admit they were arguing. Julian smiled for the cameras. Kade looked somewhere else. Rank one, it turned out, had costs of its own.

---

The recalculation posted while Meridian was still at its late dinner. Kade's tablet shook against his tray.

MERCER, KADE. MIDYEAR OPEN: QUARTERFINALIST. TOURNAMENT RECORD: FOUR WINS, ONE LOSS. ELITE OPPONENT PERFORMANCE: HIGH. SAFETY COMPLIANCE: FULL. NEW RANK: TWO HUNDRED AND ELEVEN.

The word PROVISIONAL was nowhere on it. He stared. Eli stared over his shoulder.

"That," Eli said, "is a normal response to losing."

"This system is absurd."

"That isn't a technical objection."

"I lost by three points and went up sixteen hundred and ninety-three places."

"From nineteen hundred and four, yes, I can subtract. That's the simplified output. The rules told you this morning. Opponent rank, bracket depth, clean wins, penalty rate. You beat a five hundred and three and then took a point off a seven. Your old number was six weeks stale." He took the tablet out of Kade's hands. "Here. Look at it properly."

He opened the recalculation detail. The public screen had shown six factors. The audit view had sixty-four.

Kade scrolled. Entry commitment latency. Recovery path efficiency. Opponent adaptation after failed feint. Response stability under crowd escalation. Medical compliance. Barrier proximity decision quality.

The system had not merely noted that he declined to use the Boundary. It had logged when his pupils changed, how his balance shifted while the wall was on offer, how long he stood there, and whether the impact that followed was controlled. One line carried a faculty review flag. NONSTANDARD EVENT ABORT. VOLUNTARY. SAFE. Beside it was a note with Park's initials. SUBJECT MAINTAINED RULE BOUNDARY DISCRIMINATION UNDER COMPETITIVE PRESSURE.

He read it twice. The crowd had called that moment hesitation. Park had called it control. The ranking system had given it a coefficient.

"How much did that move me?"

Eli opened the category. "Doesn't say. They publish weights by cohort, never by individual. Otherwise people optimize for the visible function."

"People already optimize for the visible function. It's called the ladder."

"They optimize for rank movement. They don't optimize for all the observations that go into making it, because they don't know what those are."

That distinction sat badly, and sat worse the farther he scrolled. The bout with Mara had thrown off forty-eight thousand timestamped events. Foot position. Field activation. Pressure on each band. Gaze direction. Heart rate. Even the ninety-second hold, when he had crouched in his sector and done nothing visible at all, had a line of its own. COACHING ABSENT SELF CORRECTION: POSITIVE.

"It graded me for thinking while nothing happened."

"Something happened. Your next exchange changed."

"That is not the comforting part, Eli."

Sera arrived with two sealed trays and a roll of tape. "What are we angry about?"

Kade turned the tablet round. "The system knows how long I look at a wall before I decide not to break it."

"Park knows that."

"Park's a person."

"Debatable before coffee."

"The system keeps it."

She read a few lines, standing. "Medical needs contact data and neurological data to stop a bad match. I'd want them to have it."

"Agreed," said Eli. He tapped the performance fields. "It doesn't need to keep all of these to do that."

Sera's face changed. She pulled a chair round beside him and sat.

The three of them sorted the fields by what they were for. Medical necessity. Match scoring. Public rank. Coaching. Anything they could explain went in one column. Anything with more than one plausible use went in a second. The second column got longer faster.

Taren turned up halfway through and stopped behind Sera's chair. "You found the performance audit."

Kade looked up. "You say that as though you knew it was there."

"Anyone can request their own."

"That wasn't what I asked."

Taren looked at the tablet. For a moment he had the particular stillness Kade had begun to notice in him near locked doors and restricted shelves. Then it was gone, and he was pulling up a chair with his easy smile. "The ranking model has a research exemption. It's in the enrollment agreement."

Eli stopped scrolling. "For anonymized training studies."

"That's how the student summary puts it."

"And the legal version?" Kade said.

Taren took the tablet and went to the permissions block without searching for it. There were six linked policies. He opened the fourth. "Operational readiness. Institutional safety. Curriculum validation. And aggregate capability forecasting."

Sera put a finger on the last phrase. "Forecasting for whom?"

"Accredited institutional partners."

"Meaning?"

Taren handed the tablet back. "The document doesn't define it here."

He had not said *I don't know.* Kade put the difference on the pile, which was leaning badly by now.

Eli copied the policy identifier into his own notes. Sera circled a field in the unexplained column. RESPONSE UNDER INFRASTRUCTURE INSTABILITY. "This was logged during your match?"

He checked the timestamp. It was the moment the cold line had reached the west wall. "Yes."

Nobody made a joke. All round the table Meridian ate its late dinner and argued over bracket mistakes. Tens of thousands of measurements had been piling up underneath a school day, and the school day did not look any different for it.

Eli closed the policy and kept the number. "A system can need a thing for one good reason and still hold on to it for another."

Kade thought of his mother's records at the hospital. Of Sen, who had known which questions to ask him before he understood why they were questions. Of an accident report with his father's name on it, and a transfer order dated twenty-three days too early to fit it.

---

Later he found Mara in the Commons, in the chair by the window, with fresh ice on her shoulder and Julian's counter running on her tablet at quarter speed.

"You beat me," he said.

"Yes."

"I went up seventeen hundred places."

"Yes."

"That doesn't bother you?"

"Your previous rank lagged your demonstrated performance."

"That sounded nearly like a compliment."

"It was mathematics."

He looked at the number again. Two hundred and eleven. It should have felt enormous. It did, for about ten seconds, and then his band buzzed.

Darius. TWO ELEVEN IS BASICALLY TWO HUNDRED AND TEN PEOPLE BETTER THAN YOU.

CORRECT, Kade sent.

GLAD ASTERION TEACHES MATH.

Then his mother, in ordinary letters, which with her meant she had sat down to write it. *You chose safe over winning. I am prouder of that than of the number. Also I watched the entire thing. Ice the arm.*

He read it twice. Then he darkened the band.

That was worth more to him than the two hundred and eleven. When he had stood at ten thousand four hundred and eighty-two, last of everyone, the ladder had been the whole world. Now it was one system among several. There were people whose opinion of him no algorithm had produced, and beside them the algorithm looked smaller. He did not think it was meaningless. He thought it was, for once, about the right size.

Mara closed her replay. "You're thinking about your father."

"You're irritating."

"I know."

He sat down across from her. "Do you ever wonder whether the rankings are partly camouflage?"

"For what?"

"All of it." He waved a hand at the window and the island beyond it. "Ten thousand students. Tested constantly. Every bout recorded. Every mistake put in a category. Every kind of person put under pressure until someone knows exactly how they behave."

He told her about the sixty-four fields and the fourth policy and the partners the document did not define. She listened without moving, the way she listened to physics.

"Measurement can have more than one purpose," she said finally.

Sen had told him once that rank made performance legible. Students could read it. Coaches could read it. Sponsors could. Governments, he supposed, could read it best of all.

Across the courtyard one of the great ranking boards was refreshing, thousands of names rolling up the dark in orderly light. For four months he had looked at it and seen a mountain. Tonight he could see another thing it might be, and that was a sensor. It was a way to put ten thousand ambitious young Resonants under load, year after year, and learn precisely what each of them did.

It should have sounded paranoid. After his father's file, it sounded like a hypothesis. He could even name what would prove it wrong, and he noticed that nobody had offered to show him.

Mara followed his eyes to the board. "One ladder that everyone can see," she said.

"And one that somebody went to a great deal of trouble to hide."

Neither of them smiled. Down on the bridge a pair of second-years were already untying the cloth from the rails, blue and white and red, and folding it away for next year. The tournament was over. The other contest had only just come into view.

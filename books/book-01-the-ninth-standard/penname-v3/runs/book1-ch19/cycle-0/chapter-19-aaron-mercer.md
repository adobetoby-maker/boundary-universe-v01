# Chapter 19 — Aaron Mercer

Mara was at the table under the ladder wall at three minutes to seven with two tablets in front of her and nothing on her plate, and Kade understood, before he had sat down, that the second tablet was his.

"Eat first," she said.

"You didn't."

"I'm not the one about to read it."

He sat. Eli arrived a minute behind him with a coffee the size of a paint can and took the third chair without asking whether there was one, which was the only reason Kade did not tell him to go.

Mara turned the second tablet to face him. Three columns. CLAIM. SOURCE. WRONG IF. She had made him build one exactly like it on a mat in Bay One, and he had complained about the third column, and here it was, filled in for every row in her flat square hand as though she had been answering him.

He read it top to bottom, and then again with his thumb on the edge so the screen would not dim.

MERCER, A. RESEARCH CLEARANCE RESTRICTED. RELATED FACILITY: ASTERION PRECURSOR PROGRAM. A Research Annex on a struck-through accession sheet. A page cut out of a bound volume with a blade, twenty-six years ago, a plate and two captions taken as one. PROGRAM STATUS: DISCONTINUED AFTER CONTACT EVENT. And at the bottom, saved offline because a refresh had tried to take it back, four words that had been on a page for less than a second. CONTACT ZERO. COMPATIBILITY STUDY.

"That could be anyone," he said.

Mara did not answer, and he hated it, and knew she was making him say the rest.

"Say it."

"It could be anyone. Wrong if column, row eleven. I wrote it before you sat down."

"You don't believe it."

"No."

He did not either. That was the trouble with a good table. It did not let you keep the row you liked.

The name should not have weighed what it weighed. Aaron Mercer was a photograph in the hall at home, and a box he had opened twice and been sorry both times, and a story that had always ended the same way. Industrial accident. Too fast. Nothing anyone could have done. He had built a whole way of walking out of rooms on top of that story, and he had never once checked whether the floor under it was real.

"There's a row that isn't on here," Mara said. "It's the reason I ran the rest."

She told him. The Sunday call. Her father asking whether the structural event had recurred, in those words, when the notice said composite failure and the faculty line said anomalous reading and nobody on the island had used his word. His face going still at Aaron's name. *Leave the Mercer family where they are.*

"Your father has access to restricted telemetry off this island."

"He should not."

"And he knew my dad's name."

"He knew something when he heard it. I don't know what."

Eli, who had been very quiet for Eli, said, "Ordering Mara not to investigate is a strategy with a known failure mode."

She ignored him. Kade was looking at the date at the top of the export, which said last night, late. She had carried this four days and brought it to him line for line, and the other copy on the table was hers, and it was the same. Nobody had ever handed him a thing that was his without keeping the better version.

The cold thing under his ribs was not for her.

"So what now," he said. "I go stand in Sen's office with a dead index and a page that isn't there and she says no before I finish."

"You could."

"I promised my mother two things." He said it to the table. "I don't open a door on my own. And whoever I'm going to stand in front of, she hears it first."

"You told me."

"I'm telling you again, because I want to walk to the Spire right now and I'm not going to." He looked up. "I need something that puts a name in that row. Not an initial."

Eli set his coffee down with the care of a person about to say something he had been waiting to say. "There's a room under the engineering library. Six terminals that read the pre-standard catalogs, because the modern index can't. The Systems Lab keeps them alive for compatibility and nobody uses them." He looked at Mara. "Your dead link was on a modern page. The old machines don't know what a dead link is. They just have the drawer."

"Do we need credentials?"

"Not for the local index. It's the same public catalog the Spire has, in a format nobody's translated."

"And if the drawer's locked?"

"Then it's locked, and we look at the outside of it and go home. I'll say that again downstairs. I've been told I rehearse."

Mara stood and picked up her tablet and left his where it was.

"Two copies," she said. "I said it was yours too. That means you decide where it goes next. Not me."

---

The compatibility room was under the engineering library at the end of a corridor that had lost its argument with the ventilation plan, and it smelled of dust and coolant. Six terminals. Twelve chairs, as though the academy expected the dead software to become popular again. A printed sign asked students to request faculty supervision before connecting external storage, and someone had added beneath it, in marker, THE PRINTER IS NOT EXTERNAL STORAGE. STOP ASKING.

Eli read both signs. "The institution remains prepared for every emergency except ours."

Mara had already found the cable at the back of the nearest terminal, and she held it up so they could both see it before she pulled it. "Offline, it reads its own drawer and reports to nobody. It also can't fetch anything the room hasn't already got. That's the trade. Say it if you don't want it."

"I want it," Kade said.

She pulled it and laid it on the desk between them.

"Order," Eli said, and Kade watched him make himself say it slowly. "Public index first. No credentials. No bypasses. If a door's already open we look through it. We don't make a new one. If it asks for a password, that's the outside of the drawer, and we're done."

"That sounded rehearsed."

"I live with you."

The terminal came up with a date eleven years old and a fan that wanted attention. Mara corrected the date by hand without touching the network. Eli fed a strip of thermal paper into a printer bolted under the desk and explained, when Kade asked, that screenshots carried metadata and paper carried fingerprints, and Mara said that in that case he should try not to commit a crime while holding it.

MERCER returned two hundred and twelve entries. Payroll disputes. Student applications. A donor who had funded a desalination pump and expected it named after him. Six citations. Kade read every line and none of them was his father.

Mara narrowed the dates. Seventy-one.

Eli had the maintenance binder open on his knee and was running a finger down a table of catalog prefixes. "Yours isn't a department," he said. "It's an import batch. When the old catalog was pulled into the new one, anything the new system couldn't classify stayed in a compatibility table. The record survived because the archive didn't know what it was looking at."

They searched the prefix. The terminal asked for a media family. Mara chose personnel and got nothing. Eli chose research administration and got nothing. Kade looked at the two words that had been on Mara's export from the start.

PRECURSOR PROGRAM.

Not academy. Not a department of the academy. The old accession sheet had named the rock a Research Annex before it was a school, and if the program had stood here before the walls did, the system that inherited the walls would have filed it with them.

"Facilities," he said.

Mara looked at him.

"If it was here before the school, it's part of the site. Legacy facilities."

Eli selected it. One result. Not a person. A conversion log.

Seven media boxes had been entered under the precursor identifier. Five were marked destroyed, water damage. One had been transferred to a classified repository. The seventh had no disposition at all. Somebody had simply never written down where it went.

Mara printed it. The thermal printer screamed for six seconds and gave up a pale gray strip, and Kade took it, and his right hand was steady, and he noticed that he had checked.

"One box," he said.

"One box the system lost track of," Eli said.

"That sounds better."

"It isn't."

"It is to me."

Mara typed the orphan number. The fan climbed until Kade felt it through the desk in the heels of his hands, and then an unreconciled roster opened with most of its names replaced by black bars.

One was not.

MERCER, AARON J.

Kade read the fields under it in the order they were given, because the order was the only thing holding him in the chair.

Age at entry: twenty-two.

Civilian technical designation: systems analyst.

Research status: subject slash participant.

Aptitude route: 000.

The fans were the only sound in the room. Eli said his name, once, quietly, and Kade did not answer, because he was looking at three digits he had first seen on a district tablet in Room Four, the ones that had frozen a national test and put two officials in Ms. Alvarez's doorway before midnight. He had told Sen, in an empty bay with blue tape on the floor, that you do not write a failsafe for a thing that never happened. You write it the morning after. He had said thirty-one years. He had not, in that room, let himself say for whom.

His father had not worked near Zero. His father had been Zero.

Mara's hand touched his forearm, two fingers, the way you would touch a rail to see if it was live. He pulled away before he had decided to, and her hand dropped at once, and the regret came so fast it had a physical location.

"Sorry."

She nodded. She did not tell him it was all right, which it was not, and that helped more than the other thing would have.

Eli opened the next surviving field. Most of it was corrupted or sealed. One line had come through.

TRANSFERRED. ASTERION SITE. PROJECT CONTINUITY.

The date was seventeen years old. Kade knew the date of the industrial accident the way he knew his own birthday, because they nearly touched, and he did the arithmetic twice. Twenty-three days. His father had been moved to this rock twenty-three days before the accident that was supposed to have killed him somewhere else.

Every memory he had of the story shifted one degree and became unreliable. His mother at the sink on the anniversary, when she thought he was asleep. The photograph she had never once moved. The watch in the box that did not run. The line he had been given at eight and nine and eleven, that some accidents happened too fast for goodbyes. All of it could still be true. Or true the way Asterion's notices were true. Technically. Selectively. Uselessly.

"After the transfer," he said. "Anything."

Eli tried. The terminal produced a list of file identifiers rather than files, most of them blacked out, and two metadata fields that had survived.

MEDICAL OVERSIGHT: A. SEN.

CONSENT STATUS: REAUTHORIZED.

"No first name," Eli said, and nobody in the room pretended that was a distinction.

Kade read the second line three times. Consent. She had made him ask Eli before he shared the tactical model. She had stood in a doorway and told him that power made shortcuts around consent easy to justify, and he had thought it was a lesson. It had been a confession with the name filed off.

"What does reauthorized mean?"

"Could be renewing a study form," Eli said. "Could be changing the whole project underneath somebody. The word doesn't say."

"Can you open it?"

"No."

"Can you get around it?"

Eli gave him the look he deserved, and Kade already knew the answer was yes in theory and terrible in practice, and that asking had been a way of not deciding.

"Right," he said.

Mara printed the index entry, broken lines, every other field a black rectangle, and laid it beside the conversion log and lined up the box numbers with her thumbnail.

"The reauthorization is on the intake record. Same box. Not a later note."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning we know when the word entered the system. Not what it meant."

He wanted the difference to be pedantic. It was the only honest thing anyone had said in a quarter of an hour.

Eli opened the roster's data dictionary. Most definitions pointed at a server that no longer existed. The three stored locally were worse than nothing.

SUBJECT AND PARTICIPANT: OPERATIONAL ROLE VARIES.

REAUTHORIZED: SEE GOVERNING INSTRUMENT.

CONTINUITY: SEE PROGRAM AUTHORITY.

Each one used three more hidden things to explain the first.

Kade touched GOVERNING INSTRUMENT. The terminal asked for a credential.

He had his hand back over the keys before he knew it, and Mara had his wrist. Not hard. Enough.

"Open door," she said. "Not a new one."

He looked at her hand. He looked at Eli, who had not moved, who was not going to tell him what to do and was going to remember what he did. He had said the rule himself at breakfast. It had been easy at breakfast.

Kade took his hand back.

"Open door."

She let go. He sat with the shape of it, which was that restraint felt exactly like surrender from the inside, and that Sen had been the one to teach him the difference, and he did not know anymore what she had been teaching him for.

Mara ran the last search herself, because it was her fragment. CONTACT ZERO.

REFERENCE EXISTS. ACCESS PROHIBITED.

No title. No summary. Just proof the phrase was real.

He looked at the strips on the desk. Aaron Mercer. Zero. Precursor program. Project Continuity. A. Sen. Contact Zero. He could feel the shape of a system assembling around him, edges and loads, and for the first time in his life the thing he was good at was no use. He could see that the parts were connected and not one line of the connection.

He stood so fast the chair rolled into the next desk.

"I have to call my mother."

"Kade."

"She hears it first. That was the second thing." He was already at the door. "I'm not going to Sen. I'm going to a stairwell."

---

The engineering library had a service stair nobody used, and he went up two flights and stopped on a landing with a caged white light and a fire door and put his free hand flat on the concrete.

She picked up on the second ring.

"It's before noon," Elena said. "What happened."

"Did Dad work at Asterion?"

The silence was not the kind where a person searches. It was the kind where a person recognizes, and he knew her voice well enough to tell the difference.

"Mom."

"Kade."

"Did he."

"Yes."

One syllable. He had built seventeen years on a floor, and the floor had just told him what it was made of.

He closed his eyes. "Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because I was told not to."

"By who."

"I'm not going to answer that yet."

"You could."

"I could. I'm choosing not to say a name I was warned could put you in danger until I know what you're holding. Tell me what you've got. It's the deal."

He told her. The roster with the black bars. The three digits. The transfer, twenty-three days before. A. Sen. He heard her breathing change at the last one and did not stop.

"And I'm not alone," he said. "Mara found the first row. Eli's downstairs with the binder. You're hearing it before anyone with an office does. That's both things. Now I want the rest."

"Yes," she said. "All right."

She had been twenty-four. Aaron had not come home, and two men she had never seen came to the apartment, dark suits, no uniforms, identification shown too fast to read, and one of them said Aaron's full name in her doorway and told her to sit down.

"Where was I?"

"In the apartment."

"You let them in with me there?"

"I didn't let them do anything. They knocked and I opened the door and it was already happening." A breath. "You were in the blue chair by the window. You'd learned to pull yourself up and not how to get down. You had a wooden spoon and you kept dropping it and shouting until I gave it back."

A blue chair he knew only from photographs. A baby making a spoon the largest available emergency. His mother, seven years older than Mara was now, opening a door to men who already knew.

"One spoke," she said. "The other walked through the apartment and looked at everything. He touched the photographs on the shelf and Aaron's drafting case, and he opened the kitchen door and stood in it a long time. I've had seventeen years to think about why and I don't know."

Her voice had gone into the register she used for emergency-room stories that still had blood in them. Flat and exact.

"The one who spoke put an envelope on the coffee table. There had been a containment failure at a private industrial site. Aaron had been supporting a federal research partnership. There were no recoverable remains. He said that with you in the chair."

"What did you do."

"I asked him to say it again."

Of course she had.

"He said it again. The same words. I asked where the site was, and it was classified. I asked who had been with him, and that was classified. I asked who had identified him if there was nothing to identify, and he pointed at the envelope."

"Do you still have it?"

"It's in the box. Under the cardboard insert. You opened the certificate. You didn't lift the insert."

There had been a second layer to the box. Even the box had kept something from him.

"Did you see a body."

"No."

The word cracked in the middle. He leaned his weight into the wall.

"What did they give you."

"A report. The certificate. His things." She did not make him ask. "The watch. His wallet. His keys. The pencil he chewed. The ring he wore on his right hand when he worked."

He knew all five. The watch that did not run. The thin brown wallet with the expired transit card still in it. The pencil with the teeth marks along the barrel. Keys that opened nothing Elena owned, and a plain steel band that had been too big for any finger he had at twelve and was nearly right now.

"Did the things prove it?"

"They proved someone had emptied his pockets."

No hesitation. She had had that sentence a long time.

"Why keep the keys?"

"Because throwing them out would have meant agreeing there was nothing left for them to open."

He had held those keys at twelve and thought them an adult's failure to clean out a drawer.

"Did you believe them?"

"At first."

That was worse than no.

"What changed."

"A woman came. Three weeks after."

He had known since the terminal, and it still arrived like a step that was not there. "Who."

"Kade."

"Mom. Please."

The call went quiet, and on her end a cart went past, and a voice asked her something, and she said "Two minutes" in the voice that made people believe she could hold time still. A door shut.

"Amara Sen."

There was nothing in him shaped to say.

"After dark," Elena said. "It was raining. Ordinary rain. No coat, no umbrella, hair wet through. She looked like she hadn't slept in a week. I nearly didn't open the door."

"Why did you."

"She said Aaron used to call me Len when he wanted me to stop being angry."

He had never heard the name. "Did it work?"

"Never." It came close to a laugh and failed. "She knew other things. The burn on his left thumb. The notebook he kept in the freezer because no reasonable person would look there. The way he drew a box around any equation he didn't trust."

"So she knew him."

"She had access to things about him. That's what I could prove."

That was his sentence. Or he had it from her.

"She said the Asterion site. Never the academy. She said Aaron had done something important and there were people who would use his work if they could reach it, and the safest thing I could do for you was let the accident stand. She said people would watch the official file for anyone who refused the story. Questions would make attention. She didn't promise to stop it. She didn't offer anything." A pause. "She said he had protected the continuity of something. I thought she meant the research."

He looked at the strip in his hand. PROJECT CONTINUITY.

"Did you ask if he was alive."

Her answer took too long.

"I asked whether she'd been there when he died."

"That isn't what I asked."

"It's what I asked her."

"What did she say."

"She said she had been there when the project ended."

He knew that sentence. He had been given one like it in an empty bay. True along an edge too narrow to stand on.

"And you let her go."

"I threw a glass at the door after she'd shut it."

He stopped on the landing.

"You threw a glass."

"It was a bad glass."

The sound that came out of him was small and broken in the middle, and she heard it, and neither of them called it a laugh.

Then the stairwell tilted, because the pieces had finished landing. Sen had known his father. Sen had gone to his mother in the rain three weeks after the story and asked for her silence. Sen had stood in a doorway and said no before the word father was out of his mouth.

"I have to go."

"Kade. Wait."

He did not end it. Barely.

"One thing before you decide what you do next."

"What."

"I didn't keep it from you because I thought you were weak."

He said nothing.

"I kept it because I was scared. I'd already lost him. I was twenty-four and angry and I had a baby with his face, and everyone who came to my door told me not to ask. So I stopped asking. It isn't a defense."

The honesty went through the anger the way a justification never would have.

"You tell me not to run," he said.

"Yes."

"You did."

"Yes."

No excuse after it. Just yes. He slid down the wall until he was sitting on the step, and for a while neither of them said anything, and it was not the silence before a hang-up.

"What was Zero Protocol?" he asked at last.

"I don't know."

He believed her.

"What happened to him."

"I don't know."

"Was he Resonant."

The pause was different. "Yes."

His head came up. "You knew that."

"I knew he could do things nobody could explain. I never knew what they called it. I was never told a Standard."

"What could he do."

She took her time. "I saw him move force once. No Conduit. Before you were born, a shelf came off the wall in the apartment. Full of books. It should have hit me."

"What happened."

"It fell sideways."

"Sideways how."

"I don't know how. He didn't touch it. He was across the room. It went sideways, and he said the shelf had misunderstood gravity, and I told him gravity had understood perfectly and he was avoiding the question."

"Did he explain?"

"He tried. He drew three diagrams on the back of an electricity bill and two of them contradicted each other. Then he got a nosebleed and said it was dust."

Kade touched two fingers under his own nose. Nothing now. Only the memory of the heat after the floor broke, and copper at the back of his throat, and Park's card with its three lines.

"Often?"

"Near the end, sometimes. Headaches. He said the lab lights were bad."

He looked up at the caged white fixture. Cheap light. Nothing special.

"Did you believe that?"

"I believed he needed me to. That's a different thing."

"Why not when my headaches started."

"Because until your test, your headaches were the headaches of a boy who slept four hours and lived on fries."

"That remains a strong diagnosis."

"It does."

The old rhythm held for one exchange, and then she said, quietly, "After Northline called me I took the box down. To see if I'd missed something. Everything looks like something once you've got a reason to be afraid of it."

He sat on the next step down. Above him a door had opened and not closed, and somebody was standing on the other side of it not pretending very hard.

"Tell me one thing that isn't evidence," he said. "About him. Nothing Resonant. Nothing useful."

She was quiet a moment.

"He couldn't cook rice."

"Rice is water and patience."

"He distrusted both. He burned the bottom and left the top raw, every time, and I let him try more times than was reasonable because he treated the pot like a system he could solve."

"What else."

"He sang doing the dishes. Badly. He never knew the words, so he made up technical ones and claimed they were improvements. He carried mints and never ate them. He fixed things before asking if you wanted them fixed. He hated being in photographs unless he was the one holding the camera. He could make you furious and then ask one question that proved he'd heard every word."

She stopped. He waited through the hospital quiet behind her.

"He wanted you," she said.

His hand tightened on the phone. "You don't have to make him better for me."

"I'm not."

"You don't know what he chose."

"Neither do you."

It landed clean because it was true, and she went on before he could make it a fight.

"Wanting you doesn't prove he did right by us. It doesn't prove anything except that he wanted you. You asked for something ordinary. That's ordinary."

He looked at his empty hand. He had no memory of Aaron holding it. No voice to go with the photographs. He had burned rice now, and bad songs, and a shelf that fell the wrong way, and it was not enough, and it was more than he had owned at seven that morning. For seventeen years his father had been an absence with a frame around it. Now he was becoming a person in pieces, and it was far harder to miss a person than a photograph.

"Don't go to Sen alone while you're this angry," Elena said.

"That's most of my plan."

"I know it is."

"I'm not hanging up."

"Good."

They stayed on the line two more minutes and said nothing useful, and when he ended it he did not get up.

The door above him finished opening. Mara came down as far as the landing and stopped. She did not ask if he was all right, and he was grateful for it.

"She knew," he said. "Some. Not all."

"And Sen."

"Sen knew him. She went to my mother three weeks after, in the rain, and asked her to keep quiet." He looked up. "She said no to me in a doorway, Mara. She had it ready."

Mara's face went very still.

"Then she lied to you."

"Yes."

She sat one step below him. Not touching. Not leaving.

"I want to go at her," he said.

"That sounds like you."

"I also want to leave."

"That also sounds like you."

He gave her a tired look. "Helpful."

"I'm not trying to be helpful."

"Then what are you doing?"

She looked at the fire door.

"Making sure the next thing you do is your decision."

He sat with it. The anger was still there, all of it, but for once it did not feel like clarity. It felt like weather. Real. Strong. Passing through.

---

The academy did not stop because his childhood had become unreliable. At thirteen hundred his band reminded him he had Mechanics, and for one unreasonable second he hated Professor Amadi for expecting him to be there.

"You have class," Mara said.

"My father was in a classified precursor program."

"Yes."

"That feels like an excuse."

"Probably is." She stood. "I think you should decide whether not going helps."

It was irritating enough to work.

Amadi had scheduled fifty minutes on load paths. Of course she had. Each pair had a tabletop truss kit, four weighted blocks, and a tray of ceramic failure tabs, and the point was to make an invisible thing visible: build the frame, predict which tab cracked first, then load it until the prediction became expensive.

His partner was a Sol second-year who labeled every strut before touching it and slid the worksheet across without ceremony. "You calculate. I'll build."

"Fine."

He wrote the first equation and it turned into the roster under his pencil. One load coming in through a point nobody had labeled honestly. One support carried somewhere else. A whole structure standing on the assumption that a missing member was gone.

"Mercer."

She was pointing at the page. He had put the same value into two supports.

"That one's negative."

"Right."

They built it. She seated each connector with her thumbnail and checked the alignment at table level, and he placed the first block where his numbers said, and the wrong tab snapped.

It was a tiny sound. He flinched hard enough to catch the tray with his wrist, and three tabs went across the floor, and two people turned. His partner did not. She crouched and started collecting.

"The diagonal was carrying more than the model said," he told her.

"The model said exactly how much it was carrying."

He looked at the page. The negative sign he had added sat in front of the correct number. The next line still used the old one.

"I carried the error forward."

"You did."

No comfort in it and no accusation. A broken tab and a fact. They reset. The second time the equations stayed equations for almost a minute. She loaded one block, then two. Across the aisle a frame collapsed and got a cheer, and Amadi said that structural failure was not a sporting event, and the room was quiet for four seconds. Kade set the third block. The frame held.

His partner said his name again. The fourth block was in her open hand.

"Sorry."

"Do we load it?"

He looked at the truss and could not remember which tab they had predicted.

"Give me a second."

She gave it to him. Waiting made it worse. The class went on around them, weights settling, tabs cracking, everyone else's invisible forces ordinary enough to compute.

He pushed the worksheet toward her. "Check me."

She traced the numbers. "You dropped the lateral component."

Again. He had built the same absence into the structure twice in one hour.

"Right."

She took the third block off before the frame could go.

"Do you need to leave?"

The answer came up on its own. No. He held it behind his teeth.

"Probably."

"Then why are you still here?"

He had nothing for that. On the board a beam sat on four supports. Halfway through, Amadi called on him.

"Mercer. If the fourth support is removed, what happens?"

He looked at the diagram.

"Depends whether the other three know it was carrying anything."

Amadi paused.

"That was not the mathematical answer."

"No."

"Are you able to continue?"

Quiet. Not for the room. He considered the old answer, the one that had always worked. Fine.

"No," he said.

She nodded at the door. "Then go."

He went. The permission nearly undid him worse than the roster had.

He walked the island for an hour, and not toward the Spire, which was a kind of progress. Across the forum, past the ranking boards, along the western seawall to the observation deck where the wind came off the Pacific with nothing in its way. His number still read 1,904. Yesterday it had been enormous. Today it was absurd. The academy could measure his footwork to four places and had either not thought to tell him who his father was, or had thought about it and decided. The difference was the only thing he cared about, and there was no board for it.

At 14:22 Eli found him. Kade did not ask how.

"Water," Eli said, "or aggressively orange."

"Water."

Eli handed it over and sat on the rail and let a minute go by, which for Eli was discipline.

"I copied the file references. Not the sealed ones. Identifiers and hashes. If any of it disappears, we'll know it was there."

"That's paranoid."

"I prefer audit-conscious."

Kade almost smiled.

Eli looked at the water. "My dad's an electrical contractor. He thinks this place is ridiculous. He sends me pictures of breaker panels I did not ask for." A pause. "He cried when I got in. I'm saying I know exactly what my father does, every day, and I can't imagine finding out he'd had a whole other life with a classification on it."

Kade looked over. "Neither can I."

"That seems inaccurate, since you currently are."

"Thank you."

"I'm helping."

He was, in the annoying way that was becoming the only way that worked. The problem was no smaller with Eli on the rail. Kade was less alone inside it, and the difference was turning out to be the whole of what got him from one hour to the next.

---

At sixteen hundred he requested a meeting with the Director, and the scheduling system declined him before he had finished typing the reason.

DIRECTOR UNAVAILABLE. SECURITY SESSION.

He suspected her at once, and knew that he would have suspected her if the message had said she was at lunch.

Taren found him in the Commons staring at it. "You look like a person considering property damage."

"Sen's unavailable."

"That happens. She runs the place."

"Today."

Taren's face changed by almost nothing, and Kade, who had spent four months learning to read the tell before a punch, saw it.

"You know something."

"No."

"That was fast."

Taren held his eyes. "I know the Directorate has people on campus this week."

"What Directorate."

Taren blinked, and then smiled, easily. "Continuity Directorate. Government policy people. They come after a big security event and ask everyone to fill in forms."

"How do you know?"

"House liaison notices."

"Show me."

The smile held. "The notice?"

"You said notices. Open one."

Behind them the Commons doors let in a group back from afternoon drills, loud, then thinning toward the lifts. Taren took out his tablet and scrolled the House channel with the screen angled toward Kade, unhurried. Meal assignments. A maintenance closure. An advisory about visiting officials that named no agency at all.

EXPECT LIMITED ADMINISTRATIVE ACCESS DURING EXTERNAL SECURITY REVIEW.

"No Directorate," Kade said.

"The liaison briefing was more specific."

"Which briefing."

Taren locked the screen. Half a second late.

"Upper-year captains get context."

"You're not a captain."

"I help with scheduling."

Another good answer. Kade had spent months admiring how Taren knew where everyone would be before they got there. Now every useful thing about him had a second edge on it.

"Did the briefing say anything about Project Continuity?"

Taren's eyes went to the folded strip of thermal paper in Kade's hand and came back.

"I don't know what that is."

Slower than the others. Kade filed the speed beside everything else and did not say so. The name meant nothing yet. Continuity Directorate. Project Continuity. One more line between two things he could not see.

---

That evening Elena sent a photograph.

Not from the box. One he had never seen. Aaron at a kitchen table, younger than any picture in the hall, a mechanical pencil in his teeth, drawing something on a sheet of paper while Elena, beside him, laughed at whatever he had just said. Behind it, photographed separately, the back of the print in his father's hand.

SYSTEMS ARE JUST AGREEMENTS THAT HAVE FORGOTTEN THEY WERE NEGOTIATED.

He read it until the screen dimmed, and turned it back up, and read it again. He did not know whether Aaron had meant a truss or a country or something Kade had no word for yet. He knew the sentence sounded far too familiar, and that he had never heard it before.

Mara found him under the ladder wall with the photo open. He turned it so she could read the back.

"That sounds like you," she said.

"No." He looked at his father's face, at the pencil, at the laugh he had never heard. "I sound like him."

It hurt in a place he had not known he had.

She sat down beside him. Across the room Eli was repairing a sensor array with a spoon in his other hand. Sera had her Response material spread across two chairs and a look that dared anyone to move it. Taren was gone somewhere. The House went on being a House, whatever had happened to anybody in it that day.

He looked at the photograph again. He wanted answers. He wanted to stand in front of Sen. He wanted to be on a boat before the island could change the story a second time. All three were true at once, and that was the problem.

If Aaron Mercer had not died the way Kade had been told, then something else had happened to him, and every person Kade trusted had been handed a different piece of it.

And he had spent his whole life being able to see the edges of a system from inside. He sat under the ladder wall with his father's handwriting on his screen and could not tell, for the first time, which one he was in.

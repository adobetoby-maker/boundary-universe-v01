# Chapter 19 — Aaron Mercer

Mara had booked the carrel at the top of the east stair for seven, the one with the link that did not stutter, and Kade understood what that meant before he reached the last step. She had picked the room where it started. She did not do things by accident and she did not do them for comfort, so the choice was information, and he took it the way she had taught him to take everything. Claim. Source. Wrong if.

She was standing, not sitting. Two tablets on the desk, face down, the same House loaner model with the same scuff on the corner where generations of first-years had dropped them.

"Which one's mine?"

"Either. They're the same file." She turned one over and pushed it an inch toward him and kept her hand flat beside it a second, as though the tablet might slide off the desk on its own. "I deleted one row from both. The one that summarized your record. It wasn't evidence. It was just your life."

He sat. He read.

She had built the table the way she had made him build his in Bay One, on a mat with blue tape at his feet, and he had complained about the third column then, because the third column was where you wrote down how you could be wrong, and nobody enjoyed that. He went to it first now. Not the claims. The wrong-ifs. Reading them was the only way to trust the rest.

*Wrong if a legitimate academy contact sent him a private summary.* *Wrong if MERCER, A. is another person.* *Wrong if the page was cut for an ordinary reason.* Each one stood there honestly, and each one, when he tried to lean his weight on it, gave a little and did not hold.

Then the claims. A research clearance under his father's initial, restricted, with no date and no agency. A precursor program on this rock before there was a school. A Research Annex on an accession sheet somebody had struck through in three inks. A page cut from a bound volume with a blade, twenty-six years ago, a plate and two captions taken as one. A program discontinued after something called a contact event. And at the bottom, saved offline because a refresh had tried to take it back, four words that had lived on a screen for less than a second. Contact Zero. Compatibility study.

He put his thumb on the edge so the screen would not dim, and read it again, and the second time through he noticed he was doing what he did on a mat when Park asked for a number. Reporting from the outside. Right hand steady on the glass. Breathing on a count. Aaron Mercer had been a photograph in the front room and a face on a badge too small to read, and a black box on the closet shelf he had opened once, at eleven, and put back in order, and never asked to see again because it was hers. He had built a way of leaving rooms on top of that box. He had never once checked what the box was standing on.

"Say the row that isn't on it," he said.

She had not sat down. "How do you know there's one?"

"Because you don't run a search like this off a dead index and a cut page. Something made you look."

She told him. Sunday, nine o'clock, her father on a link from a room with a flag in it. Five questions about her semester and one he had come for. Whether the structural event had recurred. In those words. The academy notice said composite failure. The faculty line said anomalous reading. Nobody on the island had used his word, and her father did not work on the island. Then Aaron's name, and her father going still in a way she had been reading since before she could read, and an instruction. Leave the Mercer family where they are.

Kade listened without moving. When she finished, he found that the cold thing under his ribs had a direction, and the direction was not toward her.

"Your father told you what he wants."

"Yes."

"And you built this anyway."

"He set a condition. I've met every condition he's ever set. This one I looked at." She finally sat. "I told Sera the winning line before I started. One link between your father and this island that didn't come from his face or a public death record. I have one. That's where it stopped being mine."

"You could have run it another week and made it cleaner."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because that's how a thing becomes a possession. I've watched it done." She looked at the second tablet. "Two copies. Line for line. You decide where it goes."

The link in the carrel hummed. Downstairs somebody dropped a pan, and somebody else applauded.

He had promised his mother two things in a hallway with a box on the table. He did not open a door on his own. Any door. Records, rooms, her. And whoever he was going to stand in front of with what he found, she heard it first. Not after. Not three words. He had told Mara the promise across a dining-hall container two nights ago without knowing what she was holding, and she had heard it, and here she was, handing him a door with his father's name on it and asking where it went.

"I'm not going to Sen," he said. "Not with an initial and a hole in a book. She says no before the word's out. I've watched her do it."

"Then what?"

"I need a name in that row. Not a letter." He turned the tablet face down, the way she had left it. "And I'm not going to get it by pulling a cable out of a wall. There's a desk."

---

Eli caught them at the bottom of the stair with a coffee the size of a paint can and the expression of a person who had been told nothing and worked out most of it from faces.

"Records," he said. "Old administration wing, ground floor. The site-history desk."

Mara looked at him.

"Architecture students use it for the seawall permits. I used it last month for a duct route that predated the duct." He fell in beside them. "There's a form. It's paper. It has a box on it nobody ticks."

"Which box?"

"Include unreconciled legacy media." He drank. "When the old catalog got pulled into the new one, anything the new system couldn't classify was left in a holding table with no disposition. It isn't restricted. It isn't anything. Nobody decided. The desk will put it on a reader if you ask, and nobody asks, because nobody knows the box is there."

Kade stopped on the covered way. "You've been waiting to say that since the stairs."

"I've been waiting to say that since the cut page. I rehearse. I've been told."

"Say the rest."

Eli said the rest slowly, which for Eli was physical labor. "It's a counter. You give the clerk a form with your name on it and a reason, and it gets logged like every other request in the building, and the log is a thing the Director's office can read whenever it likes. We look at what the desk puts on the reader. We don't ask for anything the reader doesn't already have. If it says sealed, that's the outside of the drawer."

"That's a door with my name on it."

"Yes."

"Good," Kade said, and kept walking, and felt the two of them look at each other behind his back.

The old administration wing had been the first building on the island with windows, and it kept them the way an old man keeps a good coat. The site-history desk was a counter under a stair, one clerk, and two architecture students ahead of them arguing about a permit that predated the seawall by a year. A printed notice on the counter said the desk would close at eleven thirty for the external security review and reopen Thursday. Kade read it twice. Thursday was a long way from a Tuesday morning.

The form was paper. Requester. House. Purpose. A column of small boxes down the right side, most of them about drawings.

He wrote his name. He wrote Meridian. In the purpose line he wrote *family history*, and looked at it, and did not add anything to make it sound like coursework.

Mara, beside him, said quietly, "Scholar clearance can co-sign. It reaches further."

"I know."

"I'm offering."

"I know you are." He ticked the box Eli had named. "It's my father. It goes under my name or it doesn't go."

She took the pen back without a word, which was how she agreed with things.

The clerk read the form the way clerks read forms, and stopped at the name, and looked up, and Kade watched her recognize his face from a feed and decide not to say so. Then she read the purpose line, and read it again, and something in her posture became careful.

"This is logged," she said. "Like anything else."

"I'd assumed."

"The unreconciled batch is slow. It's on the old reader." She glanced at the notice. "I can put it up. I can't promise the review lets you finish."

"Then we'll read fast."

They sat on a bench under a window. Eli took the maintenance binder out of his bag, which he apparently carried everywhere now, opened it to the catalog prefixes, and did not talk. Mara sat with her hands folded, which she did when she was refusing to touch a thing before it was ready. Kade watched the two architecture students leave with their permit and felt the building settle around him the way a structure did when a load came off one side and had not yet found the other. His right hand was on his knee. The last two fingers were still. He noticed he had checked, and that checking had become a habit, and that the habit was the shape of the cost.

The clerk came back with a cart. On it was a reader older than the school's charter, a gray box with a screen the color of weak tea, and beside it a disposition ledger in a paper binder, which she opened on the counter and turned to face them.

"Seven media boxes entered under the precursor identifier." She ran a finger down the column. "Five destroyed. Water. One transferred to a classified repository, and I can't tell you which. One with no disposition." She tapped the line. "It's on the reader because nobody ever wrote down that it shouldn't be. That's the whole of my authority. If it asks for a credential, I don't have one to give you."

"Understood."

"I'll be at the counter."

She went, and stayed within earshot, and Kade did not blame her.

The reader took eleven seconds to draw a page. The first card was not a person. It was a facilities movement order, the kind of form that moved equipment from one dock to another, except that halfway down the equipment stopped and the people started. PERSONNEL TRANSFERRED TO SITE. Four lines blacked out. One not.

MERCER, A. J. SYSTEMS ANALYST.

A date.

He knew the date of the accident the way he knew his own birthday, because they nearly touched, and he did the subtraction, and did it again, and got the same number both times.

"Twenty-three days," he said.

Mara looked at the card. Eli had stopped turning pages.

"He was moved here twenty-three days before he died somewhere else."

Nobody offered him an ordinary explanation. He would have taken one. There were none.

The story had always been the same shape. A private industrial site. A containment failure. Too fast for anyone to do anything. He had been given it at eight, and at nine, and at eleven with the box open on the table, and he had never tested it, because you did not test the floor of the only room you had. And the floor had a movement order in it. His father had been on this rock. He had been here, and then the story had put him somewhere else to die.

"Next card," he said.

Eleven seconds. A roster fragment, unreconciled, most of its names replaced by black bars. One was not.

MERCER, AARON J.

Age at entry: twenty-two.

Civilian technical designation: systems analyst.

Research status: subject, slash, participant.

Aptitude route: 000.

The screen was the color of weak tea and the three digits were the same color as everything else on it, and they were the three digits from a district tablet in Room Four that had frozen a national examination and put officials in the doorway before midnight. He had told Sen, sitting on a floor inside a blue line, that you did not write a failsafe for a thing that had never happened. You wrote it the morning after. He had said thirty-one years. He had not let himself say who the morning after had been for.

His father had not worked near Zero. His father had been the morning after.

Eli said his name. Kade heard it from a distance and did not answer. Mara's hand came to the edge of the counter an inch from his and stopped there, and he saw her stop it, and something in him that had been about to pull away found nothing to pull away from, and was left with its weight on the wrong foot.

"Keep going," he said.

The next card carried a heading. PROJECT CONTINUITY. Under it, a list of file identifiers rather than files, most of them black. Two fields had survived the conversion the way a nail survives a fire.

MEDICAL OVERSIGHT: A. SEN.

CONSENT STATUS: REAUTHORIZED.

"No first name," Eli said, and did not pretend it helped.

Kade read the second field three times. Consent. In the same hour she had first said the word *boundary* to him, Sen had told him that the observations had to come from Eli and not from him, and that if he sent them without asking it would be the first shortcut, and she would know what kind he was from it. He had carried that to the long table under the ladder wall and said it to Eli in her words. He had thought it was a principle. It was a word on a card with her initial beside it, seventeen years old, next to a man whose consent had been re-something-ed by somebody.

"What does reauthorized mean?"

"On a study form? Renewed." Eli's voice was very level. "On a project? It could mean they changed what the project was under him and had him sign again. The word doesn't say. It's built not to."

"Can you open what it points to?"

Eli looked at the card. "It says see governing instrument. That's a repository the reader doesn't have."

The clerk had come back to the end of the counter. "That reference goes through the Director's office," she said. "I can put a request in under this form. It would be on the desk by Thursday. I can't tell you what comes back."

The Director's office. His name on the form. A request that said, in an institution's own handwriting, that Kade Mercer had found the card with her initial on it and wanted the rest.

He thought about it for exactly as long as it deserved, which was longer than he liked.

"No," he said. "Not yet."

Mara's head turned a fraction.

"Somebody hears it before her," Kade said, and did not explain, because Mara already knew, and Eli had worked it out from his face, and the clerk did not need to. "Is there a term index?"

There was. He asked the clerk to run one phrase, and she typed it, because it was her reader.

CONTACT ZERO.

The screen thought about it for longer than eleven seconds and came back with a line in a different font, as though a newer system had reached down into the old one to answer for it.

RESTRICTED SUBJECT TERM. REQUEST DECLINED AT COUNTER.

The clerk read it, and her mouth tightened.

"I have to log that as well."

"I know."

"I'm sorry."

"You've been fair," Kade said, and meant it, and it did nothing.

Eli was writing. Not the cards. The identifiers along the bottom of each, and the hash strings after them, in a column in the back of the binder, in pencil, in a hand as neat as a ledger. Mara photographed nothing. She copied the box number and the date of the movement order onto the export, into the row that had said MERCER, A., and then, with the stylus hovering, she changed the row.

MERCER, AARON J.

A name. Not a letter.

The clerk cleared her throat. Eleven twenty-six. On the door behind her a second notice had gone up while they read, and it said what the first had said in more words, and the words included *visiting officials*.

He thanked her. He signed the reader back in like a man signing for a package. Outside, on the covered way, the wind came off the water hard enough to make the three of them lean, and Kade stood in it with a movement order and a roster fragment in his head and felt, for the first time since he had learned he could do it, the edges of a system assembling around him whose inside he could not see one line of.

"I have to call my mother," he said.

"Now?"

"She hears it first. That was the second thing." He was already walking. "I'm not going to the Spire. I'm going somewhere with a door."

---

Meridian's drying room was in the basement under the equipment cage, six machines and a folding table and a window well that let in a square of gray, and nobody went there before noon because nobody in the history of the House had done laundry before noon. He shut the door. He put his back against the one machine that was running and let it shake him, because it gave the shaking something else to be.

She was on shift. He knew it when he dialed. It rang four times and she answered anyway, in the voice that meant a corridor.

"You're calling in daylight."

"I've got something. You hear it first."

Nothing for a second. Then the corridor noise cut off, and a door closed on her end, and he could picture the supply room at St. Vincent because she had described it once. Shelves of gauze. The one chair nobody was supposed to sit in.

"Four minutes," Elena said. "Then somebody's going to need me. Say what you've got. All of it. Then I'll tell you what I can answer and where I'm choosing not to."

He told her. A form with his name on it. A movement order that put Aaron on this rock twenty-three days before an accident somewhere else. A roster with his father's name and the three digits. Subject slash participant. A project called Continuity. Medical oversight, an initial and a surname. Consent, reauthorized.

He heard her breath change at the initial, and did not stop.

"Mara found the first row. Eli's holding the identifiers. A clerk logged the request. You're hearing it before anyone with an office does. That's both things. I kept both."

"Yes," his mother said. "You did."

"Did he work here."

"Yes."

He had known since the movement order. It still went through him like a step that was not there.

"On what."

"I don't know. Classified. He said Resonance research, and a site, and that it mattered, and after he was gone everyone who came to my door told me I'd already been told more than I should have."

"Who came."

"Two men. Suits. No uniforms. One talked and one walked through the apartment touching things. They said a containment failure at an industrial site, that he'd been supporting a federal partnership, that there were no recoverable remains. They said it with you in the room. They gave me an envelope and a certificate and a bag with his things in it, and I asked the one who talked to say the whole of it again, and he did. Same words. Same order."

"Did you see a body."

"No."

The word cracked. She let it.

"What was in the bag."

"His watch. The pencil. His badge. You've seen those. And a report I have never let you read, because it's under the insert in the box and you never lifted the insert." A pause. "That's mine to have kept. I'm not going to pretend it isn't."

He looked at the square of gray in the window well. Even the box had had a second floor.

"What did the things prove?"

"That somebody had emptied his pockets."

She had had that sentence a long time. He could hear the wear on it.

"Then how did you know it was more than what they said."

"I didn't. At first I believed them. I'm telling you that so you'll know I'm not making myself clever after the fact." The corridor noise came and went behind her door, and she waited for it. "Three weeks later a woman sat in my waiting room for four hours on a night shift with a paper cup she never drank from. She waited for my break. She was younger than I was. She looked like she'd been awake since it happened."

He had known since the card with the initial. He asked anyway, because he needed her to be the one to put it in the air.

"Who."

"Kade."

"Mom."

"Amara Sen."

The machine at his back went into its spin and shook the whole room, and he let it.

"She said the Asterion site. She never once said academy. She said your father had done something that mattered, and that there were people who would use his work if they could reach it, and that the safest thing I could do for you was let the accident stand. She said the file would be watched for anyone who refused the story. She said questions would make attention, and the attention wouldn't be on me." Elena's voice had gone into the register she used for the stories with blood in them. Flat. Exact. "She didn't offer anything. She didn't promise to stop anything. She asked me for silence, and she told me who it was for."

"How did you know she'd known him. Him, not his file."

"She knew he drew a box around any equation he didn't trust. She knew he'd been trying to teach me to read a drawing and I'd been pretending to be worse at it than I was so he'd keep trying. Nobody had that from a file."

"Did you ask if he was alive."

The pause was the wrong length.

"I asked whether she'd been there when he died."

"That's not what I asked you."

"It's what I asked her. It's the question I had." The chair creaked. She had sat in the chair nobody sat in. "She said she'd been there when it ended."

He knew that sentence. He had been handed one exactly like it inside a blue line by the same woman. True along an edge too narrow to stand on.

"And you let it stand."

"I did."

"For seventeen years."

"Yes."

He waited for the defense. There was a place in the conversation shaped for one. She did not put anything in it.

"You've told me my whole life not to leave a room before it's finished," he said.

"I left one for seventeen years. I know exactly what it costs. That's why I say it." The corridor noise again, closer. "I didn't keep it because I thought you couldn't carry it. I kept it because I was twenty-four and I'd lost him and I had a baby with his face, and everyone who came to my door told me that asking would put the baby somewhere I couldn't reach. So I stopped asking. That's not a reason. It's what happened."

The honesty went through the anger the way a justification never would have. He slid down the machine until he was sitting on the concrete with his back against the warm metal, and for a while neither of them said anything, and it was not the silence before a hang-up.

"Was he Resonant," he said at last.

"He could do things nobody could explain to me. I was never told a Standard. I never heard the word Zero until your test."

"What things."

"A bookshelf came off the wall in the apartment once, full, and it should have hit me, and it went sideways instead. He was across the room. He said the shelf had misread the situation. Then he got a nosebleed and blamed the plaster dust, and I let him, because he needed me to."

Kade put two fingers under his nose. Nothing. Only the memory of copper after the floor broke, and Park's card with its four triggers, and his thumb under them.

"Headaches?"

"Near the end. He said the lab lights were bad. I'm a nurse. I knew it wasn't the lights."

"And when mine started."

"Until your test, yours were the headaches of a boy who slept four hours and lived on fries."

"That's still a good diagnosis."

"It's an excellent diagnosis."

The old rhythm held for one exchange. Then somebody knocked on her door, and she said, "One minute," in the voice that made people believe she could hold time still, and came back.

"Do you want the part that isn't evidence?"

He had not known that was a thing she could offer. "Yes."

"He couldn't cook rice. Water and patience, and he distrusted both. He burned the bottom and left the top raw every time, and I let him keep trying because he treated the pot like something he could solve. He hummed doing dishes and never had the tune, and when I told him so he said the tune was a draft. He couldn't stand to be in a photograph unless he was the one holding the camera, which is why there are two of him and forty of me. He fixed things before anyone asked. He'd get you so angry you couldn't see, and then ask one question that proved he'd heard every word, and you'd have to forgive him just to answer it."

She stopped. He waited through the hospital quiet behind her door.

"He'd have asked your questions in the order you asked them," she said. "That's not evidence either. I'm telling you anyway."

His hand tightened on the phone. He had no memory of his father's voice. He had a watch that did not run and a badge too small to read, and now burned rice and a hummed draft of a tune, and it was not enough, and it was more than he had owned at seven that morning. For seventeen years Aaron Mercer had been an absence with a frame around it. Now he was arriving in pieces, and it was a great deal harder to be angry at a man who could not cook rice than at a photograph.

"Don't go to her alone while you're like this," Elena said.

"I know what you're going to say."

"Then I don't need to say it. Say it back."

"Not alone. Not angry."

"And?"

"You hear it first."

"You already did that one. Do it again next time. That's the whole job." The knock came a second time. "I have to go. I'm not hanging up on you. I'm going to a patient."

"Go."

"Kade."

"Go, Mom."

She went. He sat on the concrete with the machine going into its last spin behind him and did not get up until it stopped.

---

Mara was on the basement stairs with her tablet dark in her lap and her hands folded on it. She had not been listening. The door was thick and the machine had been loud, and she would have told him if she had.

"She knew some," he said. "Not all. Sen went to her. Three weeks after. Sat in a hospital waiting room four hours to ask her to keep quiet, and told her it was for me."

Mara's face did the thing it did when a barrier she had been reading turned the other way.

"Then you have the second list," she said.

He had to think a second what she meant. Bay One. Two lists. What they would expect to find if Sen had never met his father. She would have said no at the speed she said everything. She would not have had the word before the floor. There would not be a thirty-one-year-old failsafe in the national examination. And she would not have sat in a waiting room in a city she had no reason to be in, with a paper cup, knowing what Aaron Mercer drew around an equation.

"The second list's empty," he said.

"Yes."

"She lied to me in a doorway."

"Yes."

He sat on the step above her. Not touching. She did not move away.

"I want to walk up the Spire and stand in her office until she says it."

"That sounds like you."

"And I want to be on the next shuttle."

"That also sounds like you." She did not look at him. "You have Mechanics at thirteen hundred."

"My father was a subject in a classified program under this school."

"Yes. And you have Mechanics."

"That's not an answer."

"It isn't meant to be one. It's a room with a professor in it." She stood. "Angry in a room with Amadi is safer than angry in a room with the Director. I'd rather you were somewhere a person can throw you out."

It was irritating enough to work.

---

Amadi had built the afternoon around hidden loads. Of course she had. Each pair had a bench frame the size of a bread box, four gauges wired to a readout, and a cover plate bolted over one bay of the frame. The drawing on the worksheet showed the frame without the plate. The point, she said, was to find the member that was not on the drawing from the numbers alone, because a structure did not care what the drawing said. It carried what it carried.

Kade's partner was a Sol second-year who labeled her gauges before she touched them and slid the worksheet across without one word about the way he looked. "You model. I'll load."

"Fine."

He wrote the frame as drawn. Four members. Two supports. A load at the top. The readings on gauges one, two, and four came out within a few percent of his numbers, and he felt a small mean satisfaction, and then gauge three came in at nearly twice what the drawing allowed, and he stared at it.

"Something behind the plate is taking load," she said.

"I know."

"So model it."

He tried. He drew a fifth member and gave it a stiffness and ran the numbers, and they came out wrong the other way. He took it out. He put it back at a different angle. He was drawing, he realized, a member that was there and not there at once, because he could not decide whether the thing behind the plate was carrying load now or had carried it once and stopped, and the frame did not care about his indecision. Gauge three read what it read.

"Mercer."

He looked up. She was pointing at his page. He had written the same value into two supports.

"That one's a reaction. It's opposite."

"Right."

He fixed the sign and carried the wrong number into the next line anyway, and she caught that too, and said nothing about it, which was worse.

Across the aisle a pair loaded their frame past what the drawing permitted to see what would happen, and a gauge lead pulled free with a snap, and somebody cheered, and Amadi said that instrumentation was not a sport, and the room went quiet for four seconds. In the quiet, Kade's partner loaded their frame by one block and read the gauges and wrote them down, and the numbers were the numbers, ordinary and computable, and everyone else in the room had a structure that did what structures did.

"Do you want to take the plate off?" she said. "Just look?"

That was the shortcut. Look behind the plate, draw what was there, hand in a model that matched, learn nothing.

"No."

"Then you have to say what's behind it from out here."

"I know."

He could not. He kept building the absence into the model, and the model kept telling him the absence was carrying something, and he kept refusing to believe the model over the drawing, and the drawing was a piece of paper somebody had made for a class.

Amadi came down the aisle, looked at his sheet, looked at gauge three, and touched neither.

"Mercer. Gauge three reads more than the drawing permits. What is the drawing missing?"

He looked at the plate. Four bolts. Whatever was behind it had been carrying load through the whole exercise while the paper said there was nothing there.

"Whoever drew it," he said.

Amadi let that sit.

"That is not the mechanical answer."

"No."

"Are you able to continue?"

Quiet. Not for the room. He considered the old answer, the one that had always worked, the one word that had got him out of Room Four and into the hall a hundred times.

"No," he said.

She nodded at the door. "Then go. Your partner will finish the frame. You'll owe her the model."

He went. The permission nearly undid him worse than the movement order had.

---

He walked the island for an hour and did not walk toward the Spire, which was a kind of progress, and then he did.

Not to her office. To the administrative floor, in person, because a scheduling screen could decline him and a person would have to. The reception desk had a person at it and a screen behind the person, and the screen said what the desk was going to say before the desk could say it.

DIRECTOR UNAVAILABLE. SECURITY SESSION.

He looked at it a long time. He suspected her at once, and knew he would have suspected her if the screen had said she was at lunch, and did not know what to do with a suspicion that would have been there regardless of the evidence. Then he thought of a paper form with his name on it and the words *family history*, and a clerk saying *this is logged*, and a second notice going up on a door while he read, and the suspicion found a place to stand.

Taren was on the forum steps when he came out. Not looking for him. Sitting, with a sandwich, in the one place a person leaving the Spire would have to pass.

"You look like a man who's been told Thursday," Taren said.

Kade stopped. "Who told you I'd been told anything?"

"Nobody. It's on your face. Also, the whole administrative floor is Thursday this week." Taren tore the sandwich and held out half, and Kade did not take it. "The Directorate's got people on campus. They come in after a big security event, and everybody fills out forms, and nobody gets to see the Director."

"What Directorate."

The pause was almost nothing. Then the smile, easy, the one that had made forty first-years feel they belonged at a table.

"Continuity Directorate. Policy people. Government. They're harmless and they're slow."

"How do you know they're here?"

"House liaison notices."

"Show me."

Taren's smile stayed where it was. "The notice?"

"You said notices. Plural. Open one."

Taren took his tablet out and scrolled the House channel with the screen tilted so Kade could see it, unhurried. Meal rotations. A closure in the equipment bay. A line about visiting officials that named no agency at all.

EXPECT LIMITED ADMINISTRATIVE ACCESS DURING EXTERNAL SECURITY REVIEW.

"No Directorate," Kade said.

"The liaison briefing was more specific."

"Which briefing."

Taren locked the screen. Half a second late.

"Captains get context."

"You're not a captain."

"I do the scheduling."

Another good answer. Taren had a stock of them, and Kade had spent four months admiring how a person could always know where everyone would be before they got there, and had never once asked how. He had a name in his head now that had not been there this morning, and he tried it, watching Taren's face the way he watched a shoulder before a punch.

"Did the briefing say anything about Project Continuity?"

Taren's eyes went to Kade's pocket, where the folded form receipt was, and came back.

"I don't know what that is."

Slower than the others. Kade filed the speed beside everything else and said nothing about it. Continuity Directorate. Project Continuity. One more line between two things he could not see, and a friend on a step in exactly the right place, with a sandwich, who had known to be there.

"Eat something," Taren said. "You look like you did after Pike."

"I'll eat at the House."

He did not. Eli found him first.

---

The observation deck on the western seawall had nothing between it and the Pacific, and the wind came off the water with no opinion about anybody's father. Eli came up the stair with two bottles and sat on the rail and let a full minute go by, which for Eli was an act of will.

"I put the identifiers in three places," he said finally. "The binder. My band. A page in your notation book, in pencil, on the back of the exercise you got wrong last week, where nobody would look. If any of it comes off that reader, we'll know it was there, and we'll know what it hashed to."

"That's paranoid."

"I prefer audit-conscious." He held out a bottle. Kade took it. "And I want to say a thing I'm going to say badly."

"You say everything badly. It's your best quality."

"I watched you at the counter. When she offered to send it up." Eli looked at the water instead of him. "You said no. I was ready for you to say yes. I'd worked out what I'd do if you said yes, and it was nothing, and I hated that, so I'm telling you I noticed that you didn't."

Kade turned the bottle in his hand.

"It wasn't strength. I promised my mother."

"I know. It still counted. Promises are load-bearing. That's what they're for." He drank. "I can't imagine it. Finding out my dad had a second file. I'm saying that so you'll know I'm not going to pretend I can."

"Neither can I."

"That seems inaccurate, since you're doing it."

"Thank you."

"I'm helping."

He was. The problem was no smaller with Eli on the rail. Kade was less alone inside it, and it was becoming clear that the difference was the whole of what got a person from one hour to the next.

---

The Commons that night went on being the Commons. Sera had her Response paperwork across two chairs and a look that dared anyone to move it. Eli was repairing a sensor array with a spoon in his other hand and had not noticed the spoon. Taren was gone somewhere. Taren's campaign to name the bell rope had outlived Taren's presence in the room, and Sera was refusing to use the name on principle, and the refusal had become a House tradition in under a week.

Kade sat under the ladder wall with the notation book open and did not do notation.

He drew three columns. CLAIM. SOURCE. WRONG IF.

He did not put his father in the first row. He put the people in.

*Elena Mercer was given an accident story and personal effects and never saw a body. Source: her own mouth, on a supply-room chair, today. Wrong if she is lying to me now.* He looked at that one a long time, and wrote *no* in the margin, and went on.

*Amara Sen knew Aaron Mercer and asked for silence three weeks after the story. Source: Elena. A card with an initial. A doorway. Wrong if a different A. Sen sat in a waiting room in that city that month knowing what he drew around an equation.* He wrote nothing in the margin. He did not have to.

*Alaric Vey holds a classification he should not have and reacted to the name. Source: Mara. Wrong if a legitimate contact briefed him. Then who, and why hide it from her.*

*Taren Holt knows where people will be. Source: four months of it. Wrong if he is simply good at it.* He left that one open. It was still possible.

Then the last row, because a table with only the rows you liked was a speech.

*Aaron Mercer died in an industrial accident seventeen years ago.*

Source. He wrote them down. Two men in suits. An envelope. A certificate signed two hundred kilometers away. A photograph captured eleven months before the thing it was supposed to show. A young woman with a paper cup asking for silence. A movement order that put him on this rock twenty-three days before.

Wrong if.

He sat with the pencil above the column and could not fill it. Not because there was nothing to write. Because every source that said the story was true was the same source wearing different clothes, and the only thing he could put in the third column was *wrong if all of them are lying*, and that was not a test. That was the claim.

His band lit. Elena.

A photograph. Her kitchen table, the one where things in that house got fixed. The black box, lid off. The cardboard insert lifted and set aside. Under it, a brown government envelope, the seal across the flap unbroken.

*When you come home. We open it together. Not before. Not alone.*

He read it until the screen dimmed, and turned it back up, and read it again.

Mara sat down beside him. She looked at the table in the notation book, and at the empty third column, and at the photograph, and did not say it would be all right, and did not touch him, and did not leave.

"You can't fill it," she said.

"No."

"That's not a failure of the table."

"I know what it is." He looked up at the ladder wall, where his line read MERCER, KADE. 1,904, and the number had been enormous yesterday and was a joke tonight. "It's a system that can measure my footwork to four places, and it either never thought to tell me who my father was, or it thought about it and decided. And there's no column for which."

She said nothing. Across the room Eli had found the spoon and was looking at it with real surprise.

He had spent his whole life able to see the edges of a system from inside it. That was the one thing he had. He sat under the wall with a sealed envelope on a screen and a professor's frame that carried a load the drawing did not admit, and his mother's voice saying *I was there when it ended* in another woman's mouth, and could not tell, for the first time he could remember, which of them he was standing inside. The school. The story. The family. The card with an initial on it. All of them had a piece. None of them had the whole. And every one of them, when he leaned his weight on it, gave a little.

He closed the notation book on the empty column and did not go to the Spire, and did not go to the shuttle, and did not decide, and knew that not deciding was going to hold for exactly one night.

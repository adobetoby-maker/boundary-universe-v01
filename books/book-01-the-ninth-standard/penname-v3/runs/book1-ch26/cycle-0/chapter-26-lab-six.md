# Chapter 26 — Lab Six

Internal transit was running, but not for them.

The board over the east engineering platform said the next car was held at the Spire, nine minutes. Sera looked along the platform. It had one camera, two dark tunnel mouths, and nobody on it. She turned round.

"We wait in the hall."

"The hall's empty too," said Eli. "Midyear projects closed out two days ago. It's us and a cleaning drone."

"The hall has eleven cameras. I counted on the way through."

Taren flinched. Sera saw it.

"So that somebody can see us," she said. "Not so that you can."

"I know what you meant."

"Good."

They went back up the ramp into the central hall of the engineering wing. It was long and wide, with a polished floor and laboratory glass down both sides, and most of the glass was dark. The drone was working along the far wall. They had passed Lab Six on the way to the platform without looking at it. They passed it again now and stopped farther on, outside Lab Five, because Eli had stopped.

He was looking through the glass at something on the center table.

It was a frame of four jointed braces, half assembled, lying around a hub as though somebody had taken a small bridge apart and begun teaching it to crawl. Colored tape ran along the braces. There was blue at the feet and yellow on the telescoping arms. One hinge had been wrapped in red.

"That's yours," Kade said.

"Most of it."

"Which part isn't?"

"The table."

Eli needed both hands. He passed Taren's dead tablet to Sera without looking at either of them and woke the project display beside the door. A model opened in the air over his own tablet, the black one with the cracked corner. Two broken ends of roadway hung there with a gap between, and the frame unfolded underneath like a metal hand.

Kade understood what Eli was doing. Eli had spent the last hour finding out that a friend had been filing reports on him. He had nine minutes, and he was spending them on the one thing in the building that was entirely his.

"Emergency bracing pushes against whatever's still standing," Eli said. "So the damaged structure has to hold up the rescue gear as well as itself. Mine doesn't ask it to." He turned the model. Four anchor feet walked out past the fracture line and bit into sound ground. "The load goes out here, to something that isn't already broken."

Mara leaned in. "You're redrawing the boundary of the supported structure."

"I am using normal engineering words."

"You're doing it with steel."

"Yes. Like a civilized person."

Kade looked at the little bridge. The frame did not mend anything. It made a path across the damage by deciding which pieces no longer had to carry themselves. He thought that was worth remembering, and did not say so.

Sera put a finger on one of the load points. "What does it weigh?"

"Thirty-eight kilos."

"Too heavy."

"For you?"

"For somebody who's already wearing armor and carrying a medical pack and a line kit and possibly a person." She did not say it unkindly. She was not arguing with the machine. She was standing it in a stairwell in the rain with the lights out, to see how it did.

"It's a two-person carry," said Eli.

"Then it costs you two people before it's saved one."

"City crews work in teams."

"City crews turn up short. Every time."

Eli's hand stopped over the display. Then he opened his notes. "What number makes it useful?"

"Twenty-five. Twenty, if you want anyone to love you."

"Twenty is an insult to physics."

"Physics isn't hauling it up six flights."

He wrote it down. RESPONSE REVIEW, the line said. MASS. ONE-PERSON DRAG CONFIGURATION. He looked up again, and Kade saw that he was nearly enjoying himself. "What else?"

Sera took the question seriously. "Your feet assume level ground."

"They articulate."

"On a lab floor. Put one of them half in mud and half on loose concrete." He made a second note. "What are the controls?"

"Physical toggles. You can work them in gloves."

"Good. How does a firefighter read it?"

"The lattice shows its own status. The color shifts with load."

"And does the firefighter know which color means stop, or do they find out?"

That made a third note. Mara reached past them and selected the red hinge. "What happens when this goes?"

"If."

"When."

Eli gave her a wounded look and ran the fault.

One anchor folded. The roadway dropped twelve centimeters and hung there on the three braces that were left. Red warning bands ran out along the frame, and two arrows lit on the deck, pointing off it.

"It fails in stages," Eli said. "It doesn't let go all at once. You get a warning, and you get a way off."

"Your success demonstration is the bridge breaking," said Kade.

"Everything breaks. The engineering is in how much notice you get and where the damage goes."

Sera studied the arrows. "Make those show through smoke."

He made a fourth note. The model hung over his hand, sagging and lit red, and it was still holding. Then he reset it.

The display chimed. FELLOWSHIP DEMONSTRATION SLOT, it said, FRIDAY, 09:20. Eli swiped it away a little too fast.

"You're thinking about that tonight?" Kade said.

"I am trying to think about literally anything else. My mother says compartmentalizing is either a life skill or a future therapy bill. She works out which after the invoice comes."

"She sounds practical."

"She raised me."

"So there's evidence both ways."

Eli almost smiled. He put his palm against the locked door of Lab Five. "If I get it, I can use Systems Lab fabrication without a faculty sponsor. I could build version eight out of things a county can actually afford."

"Build it," said Sera. "Response will break it for you properly."

He brightened. "That is the nicest thing you have ever said to me."

"It wasn't a compliment."

"It is in engineering."

Kade stood a little apart and found he liked being unnecessary. In September Eli had been his roommate. By November he had been his friend, the first one at Asterion who had wanted nothing out of it. Now he had a panel on Friday, and opinions about county procurement, and a whole future that had nothing to do with Kade. It made the friendship seem less like something Kade could be said to own.

Taren had not come up to the glass. In October he would have been in the middle of it, claiming diplomatic immunity from physics or offering to steal Eli a lighter alloy from Kepler. Now he stood back by the corridor junction with his hands where people could see them.

"It was Thursday," Eli said, frowning at where the notice had been. "They had me down for Thursday."

"They moved it this morning," said Taren. "The panel chair had a conflict."

Nobody spoke. Taren heard himself a moment after everyone else did.

Eli turned round. "That wasn't posted. They sent it to me. Privately."

"It was on the engineering calendar by noon."

"You read the engineering calendar."

"I read everything. It was the job."

"Were they reading my project traffic?" Fear and anger did not make Eli look older. They made him look about fourteen. "Did your people have my models?"

"I never gave them your models."

"That isn't what I asked you."

"I know. I don't know what they could reach."

"You always know."

Taren took that without defending himself. "Not this."

The cleaning drone had worked its way along the wall to them. It stopped, because five people were standing in its path, and its amber light began to blink. Nobody moved for it. After a while Taren stepped back against the glass, and it hummed through the place where he had been standing.

It was a ridiculous thing to give way to. Kade understood why he did it anyway.

Sera checked the time. "The car's in two minutes. Walk."

---

Lab Six lay between them and the platform. Kade knew the room mainly because Eli complained about it.

It held the thermal-transfer hardware and the coolant plant, and an old experimental Conduit test bed. The test bed had been waved through three generations of safety standards, because replacing it would have cost enough to make the administration discover tradition. Eli called it the museum that could explode.

The museum stood on two levels behind one long wall of reinforced glass. The upper level was a teaching floor, with worktables and tool cages and a control booth in one corner, and it looked down over the rig. Yellow stairs went down either side of the rig to the lower chamber, where the coolant lines ran among fat shielded pipes. A narrow service spine went along the east wall. On the west side there was a pressure door wide enough for equipment carts.

Three people were still at work. Two students in gray lab coats were winching a transfer ring down from the ceiling of the teaching floor. Below them a technician in faculty gray was crouched at an open panel. One of the students saw Eli and lifted a hand, and Eli raised his tablet to her.

"They machined the red hinge for me," he said.

"The one you don't trust."

"That's why I trust them."

The lights on the teaching floor went down to their evening setting. Over the east access door, a few steps ahead, a small status lamp showed amber. It went green, and then amber again.

Eli stopped. He did not slow down first, and Kade had gone three steps beyond him before he noticed.

"What is it?"

Eli was watching the maintenance panel beside the door. A diagnostic light no bigger than a grain of rice was doing the same thing as the lamp. It went amber, green, amber.

"That's wrong," he said.

Sera came back. "How wrong?"

"It isn't broken wrong." He went closer. "It's watched wrong."

The skin tightened on Kade's arms. Behind him Taren had lost his color.

"Explain that," said Mara.

Eli already had a network map up. "I've been seeing phantom maintenance pings out of this lab. I thought it was an orphaned monitor. Some old service program asking whether Lab Six still exists. Half this island is new systems pretending the old ones are dead, so it happens constantly."

"For how long?" said Kade.

"Weeks."

"Weeks?"

"I had eighteen unexplained nodes on my list, Kade, and this one was the dull one. I was going to bring it up after we'd stopped committing archive crimes." His fingers were moving faster. "But these aren't requests. They're acknowledgments. Something in there is reporting its status. And tonight it's doing it three times as often as it did last week."

"Who's it reporting to?" said Sera.

"That's the interesting question."

"It's a Directorate overlay," Taren said. He had taken a step toward the panel. He stopped well short of it and put his hands behind his back. "I don't know this one. I know the kind. It's a passive status mirror. If they've got into a facility they don't need to control it. They only need to know what state it's in."

"What for?" said Mara.

"So they can move when it's weak."

The little light went red.

The joke went out of Eli's face completely. Kade was frightened before he knew what he was frightened of.

"What?"

Eli pulled the thermal network up large. One branch of it had gone orange, and it turned red as they watched. "That valve is not supposed to be open."

"What happens if it stays open?" said Sera.

"Coolant pressure builds in the lower chamber."

"How long have they got?"

He looked at the figures. "Not long."

"Then move," said Taren.

Eli did not move. He opened a service channel instead, and Kade knew the ugly gray interface. It was the back-room network Eli used for the drone tests that had officially never happened. A list of channels came up. Most of them had names. One said only NULL-7.

"What's that one?" Kade asked.

"Null seven. It's a dead channel. It's a legacy maintenance route, and nothing's supposed to monitor it. I send test packets down it so they don't clutter the live logs."

Sera stared at him. "You have an unmonitored network on this campus."

"I have a route that's supposed to be unmonitored."

He sent one packet down null seven. It went, and nothing came back, which seemed to be what he expected. He sent the same packet through the lab's ordinary network. A reply came back at once. Half a second later a second reply came back from somewhere else.

Eli went still. "Someone's mirroring the status traffic. Everything this lab reports, something outside is hearing too."

"Directorate," said Taren.

"It could be anyone."

"Eli."

"Fine. It's very probably the Directorate."

Inside, one of the students was looking up at the lamp over the door. It had gone to steady amber. Eli hit the wall intercom.

"Lab Six, clear the floor."

Static answered him. He tried again. "This is Navarro, outside east access. Your lower coolant valve is open."

The nearer student went to the panel on her side. Her voice came through thin and broken. "Panel shows that valve closed."

Eli turned his tablet to the glass so that she could see the red branch. "Your panel is lying to you. Go to the west pressure door. Now."

They went at once, both of them. Down in the lower chamber the technician had straightened up. The shielding kept the intercom from reaching him. The student pointed down at him and then at the stairs, with her whole arm. He looked at her, and then he bent and checked his own panel instead.

The lights in the lower chamber went from white to blue.

Eli swore. Kade had never heard him use the word.

"What is it?"

"Automatic stabilization field. The system thinks the pressure's already out of tolerance."

Mara was looking down through the glass. "Does the field protect the people in there?"

"It keeps the coolant's properties stable."

"I didn't ask about the coolant."

"No," Eli said. "It doesn't."

Sera slapped the emergency alarm beside the door. Nothing happened. She hit it again and got nothing. "The local alarm's been cut off."

Taren was already turning toward the far end of the hall. Sera's arm came out across his chest. "You tell me before you touch a system."

He stopped dead. "The building alarm is at the central junction. It's on a separate network. I'd send the alarm and the hazard code and nothing else."

She held his eyes for half a second. "Go."

He ran west up the hall. Kade had never seen him run flat out.

Mara had the emergency plan off the public wall display, and the lab opened in the air above it. "The west pressure door lets out into this hall. The lower service stair comes up on the east side."

"Not once the field's up," said Eli. "The stair becomes part of the chamber seal."

Inside, the students had reached the west door, and it had not opened. One of them was hammering the release. The other looked back across the whole width of the lab at Eli.

"There's a manual wheel," he said into the intercom. "Right side of the frame."

She found it and threw her weight on it. It did not turn.

The pressure figure jumped. Eli's tablet offered a projection of ninety seconds. It changed that to seventy-six, and then it took the number away altogether.

"Leave the door," Eli said. "Go up to the control booth. It's got its own seal."

She pointed down through the floor. "He won't leave the controller."

Eli looked at the man in the blue light. His mouth worked once.

"Then you can't help him from where you are. Move."

Kade watched what it cost him to say that. He said it anyway, and the students ran for the booth.

The technician had finally looked up. He saw the students running, and then he saw Eli at the glass. Eli pointed along the east wall, toward the local service control. The man looked where he pointed and shook his head. He spread his hands. He could not reach it from his side.

Eli understood him. So did Kade.

Eli shut the tablet. "Run," he said, and nobody argued.

---

The first blast was not loud. It was pressure.

The floor of the hall rose under Kade's feet as if the building had breathed in. He hit the wall shoulder first, and all the air went out of him. The sound arrived a second later. It was deep and metallic and wrong. Then every alarm in the east wing went off at once, and Kade understood that Taren had reached the junction.

Ahead of them a pressure door had started down out of the ceiling. It was a thick gray slab with a narrow strip of view port across it at head height, and it was not coming down slowly.

Sera went under it in a roll and came up on her feet. Mara followed, low and fast. Kade threw himself flat and slid, and felt the edge of it comb the back of his jacket.

He got to his knees and turned round.

Eli had stopped on the far side, four meters short. He had the tablet open again and he was staring into it.

"Eli!"

"The chamber's venting!"

"So get through!"

"If it vents up into the teaching floor, it takes this whole level!"

The gap under the door was half a meter and shrinking. Eli looked at his service map. He looked at the three of them. Kade watched a decision settle over his face, and it made Eli look like someone he had not met.

"No," Kade said.

"Kade."

"No. Get under the door."

"I can isolate the upper vent from the local service control. It's on the east wall, by the spine."

"Then let the response crews do it."

"The controller's compromised. It's rejecting every remote command. Somebody has to stand in front of it."

Sera dropped to one knee at the gap. Her voice was completely level. "How long until it vents upward?"

Eli checked. "Ninety seconds. Maybe."

"Can a response team be standing at that control in ninety seconds?"

He did not answer her, and that was the answer.

Kade went down flat on the floor. The gap was thirty centimeters now. Through it he could see Eli's shoes and the bottom edge of the tablet and, when Eli crouched, his face.

Kade wanted to say something that would work. *Don't be stupid. Wait. I need you.* He tried each one and heard what it was. Every one of them was an order. Ten minutes ago he had stood by a lab door and been glad that Eli's life was bigger than the part with Kade in it. It was the worst possible moment to find out he had meant it.

Eli gave him a frightened smile.

"If I get the fellowship, tell them this counts as practical experience."

"Eli."

"I know."

"Don't you—"

The door met the floor and cut the sentence off.

Kade was up and at the view port. Eli was already running, back down the hall toward Lab Six, with the tablet held out in front of him like a lamp.

"*Eli!*" He hit the door with both palms. It did not so much as ring.

Mara was at the emergency panel in the wall. "Manual override's locked out."

Taren came pounding down the hall from the junction with his dead credential already in his hand. He slapped it against the reader and nothing happened. He did it again.

"It's burned," he said. "I burned it."

Kade rounded on him. "Then unburn it."

"I can't."

"Try harder!"

The wall shook a second time. Behind the view port the hall went white, and then gray, and then there was only smoke rolling against the glass. Eli was somewhere in it.

Kade reached for all of it before he had decided to.

The cold line drew itself behind his eyes. The door and its frame were there, and the anchors that held the frame into the wall, and the wall. Beyond them was pressure. There was a great deal of it, leaning on everything. He felt where it went. He could feel a route forming, the way one formed on a mat when an opponent's weight came forward. The push from the chamber could go into the seal frame and out through the anchors into the structure. He could change where that force came to rest. He could open this.

He had opened a floor once, in November. He had not aimed that either.

A hand closed round his wrist. "Kade," said Sera.

"I can open it."

"You don't know that."

"He's in there."

"I know he is."

"I can—"

"You are standing next to a damaged thermal lab full of unstable field hardware, and you have never once done this on purpose." She did not raise her voice. She did not have to. "If you guess wrong, you make it worse. For him first."

The line sharpened until it hurt. There was smoke behind the glass. Eli had perhaps sixty seconds and a control panel to reach. Every part of Kade wanted the power to mean that he never again had to stand and wait for somebody else. He looked at that wish. It was the reason he could not be trusted with the door.

She was holding his wrist. She could not hold the other thing. Only he could put that down.

He let it go.

The pain stayed behind his eyes anyway, a thin bright wire of it, and when he looked at his hands they had a tremor in them that they had not had in the tunnel. That was the price of only reaching.

Sera let go of his wrist. "Good," she said.

He hated her for about three seconds, because she was right.

---

The response teams took fifty-four seconds to arrive. Kade counted each of them.

The hall did its best to become a disaster before they came. The alarm emptied two evening study rooms into it at once. A teaching assistant pulled open a stairwell door and let out a wall of smoke, and reeled back from it coughing. One shutter on the north corridor had jammed halfway down. Everybody coming from that direction was being funneled straight toward the sealed pressure door, which was the worst place in the building to stand.

Sera climbed onto a bench.

"Turn round! Central stairs only! Walk to the corner, and run after it if you have to!"

For two seconds nobody listened to her. She jumped down, tore a fire hood out of its wall case, and pulled it over the teaching assistant's head, and tightened the seal. Then she pointed at the two nearest students. "You two. He goes between you. Central stairs. Tell the next floor down that the east wing is closed."

They did it. After that everybody did what she said.

Mara stood at the wall plan with her tablet and crossed off corridors as their shutters reported shut. "North junction's clear. Two rooms on this level still show someone inside."

"Kade," said Sera.

He looked at her.

"Room sweep. There's a blue line painted across the floor up there. Everything beyond it shares a wall with the lab. You stay on this side of it. You do not open anything that's warm."

It was an order. He could have wept with gratitude for it.

The first study room was empty. The chairs were shoved back and the tablets had been left where they lay, and a cup was rolling in a slow circle on the floor. He looked under every table anyway.

The second room was the last before the blue line, and the line ran across its doorway. A first-year was folded up against the instructor's station inside, with both hands over his ears.

Kade crouched at the threshold. "Can you walk?"

The boy nodded and did not move.

"That's useful in theory. Try it physically."

The boy's eyes found him. He got one foot underneath himself. Kade put out a hand to him across the doorway, and stopped it. The metal door frame had a faint dull flush to it, like a stove ring a minute after it is switched on. It was warm.

He took the emergency pole off its wall clips instead and ran it out across the threshold. The boy grabbed it. Kade drew him in, hand over hand, until he could get hold of a sleeve instead of skin, and hauled. They were two steps into the corridor when a ceiling panel came down in the doorway behind them.

The boy stared at it. Kade turned him by the shoulders toward the central hall. "Keep going. Don't stop to be impressed."

When he got back, Mara had found one more signal, in a storage alcove. She read it twice. "It's a heat sensor. There's nobody there."

Taren was standing beside the junction alarm station with his hands held away from it.

"What did you send?" said Sera.

"The building alarm and the hazard code."

"Show her."

He turned the station's log toward Mara. She read every line of it before she nodded. Trust was being put together tonight out of one checked action after another. Kade did not know whether a friendship could be rebuilt that way. It had been enough to clear a corridor.

Then the hall was full of responders in sealed armor, and Sera was in among them. She gave them what she had in the order they needed it. She gave the number of people inside and where they had last been seen, the hazards of the lab, and the way Navarro had run. She told them the remote controls could not be trusted. She had not grown calmer under the emergency lights. She had grown more exact. Kade had known what she wanted to do with her life. He had not seen her do it before.

An officer pushed a portable map at her. "Which way in?"

She did not hesitate. "Service spine C. Stay off the west stair. The pressure difference will shut your team in."

He looked at her identification. "Are you Response Corps?"

"Candidate."

He nodded once and sent his people in by spine C.

Another of them said, to no one in particular, "Who's on the local controller?"

"Navarro went for it," said Kade.

The visor turned to the sealed door. "A student? What's his training?"

"Systems engineering."

Nothing showed in the faceplate. "Then if he got there, he may have bought us time."

Kade held on to that. It said *may have*, and *bought time*. It did not say dead.

Somebody put a casualty slate into his hands. "You know the missing student? Fill in what you can. Medical alerts, what he was wearing, comm ID."

The slate showed him a form. Kade knew what Eli ate for breakfast. He knew his three worst songs, and the face he made when code compiled the first time. He did not know his blood type. He stood looking at the empty box until Mara reached round him and entered Eli's student identifier, and the record filled itself in. It said no medication conflicts and no field implant, and standard lab coat, gray.

He selected LAST KNOWN CONSCIOUS. Under distinguishing equipment he wrote: *Black tablet, cracked upper left corner. Orange tool roll.* He nearly added *talks when frightened*. There was no box for that.

Mara stayed beside him. She did not touch him, and she did not move away.

She took the antistatic sleeve out of her inside pocket then, and looked at it, and looked at Sera. Sera fetched an evidence bag from the responders' station. The sleeve went into the bag. Mara signed across the seal and Sera signed under her, and on the tag Mara wrote that the contents were for Director Sen's hand and nobody else's.

"They may be able to trace who touched this lab," Taren said. "From the headers. I can translate the priority codes. That offer's still open."

"Write that on the tag," Sera said to Mara. She did not say it to him.

He did not tell them he knew the system better than incident command did. He did not ask them to trust him. He went back across the hall and sat down on the floor under a dead alarm lamp. Kade had never seen Taren sit by himself when there were other people to sit with.

*Sen receives it with all of us in the room*, Mara had said in the tunnel. There had been five of them then.

Kade did not ask Taren to come nearer, and he did not tell him to go. Each of those took effort.

At seventeen minutes the first team brought out the two students from the teaching floor. One of them was walking between two responders with an oxygen mask on. The other came on a rigid litter, under a clear hood, with one hand clenched shut on nothing. Kade knew her. She was the one who had thrown herself on the wheel.

The walking one dragged his mask down. "Navarro?"

"He's still inside," a responder said.

The student shook his head hard. "He got us the booth seal. The intercom died, and then the booth screen started giving us directions. It told us where to stand and when to get down." He coughed. "It said not to wait for the lower chamber. It said it twice."

"Eli sent that?" said Kade.

"Who else would have?" A medic pressed the mask back over his face and moved Kade out of the way.

So Eli had stopped on his way to the controller and put two people somewhere they could survive. He had not rushed at it blindly. He had been working.

Knowing it made hoping worse.

At thirty-three minutes they brought out the technician. His coat had been cut off him and one arm was bound across his chest. Coolant frost covered his boots in a blue-white skin that did not melt under the corridor lights. He heard the name through his hood.

"Navarro reached east control."

Kade was beside the litter. "Was he there? Did you see him?"

"On the chamber status board."

"Was he alive?"

"He closed the upper vent."

"And after that?"

The man's lips moved. A medic's hand came down on Kade's shoulder and steered him back. "After that the cameras failed," she said. "That's all he's got. Let him go."

It was not an answer, and it was the only one anybody had.

The first team did not go back in. The joints of their armor were scored white where the field had tried to hold metal and coolant in the same state. A second team took over their tether lines while incident command redrew the map. East control went red and the service spine went yellow. The lower chamber stayed black. Kade listened to the talk around the map. He heard field persistence and thermal inversion and structural delamination and unreliable telemetry. He understood each phrase, and he could not make them say *alive*.

At thirty-eight minutes the response commander ordered the second team out.

Sera went straight to her. "They've got four minutes of air left."

"Their suits are losing seal."

"The controller's under thirty meters past where they're standing."

"Through a chamber my instruments can't read." The commander looked at her properly for the first time. "Candidate. What's the first priority of rescue?"

Sera's hand closed hard on the edge of her comm. Kade watched her make it open again.

"Don't create more casualties."

"Then let them come out."

Sera looked at the smoked glass. Everything in her was leaning the other way, and Kade knew it because he had been there half an hour before. She keyed the comm. "Team Two, confirm your withdrawal route."

"Spine C. Ninety seconds."

She shut her eyes once. "Your route is clear."

So that was response work as well. Part of it was knowing when not to send people any farther. Kade hated that too.

At forty-one minutes a responder came out alone with his helmet scorched down one side, and Kade knew before he spoke.

"The lower lab's gone. The chamber's flooded with field-stabilized coolant. We can't trust thermal."

"Navarro?" said Mara.

He looked at his list. "Not located."

Kade took hold of those two words. They were not the word dead.

"And the local controller?" said Sera.

"Closed." The helmet turned to her. "Somebody isolated the upper vent by hand before the chamber failed."

Kade had stopped breathing. "That was Eli."

"We don't know who it was."

"It was him."

The man did not argue. "Then he may have stopped a much bigger release. This level's still here." He turned to the map. "We can't put a search into that chamber until the field decays."

"How long is that?"

"We don't know."

"Is it hours?"

"It could be."

"Days?"

He paused before he answered, and the pause told Kade what the answer was. "It could be."

Kade looked at the black square on the map. Somewhere inside it a line had closed on a status board, and it said UPPER VENT ISOLATED. Eli's last decision could be seen, and nothing that came after it could.

---

The official notice took two hours to post, and the two hours were a torment of their own.

Meridian's common room filled without anybody calling a meeting. People who had barely known Eli came down and sat. Nessa brought food, and no one touched it. Priya walked from the window to the door and back. Anton Bell mended the same training strap three times.

The House turned itself into a waiting room by instinct. The long dining table became an information desk. Someone put the engineering-wing incident feed on the wall display, and someone else switched off the comments when strangers began arguing under it about sabotage. First-years carried water up to the people who could not face the room. An upper-year wrote the response center's number on a board, large enough that no one had to ask for it twice. Nobody was in charge. The work found people who could do it.

Kade stood by the wall display. The casualty page was blank. Eli's bed was eight floors over his head, in Room 814, and he could feel where it was like a point on a map.

His phone rang, and it was his mother. He declined the call and texted her straight away. *I'm alive. Eli's missing. Can't talk yet.*

*I love you*, she sent back. *I'm here.*

She did not send a demand or any advice. He put the phone away.

An administrator came with a communications officer and asked for Mercer, Vale, Vey and Holt. Nobody asked for Julian Cross. He came down from the upper floors and walked in with them anyway, and nobody quite had the nerve to stop him.

They used Meridian's strategy room, because the interview rooms were inside the closure. The search map covered one wall. Four areas of it were green and two were amber, and the lower chamber was black.

The communications officer laid a draft on the table. LABORATORY INCIDENT, it said. ONE STUDENT UNACCOUNTED FOR.

"His name is Eli Navarro," said Kade.

"We can't release a name until the family has been notified."

"Has anyone notified them?"

The officer glanced at the administrator. "Student Services is confirming the contact details."

Three hours ago Eli had stood outside Lab Five and made a joke about his mother and a therapy bill. Now an office was confirming that she existed.

Kade stood up. "Give me the number."

"You can't make a casualty notification."

"You haven't established a casualty."

"Exactly."

The room was quiet. When the administrator spoke, he was gentler than Kade wanted him to be.

"Mercer. Suppose she hears it from you, before our liaison gets to her. What are you going to say?"

Kade opened his mouth. *He ran into the lab. He saved three people. He might be alive. He might not.* Each sentence either hid something or dressed the uncertainty up as hope. He sat down again.

"Then tell her his name. Don't let it be the last thing your form finds out."

"I can do that," the administrator said, and wrote it down.

Sera was at the map. "Why is the west teaching floor green? That pressure door never opened."

He looked at his notes. "We recovered two occupants from the booth."

"Then mark the route they came out by. Don't mark the room. People are going to read this to find out what was actually searched."

The communications officer began to object. The administrator changed the map. The green shrank to a thin line from the booth to the service spine, and most of Lab Six went black again. It was less comforting, and it was more honest.

Mara asked for the timestamp on the mirrored status packet. Taren asked whether the incident team had isolated the routing signature. Each question went into its own evidence queue. Kade had only one question.

"When do you go back in?"

The administrator looked at the black square. "When the response commander believes another person can go in there without adding a name to that notice."

Kade wanted to hate him. The man looked too tired for it to work.

The notice went live without a name. Forty minutes later it vanished from the page, and that was worse.

Mara sat down on the floor beside him with her back to the wall. "Drink something."

"No."

"I wasn't asking."

"You've been spending too much time with Sera."

"Probably." She handed him a cup of water, and he took it.

Taren stood on the far side of the room, alone. No one had told him to leave, and no one had made room for him. It was only a few hours since the archive, and already the House had felt it. For once he did not try to mend the room by talking.

The casualty page updated. Every person in Meridian went still.

Five injured. Two critical. One missing, presumed dead.

ELIAS NAVARRO.

Kade read the name until it stopped looking like language.

No body had been recovered. The notice explained that the collapse had breached the thermal chamber and flooded the lower level with a coolant built to stay stable under field stress. The property that had kept the blast from being larger also made a normal recovery impossible until the system cooled. It was technical and clean, and perhaps it was meant to be merciful.

Behind him somebody began to cry. He did not turn round to see who.

There was one more line. INCIDENT STATUS: CONTAINED. EXTERNAL ACCESS UNDER INVESTIGATION.

*External access* ought to have made him angry. He had no room for anger, because the name took up all of him.

But under the notice the display offered an evidence link, and his hand went out to it. Mara's fingers closed round his wrist before the packet list could open.

"Not here."

"They were watching that lab."

"We know they were."

"The destination could still be live."

"Which is exactly why you don't open it on the House network."

Taren had come halfway across the room. He stopped outside their circle. "I can identify the routing format."

Every head near them turned. The room had stopped being quiet with grief and become hostile.

He kept his hands down at his sides. "I can't tell you the destination. I might be able to tell you which Directorate office issued the mirror."

Kade looked from Eli's name to Taren's face. Here was something to do. It was a route, and beside it, close enough to reach, stood a person to blame. His whole body tried to make that into relief. He felt it happen, and he knew it for what it was. He had felt the same thing at the pressure door. He wanted not to have to stand and wait.

And under it was the other thing, the one he had not yet said aloud. *They already know you found the twelfth name*, Taren had said. Kade had found it. He had gone down into the archive to find more. Six minutes' walk from that archive, a lab had been opened like a trap at the moment the five of them were walking past it. He could not get round that. They had all been standing by that glass because of him.

Mara was still holding his wrist. She was not restraining him. She was waiting to see what he did.

He took his hand back from the display. "The evidence team has it."

"They may not know what they're reading," said Taren.

"Then tell them."

"They may be Directorate."

"Then tell Sen."

"Kade—"

"I can't make Eli less dead by solving you tonight."

It landed harder than he had meant it to. Taren stepped back. He did not say *presumed*. He did not produce an apology big enough that Kade would have had to comfort him for it.

"No," he said. "You can't."

He went back to his side of the room.

Mara let go. "You could have hit him."

"He might have preferred it."

"Probably."

"I might have."

"Probably."

Kade looked at the link. The archive had taught him that an old record could rearrange a life. Lab Six had taught him that a system could lie to your face while people were standing inside it. He wanted to tear through every packet until the lie had a name. He knew that was not the same as wanting the truth. Tonight it would only be a way of running away while looking useful.

Across the room Priya had begun dividing the untouched food into sealed containers. Anton had put the strap down and was writing a list of everyone who had gone upstairs alone. Nessa muted the incident feed and left Eli's notice where it was. Nobody asked Kade what the House ought to do. They did what they could without him. He felt relief at that and pain at it, and the two came together.

"Drink the water," said Mara.

He drank it.

---

He ended up on the stone steps outside Meridian, because going in properly meant going up, and up was Room 814.

Mara sat beside him. Ten meters off, Sera was still walking up and down and answering questions from response supervisors. She had become useful enough that no one had thought to tell her she could stop. One of them put a card into her hand.

"Response assessment office. When this settles, call them."

She looked at it as if it were in a foreign language. "This isn't the time."

"No, it isn't. Keep it anyway."

She put it in her pocket without looking at it again.

Julian came out after a while. He stood in front of Kade for some time, and then he sat down on the step below him. Rank One was sitting on the pavement in the cold, and neither of them cared.

Kade spoke to the ground. "If I hadn't gone into that archive—"

"No," Julian said.

Kade looked up. Julian's face was tired, and it was angry, though not with Kade.

"You don't get to make yourself the center of every bad thing that happens."

"They targeted us. They knew what I'd found, and they knew where we were. That lab went off while we were walking past it."

"Probably."

"Eli was there because of me."

"Eli was standing outside the lab because of you. Eli was *in* it because Eli made a decision."

Kade nearly snapped at him. Julian went on before he could.

"You can answer for your own choices without laying claim to everyone else's."

"You sound like my mother."

"My father would tell you the opposite." Julian's jaw had set. "He thinks leadership means every failure within reach of you is yours. All of it."

"And you don't think that?"

"I used to." He looked across at the dark engineering wing. One red light was still turning on its roof. "It makes you feel important. And it means you can never trust another person with a decision, because every decision's already yours."

Kade thought of Eli's face under the door, and how the decision had settled on it. He had hated that decision. He had not been offered a vote on it. He had been glad, earlier in the hour, that Eli's life was not his property. He could not have that while Eli was alive and take it back now.

He knew that. He could feel himself knowing it. But under it the arithmetic had not moved, and he could not make it move. If he had never looked for the twelfth name, Eli would be upstairs complaining about protein powder. Julian's sentence was true. His own was true as well, and he could not make one cancel the other.

Eli would have given them each a column in a spreadsheet. That made it worse. Kade looked away.

Across the courtyard Sera had finally stopped moving. She put both hands on the back of a bench and bowed her head.

Mara reached over and took Kade's hand. It was not romantic, and she did not try to make it look accidental. He let her.

He had spent months finding out that needing people made a life larger. Nobody had told him the other half, which was that a larger life could lose more.

His phone buzzed. *I heard*, his mother had written. *Call when you can. I love you.*

For once he did not answer with a joke. *I love you too. I can't talk yet.* He put the phone face down on the step.

Inside the House somebody rang the old bronze bell. It was not the victory peal. It was a single low stroke, and the sound went out across the courtyard and away toward the sea.

Kade shut his eyes.

Somewhere in the engineering network, a dead channel called null seven was still open, because Eli had once needed a place to send packets nobody cared about. Somewhere under the east wing, a vent had been closed by hand. And somewhere in Asterion's systems someone had been listening to Lab Six before it blew, and had chosen their minute.

All of those were facts. None of them was big enough to set against the line on the casualty page.

Eli was missing, presumed dead. Kade had spent a year learning to move a force to some place where it could do less harm. He could not find anywhere to put this, and he could not get away from the thought that he had drawn it down on them himself.

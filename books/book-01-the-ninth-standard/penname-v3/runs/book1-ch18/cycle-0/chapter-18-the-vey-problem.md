# Chapter 18 — The Vey Problem

*Replay, four fifty-one to five oh three. You stopped protecting the plan and started protecting seven. Correct outcome. Wrong reason. Sunday, usual time.*

Her father sent it at two in the morning, a week after the bell, and Mara read it at five with her boots already on. He was right about the exchange. He was right about the reason. He had been right in four lines with the time stamps attached, sent at an hour that told her he had watched the final twice in the dark and had not slept until he found the twelve seconds where his daughter had been less than she could be.

That was the difficulty with calling Alaric Vey cold. Cold people did not do that.

Sunday, usual time, was nine, and at nine the wall display in her room gave her his face with the half-second lag the island's link put on everything from shore. He wore the dark civilian jacket, not the uniform that went with the title in front of his name at public events, and the jacket made him look more official rather than less, because it meant he had chosen.

He spent eight minutes on her. Training load, and he had the numbers. Vector Analysis, and he had the Mechanics assessment date without looking it up, the one she had not mentioned. The east-platform change in the final, which he wanted to hear her defend.

"You moved Shah off the west post after Mercer went out."

"The distribution assumed he could keep Atlas's seventh busy. He couldn't, from behind glass."

"Most captains would have kept their shape."

"Then most captains would have lost."

He inclined his head a quarter of an inch, and from her father that was praise with no packaging on it, and she wanted it anyway, and disliked that she wanted it, and took it.

"You are still overextending the right shoulder in long exchanges," he said.

"I know."

"You dislike praise when you think it's being used to soften the next subject."

"You taught me to notice framing."

"I occasionally regret my parenting."

"That makes two of us."

The corner of his mouth moved. Then he said, "How is Mercer adapting?"

She did not close the coupled-field paper on her tablet yet. "Which Mercer?"

His face did nothing. She enjoyed that more than she should have.

"Kade Mercer."

"I know."

"Mara."

"Father."

Three seconds of lag and silence, which was most of their relationship compressed to fit. He knew she was being difficult. She knew he knew. The call was supposed to be about her.

"He's adapting," she said. "To being looked at. He's bad at it and getting better."

That earned the smallest movement around his eyes. Alaric Vey had spent her childhood telling her that attention was information, that everyone who watched you was measuring something whether they admitted it or not. She had learned it too well. She had also learned that he rarely asked a question for only one reason.

"You asked four questions about my semester," she said, "and one about Mercer. The Mercer one is the only one you prepared for."

A faint sigh came through the lag. "Has he repeated the structural event?"

Her stylus stopped.

The room changed the way a room does when one sentence in it contains something it should not. The public notice on the broken floor had said composite failure coinciding with an anomalous combat reading. The faculty summary, which she had read because Meridian's adviser had left it open on a screen, said anomalous combat reading and nothing else. Students called it the Mercer Fault because students named things the way they named dogs. Nobody, in any document she had seen, had called it a structural event. That was a classification. It came from a form she had never been shown.

And her father did not work at Asterion.

"What structural event?" she said.

The pause was too short for most people. Not for her. "The arena incident."

"The Mercer Fault."

"I dislike that name."

"So does he."

"Good."

She moved his face to the side of the display and opened the public notice in the space between them, so he could watch her read it. "Composite failure. Anomalous combat reading. Neither of them says structural event."

"The floor broke."

"That's a description. You used a classification."

"Does the distinction matter?"

"It mattered enough for you to use the restricted one."

The lag gave her half a second of false space. She knew its rhythm. His answer should have arrived inside it. It did not.

She was nine, in the Vey Foundation archive while he met the people who ran it, and had found two folders whose index numbers went from forty-one to forty-three. He came out and found her looking at the gap. *What is missing?* She had pointed at where forty-two would go. *No. Forty-two is absent. Missing means somebody expected it to be here.* He had made her find the accession ledger before he accepted the word, and the ledger had said forty-two had never been filed, and she had learned the difference between a hole and a hole somebody had made.

"Who expected you to know?" she asked.

His eyes narrowed. Not an answer. No longer nothing.

"You sound like Director Sen."

"Amara and I have disagreed about what deserves attention for years."

There it was again. Not the answer. The shape around it, the place a person had to step to avoid saying the thing they meant. Kade did that constantly, noticed it in everyone, and she had begun to find the habit irritatingly useful.

She thought about Sen, weeks ago, saying no to a question about Kade's father before he had finished the word. She had heard it from him in Bay One, with tape on the floor and two piles between them, and she had told him it was probably a decision and not a fact. It had been a fact about one adult. She now had, on the screen in front of her, a second adult.

"Did you know his father?"

Alaric Vey went still. Not much. Enough.

She sat forward. "Interesting."

"Mara."

"You did."

"I did not say that."

"You didn't need to."

His voice sharpened and dropped at once, into the register he kept for things that were not going to be discussed. "Do not investigate Kade Mercer's family."

That was the moment she knew that she would. She kept her face where it was.

"Why?"

"Because there are histories that do not improve through curiosity."

"That may be the least convincing sentence you've ever said to me."

"Mara."

Twice in one call. She filed it.

"Does Director Sen know you're asking me about him?"

Silence, and not the lag.

"I have to go," her father said.

"No. You have to not answer."

His face did the fractional thing it did when she had scored on him and he had decided to allow it. "I love you."

"That's unfair."

"It remains true."

The display went dark and gave her back her own face, and she looked at that for a while. She had been raised to believe discipline meant resisting impulses. She was beginning to think discipline meant choosing which impulse got to win.

---

She did not investigate. That annoyed her. She had a session in twenty minutes, and the academy did not care that her father had just turned a dead man into a classified question. Asterion kept its timetable the way the tide kept its.

Combat Hall Two had been cut into four lanes for acceleration work, each with a start plate, two pivot marks, and three waist-high barriers that turned on actuators once the runner crossed the first gate. The exercise paid for prediction. Commit before the barrier finished its turn and the lane opened. Wait to see it, and the next gate closed on you. She had run it forty times and liked it the way she liked anything that rewarded deciding early.

At the far end of the hall, past the lanes, Julian Cross had his clinic on the no-field square, forty people along the wall standing near the number, and Kade on the square with him, losing.

She saw it while she stretched and did not turn her head. Julian walked him toward the edge with three quiet decisions, the way he crossed a kitchen. Kade saw the trap one beat earlier than he had the week before, changed his feet, and lost anyway, and lay on the mat half a second, and laughed. Julian put a hand down. Kade took it. The last two fingers of his right hand were doing their separate thing as he came up, and she watched him see it and put it wherever he put it now.

She looked away before either of them noticed her looking.

Instructor Chen ran her through three sequences. The first she cleared in eight point four, which was slow for her. On the second she chose the center pivot before the first barrier had finished turning, right foot on the plate, shoulder clear of the closing edge, and the finish display gave her seven point nine.

She should have reset. Instead she looked down the hall, where Julian had Kade tracing the last exchange in the air with one hand, marking the place his attention had gone to the edge instead of the man.

Aaron Mercer.

The third tone went. She launched late, recovered before the first gate, read the barrier's opening angle as a left rotation, and committed.

It went right.

Her shoulder clipped the edge of it. The safety field took her a hand's width from the wall and held her there, sideways in the blue lattice, for half a second that felt like a paragraph, and then lowered her to the mat like something it had been asked to return.

Chen stopped the lane. The other two students on her plates stepped off and did not stare. At Asterion even concern was competitive.

"You're somewhere else."

"No."

"Mara."

Apparently everyone on the island had learned to use her name as a charge sheet. She breathed out. "Yes."

"Medical?"

"No."

"Family?"

She looked at him. Chen raised both hands, palms out. "Not asking what. Asking whether you're safe to continue."

She considered lying. Then she thought about Kade in front of a whole House admitting he had been frightened on a floor, and how much easier it had made everything after, and disliked the influence.

"Yes. Reduce speed."

He reset the lane to sixty percent and blanked the timer. No lecture. She looked at the empty display.

"I didn't ask you to remove scoring."

"I know."

"Then restore it."

"No."

He walked to the first pivot and tapped it with his shoe. "What caused the miss?"

"Divided attention."

"That describes you. What caused the miss."

She replayed the approach. Opening angle, rotation rate, the load in the barrier's lower arm. All of it consistent with left, until the actuator reversed.

"The cue was false."

"Every third sequence carries one now. The course updated yesterday."

"You changed the failure rule without announcing it."

"The briefing says not all initial conditions remain reliable. You read the briefing. You converted that sentence into ordinary variance, because the old lane paid for early commitment and you'd been paid forty times. Your problem today isn't speed. You promoted a likely answer to a fact because acting on it usually works."

The sentence arrived with her father's silence still attached to it. She disliked instructors who became relevant by accident.

"Run it again."

"New condition. Call the decision at every gate. Out loud."

"Speaking will slow me down."

"The timer's off."

That was exactly the problem.

The next run was slower and she hated it. At the first gate the barrier opened left and she said, "Likely left," and it held, and she took the pivot. At the second both lower actuators loaded equally and she said, "Unresolved," and shortened her stride instead of choosing, and the barrier feinted right, reversed, and left the center open for less than a second, and she went through without touching it. The last gate showed a clean right rotation. "Right." It stayed true. She finished four full seconds under her own speed. No score appeared.

Chen looked at the lane record, then at her. "Again?"

She was already on the plate. "Again."

By the fourth reduced-speed run, unresolved had stopped being an admission and become a condition with its own footwork, a shorter stride and a later weight and a way of arriving at a gate with two answers still in your legs. At full speed it would cost her fractions. It might also keep her out of a wall.

Her rank at the end of the session was seven. It had been part of her for months. Today it looked small beside a name in an archive she had not opened yet.

Chen sent the run record to her band before she left. Four reduced-speed attempts, no scores, and under assessment one word. CALIBRATING. Not impaired. Not distracted. Not failed.

Down the hall Kade was arguing with Julian about whether seeing a trap one step earlier counted as improvement if you still walked into it. On any other day she would have gone and settled it. Today she saved Chen's record beside the coupled-field notes and left him to his training until she had something he could use.

---

She ate lunch with Sera rather than at the first-year table, partly because Sera asked no question she did not intend to hear the answer to.

Sera looked at the tray. "You're not eating enough."

"That's Kade's line."

"It's mine. He got it off me by exposure." Sera went back to her own food and to the form on her tablet, which was not homework. The header said STUDENT INCIDENT RESPONSE, OBSERVATION ROTATION. Two clinic shifts logged. One faculty reference requested. One box, experience under live conditions, empty.

"You applied," Mara said.

Sera angled the screen away by reflex, then stopped, and set it flat. "I'm applying."

"The rotation's for second-years."

"The policy says two medical labs. It doesn't say second-years."

"The selection notes do."

"The selection notes aren't policy." Sera ate. "Kade's not the only one at this table who reads around the answer."

"Chen would sign the reference."

"I asked Park."

"Harder."

"More relevant."

Sera could have written a broken floor, a suppression alarm, and a House Trial final into that box, and had written nothing, because none of them had been hers to run. Mara respected that more than she was going to say.

"My father knows something about Kade he shouldn't," she said.

Sera stopped chewing. "That sounds bad."

"Yes."

"Does Kade know?"

"Not yet."

Sera thought about it the way she thought about a capsule, weight first. "Are you protecting him or protecting your investigation?"

Mara looked at her.

"Those look the same from inside your own head," Sera said, and went on eating.

"I need evidence."

"For which claim?"

"That what my father knows isn't incidental."

"That's your question." Sera tapped the table once. "His is going to be what it says about his father. Different question."

"I can't answer his without answering mine."

"Probably not."

"And telling him now hands him an accusation with no direction on it. He'll walk into Sen's office with it before dinner."

"Also true." Sera did not decide it for her, which would have been easier. "So what counts as enough?"

Mara looked at the tray. Her father had taught her to define the winning condition before she stepped onto any floor. She had stepped onto this one at nine in the morning without doing it.

"An independent link between Aaron Mercer and Asterion," she said. "Not my father's face. Not a public death record. Something neither of them supplied."

"Then stop when you've got it."

"Why?"

"Because after that it's his too, and you don't get to keep running it alone because you're better at running things."

That sounded like something Kade would say. Mara was beginning to suspect Meridian was contaminating everyone.

---

She opened Kade's public file that afternoon, and most of it was aggressively ordinary. Northline Alternative. Three suspensions. Two transfers. A semester of failure inconsistent with the aptitude scores beside it, which she now knew had been arranged by hand. Unremarkable medical. Mother, Elena Mercer. Father, deceased. Aaron Mercer. Industrial accident, seventeen years ago, a date close enough to Kade's birth that the two nearly touched.

She built a table beside it. Three columns. CLAIM. SOURCE. ALTERNATIVE.

First row: her father's use of *structural event*. Source, direct conversation. Alternative, a private summary through a legitimate academy contact. The alternative did not explain why he would hide the contact. It stayed in the table anyway, because a table with only the rows you liked was a speech.

Second row: his reaction to Aaron's name. Source, expression, and a warning. Alternative, he knew of the public accident, or knew somebody else in it. Weak. She disliked weak evidence most when it leaned her way.

The request panel under the file offered more. Disciplinary summaries with a House-faculty sponsor. Medical with a documented welfare incident. Either one logged to faculty, and either one required her to type a reason that was not the reason. She closed the panel. Public first. Institutional only where her scholar clearance already reached on its own. No side door from Eli. No spending Kade's trust as a permission he had not given. The rules did not make the thing harmless. They made it visible whose it was.

Public records, then. Employment, tax, a death certificate, and a short local item about an industrial containment accident outside the city, no byline, one photograph: a warehouse behind a perimeter fence, smoke from a roof vent, two emergency vehicles positioned for the camera rather than for the building. She checked the image data. Uploaded six hours after the reported accident. Captured eleven months before it.

It proved nothing. Local archives reused file photographs constantly. She entered it anyway, with the ordinary explanation in the column beside it.

The death certificate was stronger in the wrong direction. Physician's signature, municipal seal, registered time, no amendment. Then she opened the signing physician's public registration, and the license belonged to a doctor who had practiced two hundred kilometers away. Possible, during a federal incident. Not ordinary. She wrote both.

No Asterion. No Resonance registration. Nothing for her father to have flinched at.

She opened the International Resonance Authority research index, which her scholar clearance let her into on its own. No Aaron Mercer. That was more interesting than a result. People vanished from public history all the time. People did not vanish from bureaucracies; bureaucracies loved records more reliably than anyone had ever loved a person. She tried variants. A. Mercer. Mercer, Aaron. A. J. Mercer. Nothing. She left the searchable database and went down into the archived identifier index, the old layer under the new one, ugly and slow and wonderfully literal, and it gave her one line.

MERCER, A. RESEARCH CLEARANCE RESTRICTED.

No date. No agency. A dead link and a classification marker older than the academy's current charter.

Her pulse changed, and she noted that it had, and went on.

A second search on the identifier produced a fragment. RELATED FACILITY: ASTERION PRECURSOR PROGRAM.

She read it twice. Asterion had not existed under that name thirty-one years ago. Something had existed on this rock. She searched the program designation itself and got fragments: construction permits for an offshore research platform; a federal environmental waiver; a budget line routed through the International Resonance Authority a year before the Authority publicly existed under that name. Then four years of nothing. No photographs, no project description, no personnel. An absence too clean to have happened by itself.

She searched her father's name. No result. She searched Amara Sen. One publication citation, twenty-nine years old, and after it a block where records should have been and were not.

*Amara and I have disagreed for years.* About what.

Someone knocked.

She closed the archive before the second knock, and Kade leaned through the doorway with two nutrition bars in one hand.

"You studying?"

"Yes."

"That sounded guilty."

"It wasn't."

"Now it sounds worse." He came in without waiting and put the bars on her desk. "One is allegedly chocolate. The other admits defeat and calls itself protein."

"Why are you here?"

"Eli says you skipped dinner. He passed you in the library at six with the face you get."

"So Eli sent you."

"Eli proposed a drone. I'm preventing property damage."

She almost smiled. His eyes went to the blank wall display and came back. "You really were studying."

"Yes."

"What?"

"Things that don't improve through curiosity."

He looked at her. "That is an extremely suspicious sentence."

"I know."

He waited. She considered telling him everything, and imagined him walking into Sen's office with the whole of it before she knew which question to ask. He had many strengths. Patience was still theoretical.

"Vector theory," she said.

"You're terrible at lying."

"I am excellent at lying."

"No. You're excellent at not answering."

She went still, and he saw it. The joke left his face. "Did I say something?"

"No."

Another lie. Worse. He leaned on the doorframe. "Mara."

She disliked being managed with her own name, mostly because it worked.

"I found something strange," she said. "Not enough yet."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one you're getting."

A month ago he would have pushed until one of them was angry, because pushing was the only lever he had ever been handed. Instead he looked at the dark display, and at her, and said, "Okay."

That surprised her. "You're not going to argue?"

"I'm growing." She knew the phrase from his Darius stories. He picked up the protein bar and tossed it to her. "Tell me when it's enough."

He turned to go, and she said, "Kade," and he looked back.

"What do you know about your father's work?"

His face did the thing it did on a mat when Park asked him for a number. "Industrial systems contractor. Ports, power routing, automatic shutoffs. Mom says that part's true, it's on his pay stubs. She says he worked around people connected to early Resonance research, and after he died she was told some of it was classified, and that's the whole of what she knows for certain." He stopped. "And I promised her I don't open a door on my own. Any door. Records, rooms, Sen. Whoever I'm going to stand in front of with what I find, she hears it first."

Mara nodded. He watched her another second, and went, and the door closed.

She sat very still. That was not evidence. He had put it in her hands standing in a doorway with a nutrition bar, and it was his, and it was not going into a table. And the door he had promised not to open on his own was the door she had spent the afternoon opening for him, with his name on the file.

Sera's question came back. Protecting him, or protecting the investigation. She did not like the answer, which was both. It meant she had to move faster.

---

She went to paper in the morning. Not the restricted archive under old administration. The public historical collection in the Spire, where deletion could not hide, because paper had the one advantage digital systems hated. A hole in a book stayed a hole.

The librarian, Mr. Liang, brought her three bound volumes on the island's institutional history before the academy charter and set them on the reading table with the care of a man handling patients.

"Architecture?" he asked.

"Precursor facilities."

"Class?"

"Independent."

That earned a look. Independent research was ordinary. A pre-charter offshore platform apparently was not. He did not object. He came back with a fourth volume. "This one's mostly photographs."

She spent two hours turning pages. Construction crews, early seawalls, aerial images of the original platform with nothing on it that looked like a school. Scientists in captions. She photographed every personnel list and wrote the volume, page, and caption date beside each. The archive terminal offered automatic transcription and she turned it off after it rendered *containment pier* as *commencement pier* and gave one engineer three surnames in a paragraph. Paper wanted patience, and it wanted you to read what stood around the answer, which was where the answer usually kept its coat.

The first volume described a weather station and a materials laboratory on the platform before anyone had lived there. The second described emergency-energy research. The third used the phrase *academy planning site* nine years before the academy charter, and she wrote it down, and none of the three named Aaron.

Near the end of the second hour Liang came back with a foam wedge for the photograph volume. "The binding complains if you open it flat."

She shifted the book onto it. "Who wrote these captions?"

He checked the title page. "Institutional communications, mostly. Some were transferred from the platform records."

"Transferred when?"

"Depends which transfer." He drew a paper accession sheet from the rear pocket. Three catalog numbers on it had been crossed out in three inks. The oldest named no academy at all. It named the site as ASTERION RESEARCH ANNEX.

Mara kept her face still. "Can I photograph this?"

"It's part of a public volume."

"That wasn't the question."

Liang studied her for a moment, and she let him. "Yes. No flash."

She took the picture. It proved nothing about Aaron Mercer. It proved the academy's history had a predecessor the lobby display did not mention, and that somebody had renamed it at least twice. Progress. Not payoff.

Then a page was gone.

Not torn. Cut, close to the stitching, top to bottom in one stroke, no ragged fiber, no mark on the facing page. Somebody had laid a blade along the gutter with the book open. The pages on either side went from Year Three construction to Year Five expansion.

"What happened to two fourteen?"

Liang checked the binding, and then the register behind the desk. "Archive note says removed under federal retention authority. Twenty-six years ago."

"Can I see the request?"

"If it's public."

It was not. He brought the removal register anyway, because the register was, and it kept a physical description of what had gone. One photographic plate. One personnel caption. One equipment caption. None of them reproduced. But somebody, twenty-six years ago, had thought the three belonged together enough to take them all at once.

"Do retention requests normally remove the page?"

"Now? No. We'd restrict the volume." He looked down at the clean edge. "Twenty-six years ago, apparently somebody preferred scissors."

"Blade."

He glanced at her.

"Somebody preferred a blade."

She thanked him and walked back through the Hall of Standards, because it was on the way, and because she had walked it several hundred times without once counting. Eleven bronze faces along the curved wall, Sen fifth from the left and thirty years younger with the same expression. Behind each bronze the wall stepped back into a stone bay. She counted the bays out of a habit that was Kade's and not hers, and got twelve, and the twelfth had no bronze and a small plate that said LEGACY BAY 12, ACCESS SEALED, and she had known that plate was there for four months the way she knew where the fire doors were.

Probably nothing. Probably an old donor. She took a picture anyway, and did not put it in the table, because she did not yet know which column it belonged to.

---

Her band chimed on the Meridian bridge. Taren Holt.

*You missed dinner last night. Everything okay?*

She stopped walking. She had told no one she was staying in. Eli knew, because he had passed her in the library. Kade knew, because Eli had told him. Taren should not have known yet, unless somebody had told him after, which was possible and normal and worth exactly one line.

*Fine. Studying.*

*Terrible hobby. See you at breakfast.*

Innocent. Probably. She looked at it longer than it deserved, and then put it where Kade would have put it, and went in.

Meridian at dusk had become an argument about time. Taren stood on a chair by the Commons glass with his arms out, proposing Day Nine of the New Era, and Sera, from the couch, informed him that an era needed more than a week and a bell. Eli had three drone parts in his soup and was not participating. Kade sat at the long table under the ladder wall with notation homework and a face that was visibly pretending not to enjoy any of it.

It was ordinary, and the relief of it went through her hard enough that she nearly laughed.

She sat beside him. He looked up.

"Enough yet?"

"Not quite."

He nodded and went back to the page. No push. That made the thing in her bag feel heavier than paper.

---

That night she reopened the old index page, because it had one collapsed field left and she had been raised to open every folder.

PROGRAM STATUS: DISCONTINUED AFTER CONTACT EVENT.

No explanation. No public reference. She searched the phrase across every public record the academy kept about itself and got nothing. She went down to the oldest research-index syntax, the one that took a full second to answer, and one result came up and vanished before the page finished loading.

A cached title fragment. CONTACT ZERO. COMPATIBILITY STUDY.

The system refreshed. Access denied.

She stopped breathing, and noticed, and started again.

Zero. Not his score. Not necessarily. But the word ran from a buried platform to a restricted identifier with his father's initial on it to something that had happened here, before the school, and been called an event by the people who had ended a program because of it. She saved the cached reference offline, where a refresh could not take it back, and closed everything except the table.

Then she read the table from the top the way her father had taught her to read a ledger, without skipping the rows she already believed. Alaric's restricted word. His face at Aaron's name. The clearance marker. The precursor program. The Research Annex on the accession sheet. A page cut from a book twenty-six years ago with a blade. A contact event attached to the same buried program. Contact Zero.

Any one of them permitted an ordinary explanation. Together they did not permit the same ordinary explanation, and that distinction was the whole of what she had.

She added a last row. CLAIM: Aaron Mercer had a concealed connection to this site before his reported death. SOURCE: precursor clearance index, public record chronology, physical archive. ALTERNATIVE: MERCER, A. was another person, and her father's face was about something else. Possible. No longer persuasive.

Her winning condition had been an independent link. She had one.

The next step could have been another archive, another syntax, another hour spent making the evidence cleaner before she let him touch it. That was how an investigation became a possession. She had watched her father do it with things that belonged to other people, and she had the same hands.

She exported the table to a local file and deleted the row that summarized Kade's student record, because the public facts about Aaron were evidence and the shape of his suspensions was not. Then she made a second copy. One for him. One for her. The same evidence, line for line. No private version where she remained the only person who could read his life.

For the first time since her family had started assuming she would come here, Mara looked at the school she had spent her life earning and wondered what, exactly, she had been admitted to.

She opened a message.

*Tomorrow. Breakfast. I have enough.*

She read it twice and sent it.

*That sounds ominous.*

*It's informational.*

Three dots, for longer than three dots usually took him.

*You're becoming funny. I'm concerned.*

She smiled at the screen, and then looked at the saved reference beside it and stopped, because tomorrow his family stopped being her suspicion and became his history, and after that she did not think any of them would be able to pretend the island was only a school.

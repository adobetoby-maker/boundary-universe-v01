# Chapter 28 — Memorial

The Hall of Names owned two hundred chairs. By seven that morning somebody had counted the replies and found it would need about twice that. So Kade spent the first hour of the day carrying Meridian's dining chairs out to a facilities cart, four to a stack.

Anton caught him on the sixth trip, with a printed sheet in his hand.

"The Hall takes three things," he said. "A portrait, and the academy has one on file. Two hundred words for the program, which are on the corkboard being fought over. And one object from the House." He held the sheet out. "I said nothing leaves that room unless you say so. So the object's yours, by nine."

"What's everyone expecting?"

"Half the floor expects the mug. I told them no." Anton took a small specimen bag out of his shirt pocket. It held a ceramic connector about the size of a coin. A note was taped to the outside, in handwriting that could only be Julian's. *From Systems Lab, on loan. It stands for the work.* "And Cross expects that."

Kade took the bag up eight floors. He put the connector down on Eli's bench mat, beside the drone rotor, and looked at the pair of them.

The connector had no markings. Anybody passing it in the Hall would need a paragraph to understand it, and Eli would have supplied six. It was an argument about what Eli might have become. But the work was not finished, and Kade did not think an unfinished thing should be made to stand under a portrait and mean something.

The rotor meant nothing at all. It had been bent in an ordinary failed repair on an ordinary day, and had then waited three weeks in the exact middle of the mat for an appointment. All it proved was that somebody had lived in this room long enough to leave a job half done.

He picked it up. The mat looked wrong at once, and he knew why. In this half of the room, a thing without a label was a thing that was lost.

Eli's tape was in the top drawer with the pen clipped to it. Kade tore off a strip and wrote on it in capitals, because that was the local style. ROTOR OUT ON LOAN. RETURNING. K. He pressed it down on the empty middle of the mat.

He looked at the second word for a while. It was a promise about a rotor. He was allowed to make that one.

Downstairs, the two hundred words were pinned to the corkboard beside the sheet of hours, with a pen hanging next to them on a string. The Hall's biography office had written them out of the academy record. The House had until nine to correct them, and the House had been correcting since six.

*Promising* had been struck out in Sera's square capitals, with a note in the margin. MEANS THEY EXPECTED HIM TO BE USEFUL LATER. Under that, in Priya's thick pen: HE WAS USEFUL IN OCTOBER.

One word had a ring round it and no line through it. *Sacrifice.* Sera and Julian were standing in front of it, and had evidently been there some time.

"He went back in to close the vent," Julian said. "He knew what it might cost him. I don't have another word for that."

"It was a decision," said Sera. "He looked at a problem and he decided. Sacrifice is what the people who are still alive call it afterward, so they can be grateful and keep it. The academy gets to thank itself for being worth it." She had not raised her voice. "It was his. Leave it his."

Julian was quiet. Then he said, "My father would call it a sacrifice. He'd put it on a wall." He reached for the pen on its string. "Which I suppose is your point."

He struck the word out himself, and read the sentence aloud to hear how it stood. "Elias Navarro died after preventing a larger systems failure in Lab Six."

"Presumed," said Kade.

They both turned round. He still had the rotor in his hand.

"The investigators say presumed dead. Everyone else has started dropping the first word, because the second one's easier to print."

The string was too short for where he stood. He had to lean in between them to write it, small, above the line. Nobody corrected him.

Julian copied the finished text into the Hall's form and turned the tablet round.

*Elias Navarro is presumed dead after preventing a larger systems failure in Lab Six. He believed that technical excellence was unfinished if ordinary people could not afford its protection.*

"A hundred and ninety-four words," he said.

"You can lose the line about his standing," said Kade. "That gets you under one-ninety."

"I can't. The form won't send without academy standing. It's a locked field."

"You're first of ten thousand," Priya said, from the table. "If anyone gets to say what standing is worth at a memorial, it's you. I resent that. But there it is."

Julian looked at the field. Then he deleted the sentence and typed two words into the box. NOT RELEVANT. The form turned amber.

"Is amber bad?" said Kade.

"Amber means a person has to look at it." Julian pressed send. "Good. A person should."

---

They took the last of the chairs over at half past nine. Kade pushed the cart, with the rotor riding in the top stack. Sera had cut a strip of blue from the House roll for every Meridian student who was going, and Kade's had come untied twice already. The third time, he steered one-handed and held the wrist out sideways. Mara, walking beside the cart, fixed the knot in about four seconds without breaking step.

Taren was waiting at the foot of the Hall steps.

He had a security officer on either side of him. He was cleared to attend under escort and not cleared to set foot in Meridian, so this was the first place he could have asked. It was also the most public place on the island, with Sol going up the steps in a body.

"Sera," he said. "Is there one I could wear?"

Sera's hand went to her jacket pocket, where the spares were, and closed.

"Here? In front of every House there is? You walk in wearing that and they'll all read it the same way. Meridian took him back."

"I know how it reads."

"You wrote reports on him."

"I wrote reports on all of you. I know that doesn't help."

He could have said that the reports had been getting worse on purpose since the House Trial. He could have mentioned the credentials he had burned to get them out of the archive. Kade watched him come to each of those and leave it where it lay.

"I'm not asking the House to let me off," Taren said. "I'm asking to carry him in. As I understand it, that's all the strip is for."

A boy from Sol had slowed on the steps, and he was not looking at Sera. He was looking at Kade, as people had since the Trial, as if Kade were where things got settled. He could have settled this. Taren had helped to save them, and Taren had made the attack easier for the people who sent him. Both were true. Kade had not worked out what they added up to, and he was not going to hand Sera a sum he hadn't finished. He took the rotor off the cart and held it, and kept his mouth shut.

It was Anton who spoke, and he spoke only to Sera. "I keep the roll, so I'll say what I was told, and then I'll stop. It goes on a bed when somebody's missing, or dead, or arrested. Arrested is on the list. The roll's never asked what anybody did. It marks who's gone." He stepped back. "The rest is your pocket."

For some seconds there was only the sound of feet on the steps. Sera took out one strip. She did not hand it over. She laid it on the stone rail between them.

"You tie it yourself. Outside the cuff, where it shows. If you're carrying him, you carry him where people can see. And when they ask you what it means, you're the one who answers. Not us."

"That's fair." Taren picked it up. "Thank you."

"It doesn't change anything."

"No."

He tied it one-handed and made a poor job of it. One officer noted the exchange on her tablet. The other shifted half a step, to keep Taren inside arm's reach. The strip had altered neither of those things, and Kade found that he was glad. The House could make room for Taren's grief without pretending trust had come back with it.

He had walked past the Hall of Names fifty times and never gone in. Its doors were dark wood, on an island made of glass.

His feet slowed at the top step. But a steward in gray gloves was waiting inside for the House object, and the person who had signed for the room was supposed to be the one who brought it. He had something to carry. He suspected, going through the doors, that this was why the custom existed.

Inside, the walls were bronze from the floor to well above his reach. The names ran in narrow strips, row over row: students, staff, graduates killed at field disasters, people lost in the early Resonance years. Each strip carried a name and two dates. There was not a rank or a Standard anywhere. It was the only wall on Asterion that did not tell you who was winning.

Eli's portrait stood at the front. He had sat for it straight-faced, in a collar, and Kade had never seen a less honest picture of him. On a table below it, on a square of blue cloth, lay a bronze strip. ELIAS NAVARRO. It had no dates. The mounting pins on the back had not been trimmed, so that it sat a little tilted, like something put down for a moment.

The steward took the rotor in both gloved hands and set it on the cloth beside the academy crest, as gravely as if it had been a medal. It looked ridiculous there. Kade nearly laughed, and for once it did not feel like a betrayal.

"Why is the name loose?" he asked.

"A name goes on the wall when the recovery finding is final, and not before," the steward said. "His family asked that nobody be made to wait for an investigation before they could be in a room together. They also asked us to keep the record accurate. We can do both. That's what the cloth is for."

It should have sounded like procedure. He wanted the tilted bronze to be a clue. He had a device number he wanted to be a clue as well, and two wants did not add up to one fact. The pins were uncut because a file was open. He made himself leave it at that, and went to sit down.

He took the aisle seat in Meridian's second row. The House had kept the chair beside his empty.

The room went on filling. He had pictured this grief as the size of room 814. Engineering took three rows on its own. Owen Park sat with the Vector Analysis students, and Professor Amadi had their aisle. The professor with the orange tool case was there, two seats along from a colleague Kade did not know. Instructor Hale stood by the doors. Two maintenance technicians sat in work gray, and one held a battered sensor housing in his lap with both hands, like a dish brought to someone's house. Behind them sat a woman in a mainland municipal-response uniform.

"He knew every person on this island who could unlock a panel," Sera murmured. "And he wrote to her about the stabilizer until she wrote back."

The steward closed the doors. There was no tone and no announcement. Everyone stood, and the minute began.

Kade counted fifteen seconds and lost the count. The silence filled with everything he had been holding at arm's length, and the last of it was Eli on the wrong side of a gray door. When the minute ended, nobody looked relieved.

"We don't keep an order here," the steward said. "Whoever has something, come up to the cloth and tell us who you are."

Priya said he had come to find her after the forty-seven-second fight, ranked several thousand places below her, with diagrams.

"Two of them were right," she said. "I never told him which."

The colleague turned out to be Professor Kim, who ran the first-year systems labs. Kim held up a connector like the one in the specimen bag. Mr. Navarro had thrown it out of his own design for costing forty-three credits more than the same part in industrial ceramic.

"I said forty-three credits was nothing at laboratory scale. Mr. Navarro said laboratory scale was where expensive mistakes went to become policy." Kim looked at the portrait. "I've since used that on two committees. I'd like to correct the attribution."

The woman in the uniform was Captain Ortiz, and she had never met him. He had sent her nine messages. One was a question about stabilizing a collapsed bridge deck, seven were corrections to her answer, and the last was an apology for the seven. People laughed, and she let them. Then she crossed to the technician, took the battered housing out of his lap, and brought it up to the cloth.

"He wanted rescue gear an ordinary department could afford. And he asked me who gets protected first when the good equipment is scarce. That's the best question anyone's asked me in ten years, and I gave him half an answer." She set the housing down beside the rotor. "I hope somebody finishes asking it."

Mara spoke about the three hours Eli had spent proving a training algorithm shortchanged left-handed users, after faculty called the gap statistically insignificant. "He did not accept *insignificant* easily," she said. Her voice was perfectly steady. She kept both hands flat on the edge of the table, so that nobody past the second row could see what they were doing. "He also never knew when to stop explaining. I do."

Sera stood at the cloth looking annoyed at being looked at.

"Eli thought every problem was technical until proven otherwise. He was wrong about that all the time." People smiled. "But he thought a thing could be made better if somebody cared enough to find out why it broke. I've been offered the accelerated track for the Response Corps. I'm taking it."

Kade looked up. She had told him about the offer. She had not told him that.

"I want to be the person who knows what to do when a building turns into a problem. He wanted to build what that person would need." Her fist closed on the strip at her wrist, and her voice cracked, once. "I intend to get good enough to break every single one of them for him."

The Hall laughed, and a good many of them were crying while they did it. Sera sat down fast. Kade kept his eyes on the portrait, to give her privacy.

Dr. Sen came up last. Kade had his anger ready for *brilliance* and *potential*. She talked about a student who filed safety paperwork on the second request, and had once corrected a faculty equipment specification in the middle of his own disciplinary meeting.

"Elias Navarro believed that rules owed people reasons," Sen said. "This made work for a number of us. He also believed that a system which failed the people who depended on it most was not a finished system. I have looked for the flaw in that. I haven't found it."

*Believed.* He heard the past tense go by in it, as it had gone by in everyone's.

Every story had made Eli more present and harder to reach, and Kade's body had started making its old plan without consulting him. Doors, corridor, air, distance.

"Is there anyone else?" said the steward.

Kade stood up.

He was in the aisle before he understood which way he was facing. He was facing the doors. Hale saw him coming and did exactly what Kade had known he would. He asked nothing. He put a hand to the dark wood and opened it a hand's width, and a bar of daylight fell across the floor.

Nobody in that room would have thought less of him, and that was what made it dangerous. Nobody had stopped him at the shuttle gate either. He had worked out there what an open door was for. It was so that staying would count.

Four hundred people had watched him get up. He turned round and walked the other way.

At the cloth he found that he had nothing prepared. Ordinarily he put a joke into a silence before it turned dangerous. This one already had.

"Kade Mercer. I'm the roommate." He breathed. "I was leaving just then. Most of you saw. I'm good at it. Ask anyone in Meridian."

"He's excellent," said Sera, not quietly. A laugh went along the Meridian rows, and something in his chest let go by a notch.

"There's a box of noodles in our room with a label on it. The label says they don't become mine when he's in class. He wrote it in the first month, about somebody he'd known four weeks, and he was right." Engineering laughed. "He's the first person here who never asked me what the zero meant. He asked if I'd eaten. Then he put the answer in a model. The model has twenty-three columns, and one of them is just for the fights I lose by being clever."

His voice went. He waited until it came back.

"I've run most of my life on one rule. Don't need anybody, and nobody gets a lever. Nobody can leave a hole." He looked at the empty chair on the aisle. "I thought that was being smart. This week I thought it had been proved."

"It's wrong. Needing people isn't weakness. It's just expensive."

There was a small, pained laugh.

"Eli's worth it."

He heard the tense after it was out. He left it where it was.

He could have stopped there. But the housing sat beside the rotor.

"He isn't done, either. I can't finish it for him. I can't build any of it, and I'm not going to stand up here and promise his work on his behalf. It's his." He found the three rows of engineering. "So I'm only asking. Don't turn it into a plaque. He'd file a complaint about the plaque. Build the thing. Make it cheap enough for the people he meant. Then give it to Sera."

Professor Kim covered a laugh with one hand. Sera's eyes were wet, and the look she gave Kade promised consequences.

He walked back on borrowed legs. When he sat down his hands were shaking, and it was not the Boundary tremor. Mara laid her hand flat over the nearer one, as she might have steadied a plate on a rig. He turned his own over and held on, and did not pretend to either of them that he hadn't.

---

The Hall did not empty quickly. People stood about the courtyard with small sandwiches and told the stories that had not been fit for the cloth.

Kade was holding two sandwiches. Sera had given him hers when Captain Ortiz walked up and put the housing into her hands.

"Navarro told me you break emergency equipment," Ortiz said. "So tell me what broke this."

Sera turned it over once. Up close it was older than it had looked. One corner had been rebuilt in gray polymer.

"Not the battery. You'd have swapped a battery and never thought about it again." Her thumb found the cable port. "The locking collar's cracked. And look at the wear round it. This has been plugged and unplugged more than a field unit should be in its life. You were short of cables, and moving them between housings."

"Six housings and four good cables. The connector's proprietary, and the maker stopped making it."

"So you swapped until a collar went. Then you were down to three sensors."

"Two. One cable went under the west slab with a responder on the end of it."

"Bridge collapse outside Denver," Ortiz said. "That unit came through the first impact. It failed while we were monitoring aftershocks."

"And the responder?"

"Recovered. Alive."

Sera's shoulders came down by a finger's width. Ortiz tapped a ceramic piece set into the rebuilt corner. "That part's his. He wanted to standardize a replacement and publish the pattern, so any department could make its own. I told him we could buy new housings quicker than we could certify an unofficial part. He sent nineteen lines of cost comparison. So I asked if he'd ever stood beside a responder trusting a part that might fail under load."

"What did he say?"

"He didn't argue. Tip it to the light."

Something had been etched along the seam, very small. Sera read it out. "Test to failure. Then test the failure."

"He asked what our certification rig would need. Then he sent plans for one. We couldn't afford to build it."

So that was the other half of Eli's question, and Kade had never thought of it. Asterion could make a cheap part any day of the week. Nobody had solved how a department outside Asterion could afford to prove the cheap part was safe.

Sera handed the housing back. Ortiz took a plain paper card from her breast pocket. It had no academy seal on it.

"Your assessment lead says you start this month. Come and do a shift with people who can't fix a cracked housing by opening a laboratory account. Working, not watching. Supervised, and unpaid."

Sera did not take it at once. "Can I bring his connector plans?"

"If his family and his fellowship committee say so. Not before."

Kade saw that it was the right answer. Being missing had not made Eli's work public property. Sera took the card.

"Then I'll ask them."

Ortiz sent the housing's failure report to her tablet. Sera read the report first and the card second, which convinced Kade that she would call. He ate her sandwich.

He had gone back to stacking chairs when Professor Kim found him.

"Cheap enough for the people he meant," Kim said. "Did you know that was the part in danger, or was it luck?"

"I didn't know any of it was in danger."

"The quickest way to get his design through review is to swap the affordable parts for certified ones, and redefine the users as agencies with Asterion procurement access."

Kade set down a chair. "Then it passes, and the point's gone."

"He used very nearly those words, in a complaint." Kim looked tired. "I'm telling you because the committee can't strike his cost assumptions without notifying his working group, and you're in it. He listed you as a collaborator."

"I'm not an engineer."

"No. Under *contribution* he wrote, 'Asks annoying system-boundary questions.'"

Kade laughed before he could stop it. It hurt, going through.

Kim held out a data wafer with a green stripe down one edge. "His proposal. Understand what this is. His family decides what formally becomes of it. The working group has technical access and nothing more. Captain Ortiz has one prototype, made from plans he released to her department. She may keep that unit and test it, and she may not distribute the pattern. Nothing is disposed of for thirty days, or longer if the family asks. The fellowship is suspended. It is not closed."

Across the courtyard, the professor with the orange tool case was talking to the two technicians. She had used the same word in his doorway.

He took the wafer. "I'm not promising to finish it."

"You're not qualified to promise that." Kim very nearly smiled. "You are qualified to ask whether the people who finish it kept the problem he set out to solve. Do nothing with it today."

Kim went back to the students. Eli was gone, and he had still managed to set homework.

Taren was at the edge of the courtyard between his officers, with the strip crooked over his cuff. A girl from Kepler had stopped to ask him about it, and he was answering her himself, and not enjoying it. He looked across once and then away. The anger was still there, and Kade was too tired to lift it. He understood, for the first time, that this was not the same as forgiving anyone, and that nobody could make him pretend it was. It would keep.

He found Mara by the cart. "I told my mother I'd call after. I'm going up."

She studied his face. "Do you want company?"

"After."

"Then I'll be in the common room."

---

The card was still on the door of 814. ENDS: ON RETURN, OR DAY SEVEN. The strip sat crooked on its bedpost, and the bed was as empty as he had left it.

He sat down at Eli's desk, which he had walked round all week. He put the wafer on the mat beside his tape label and did not go looking for a reader. Today was not the day to turn grief into a project.

He had told his mother he would call after. She was on shift until six, so he began a message to hold the place. *Spoke. Tried to leave first, then didn't.* A gray notification slid down over the words, the kind that told you a filter had been changed. He flicked it away. *Calling tonight.* It came down again. He flicked it away again and sent the message.

It came down a third time.

Housing did not send anything three times. He opened it.

It was a plain gray window, and he had seen it before, over Eli's shoulder on the last afternoon. It held a short list of channels, and the one at the bottom had woken up. NULL-7. The sender field said only NULL.

There were three messages.

DUDE.

DO NOT FREAK OUT.

I AM SERIOUS ABOUT THE FREAKING OUT. THERE ARE SENSORS.

Kade stopped breathing.

Then he did what he had done with the press at dinner. He turned the tablet face down on the mat, carefully, because his hands had started to shake.

Somebody could be cruel. A dying system could cough up an old packet. Null seven was a dead-letter box that nothing logged and nothing listened to, and Eli's tablet had gone into the spine with him. For two days Kade had refused to build Eli out of a missing device number. He was not going to build him now out of capital letters and wanting.

The tablet buzzed against the mat.

He looked up without moving his head much. The housing sensor sat in its corner of the ceiling, as it had all year. He had never once asked who could see through it.

He turned the tablet over slowly, like a man with nothing on his mind but filters.

ALSO NEVER TELL SERA I SAID DUDE ON THE DAY OF MY OWN MEMORIAL.

He typed, *Prove it. Nothing from this room. You said yourself the room has eyes.*

The pause was long enough to hurt.

THAT IS ANNOYINGLY RIGOROUS. I BLAME MARA.

FINE. THE COLUMN CALLED MERCER GETS CLEVER HERE. STILL THE MOST PREDICTIVE THING I HAVE EVER BUILT.

ALSO DO NOT LET ANYONE CLOSE THE TICKET FROM MARCH. THIS CHANNEL IS LOAD-BEARING.

The model lived in a file, and a file could be opened by whoever held Eli's tablet. An officer had the March ticket in his notes. A stranger could have found the column. But a stranger would not have stopped to be proud of it, or fretted about paperwork in the middle of coming back from the dead. It was not proof. It was evidence, and all of it pointed the same way.

*Eli?*

YES.

For three days he had stood braced against a weight. Now it was gone, all at once, and he very nearly went over into the place where it had been. He had supposed hope would be gentle. But his body knew to the gram what there was to lose, and hope hit him with all of it.

TELL NO ONE YET. SEN KNOWS. MARA CAN KNOW. SERA WILL MURDER ME, BUT SHE CAN KNOW AFTER WE MOVE.

*Sen knows.* An hour ago she had stood at the blue cloth and said *believed*, in the past tense, to a room full of Eli's friends. Then she had watched Kade pay out in public what Eli cost. Rage and relief went through him so close together that he could not tell them apart.

*Where are you.*

CLASSIFIED ALIVE. IT IS VERY INCONVENIENT.

*Why.*

BECAUSE THE PEOPLE WHO BLEW UP LAB SIX ARE STILL WATCHING. POSSIBLY THIS. WE NEED TO TALK SOMEWHERE THIS CHANNEL IS NOT A TERRIBLE IDEA.

He thought of status traffic copied off the island, and a device that had stopped reporting before the coolant went. The pieces did not yet make a picture, and he would not force them into one.

ALSO I HEARD ABOUT THE SPEECH.

His throat closed. *How.*

CLASSIFIED ALIVE, REMEMBER. There was a gap. WORTH IT IS A LOT OF PRESSURE.

Kade wiped his face on the sleeve of his one good shirt.

*Get used to it.*

I AM TOLD YOU USED THE PRESENT TENSE. The gap this time was longer. THANK YOU FOR THAT.

He laughed. He let it happen, and kept it quiet for the sensor.

Then he pocketed the tablet. Most of what he wanted was old habit in new clothes. He could walk into Sen's office and break something. He could sit here alone and hold the secret, because carrying things alone had always felt like strength. But the message said *Mara can know*, and Mara would not believe one word of it until she had tested it to failure. It needed exactly that. So did he.

He got up like someone going down for a sandwich, and left the door on its wedge.

The card was where Anton's pen had left it. ENDS: ON RETURN, OR DAY SEVEN. It had always been printed like that, and he had been told not to read anything into it.

He read into it all the way down the stairs.

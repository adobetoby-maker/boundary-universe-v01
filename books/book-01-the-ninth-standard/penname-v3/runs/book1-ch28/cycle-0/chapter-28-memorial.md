# Chapter 28 — Memorial

The chairs were not for the memorial. They were for the argument that had to happen first.

Kade carried the last two in from the stair landing and set them at the long common-room table, and by ten past eight most of Meridian was sitting round a checklist. The Hall of Names had sent it overnight. It allowed one formal portrait, one object from the student's House, and one biography for the printed program, not to exceed two hundred words.

Julian Cross had got Eli's life down to one hundred and ninety-eight.

Sera read it over his shoulder with her arms folded. She read the way she checked a stairwell, looking for the part that would give way.

"Take out *promising*."

Julian looked up. "Why?"

"Because it means the adults expected him to be useful later."

"He was useful in October," said Priya, from the far end. She had come in ten minutes earlier, taken the chair Julian was using, and told him that helping Meridian did not make him Meridian. Julian had moved one chair to the left and kept typing.

Three things lay on the table in front of Kade. One was the bent drone rotor from the middle of Eli's bench mat. Kade had carried it down himself. One was a ceramic connector the size of a coin, in a specimen bag, sent over on loan from Systems Lab. The third was a space. Anton had laid a folded napkin there where the mug would have gone, because Kade had said *not the mug* at dinner and Anton believed in keeping a record.

"The connector," Julian said. "It stands for the work."

"The work isn't finished."

"That may be why it stands for him."

Kade turned the bag over. The connector had no markings and no visible reason to matter. Anyone walking past it would need a paragraph to understand it, and Eli would have supplied six. It was an argument about what Eli might have become.

The rotor argued nothing. It had been bent in an ordinary failed repair on an ordinary day, and then it had waited three weeks for an appointment. No committee would ever review it. It proved only that a person had lived in room 814 long enough to leave something half done there.

He pushed the bag back toward Julian. "Rotor."

Nobody argued. Sera had gone back to the biography.

"Take out *sacrifice* as well."

Julian's hand stopped over the screen. "He went back into Lab Six to close the vent."

"He did. He decided to. Call it a sacrifice and it turns into something the academy gets to thank itself for surviving." She did not raise her voice. "It was his decision. Leave it his."

The table was quiet. Julian deleted the word, and read the new sentence aloud to hear how it stood. "Elias Navarro died after preventing a larger systems failure in Lab Six."

"Presumed dead," said Kade.

Priya's chair creaked. Nobody corrected him. The investigators used both words. Everyone else had begun losing the first one, because the second was easier to print.

Julian put it back. Then he turned the tablet so the table could read it.

*Elias Navarro is presumed dead after preventing a larger systems failure in Lab Six. He believed that technical excellence was unfinished if ordinary people could not afford its protection.*

"One hundred and ninety-four," Julian said.

"Lose the line about his rank," said Kade. "That gets you under one-ninety."

"The biography office requires academy standing. It's a locked field."

Priya held out her hand. Julian, who had learned something about pens this week, gave her the tablet. She deleted the sentence, found the locked field, and typed NOT RELEVANT into it. The form turned amber.

"There," she said. "Now it can argue with me."

At the bell cabinet Anton had the roll of blue cloth out, and Sera had the kitchen scissors. She measured each strip against her forearm, as he had measured Eli's, and cut one for every Meridian student who was going.

Kade tied his own and tied it badly. Mara, beside him, watched the knot fail twice and kept her hands in her lap. When he held out his wrist she fixed it in about four seconds.

The common-room door opened. Taren stood in the corridor with a security officer on either side of him. He had been cleared to attend under escort.

He looked at the cloth in Sera's hand.

"May I wear one?"

Sera closed her fist round the roll. "You don't get to walk into that Hall wearing proof that Meridian forgave you."

"I wouldn't use it as that."

"You wrote reports on him."

"I wrote reports on all of you."

"That doesn't make it better."

"No," said Taren. "It doesn't."

He could have said that his reports had been getting worse on purpose since the House Trial. He could have mentioned the credentials he had burned. Kade watched him think of each one and leave it where it was.

"I'm not asking the House to find me innocent," Taren said. "I'm asking if I can carry him. That's all the strip is for, as I understand it."

Sera's knuckles were white. Kade felt the room lean slightly toward him, the way rooms had started to since the Trial, as if he were the one who settled things. He could have settled this. Taren had helped to save them, and Taren had made the attack easier for the people who sent him. Both were true. Kade had not yet worked out what they added up to, and he was not going to hand Sera a sum he hadn't finished. He looked at the rotor and kept his mouth shut.

Priya got up and went to stand by the cabinet. She did not touch the roll.

"It marks somebody missing," she said. "That's what I was told it's for. It was never a character reference."

Then she stepped back, and the decision stayed with Sera.

For some seconds the only sound was Julian, correcting the Hall's punctuation. Sera cut one more length. She did not hold it out. She put it down on the end of the table nearest the door.

"You tie it yourself."

Taren came in between his escorts and picked it up. "Thank you."

"It doesn't change anything."

"I understand."

He tied it one-handed and made a poor job of it. Nobody helped. One officer noted the exchange on her tablet. The other moved half a step, to keep Taren inside arm's reach. The strip had changed neither of those things, and Kade found that he was glad of it. The House could make room for Taren's grief without pretending trust had come back.

Julian sent the biography with the rank field still amber. Priya picked up the rotor. Kade had nothing to carry, and did not know what to do with his hands. They went out together.

---

He had walked past the Hall of Names perhaps fifty times and never gone in.

Its doors were dark wood. Nearly everything else on Asterion was glass. You could read anyone's rank from across a plaza, and see footage of a bout before the loser had finished showering. The Hall showed nothing until somebody opened it.

Meridian stopped on the steps because Kade had stopped.

"Coming?" said Sera.

"In theory."

"Theory doesn't count as walking."

"I know."

Mara stood on his other side. Neither of them touched him, and he was grateful for it. Ten meters off, Taren waited between his officers with his crooked knot. The doors opened, and because the people in front of him moved, Kade moved as well.

It was bigger inside than the courtyard allowed for. The walls were covered in names, in narrow bronze strips set in rows from the floor to well above his reach. They were not only students. There were faculty and staff, graduates killed at field disasters, and people lost in the early Resonance years.

None of them had a rank or a Standard. Each strip carried a name and two dates and nothing else. It was the only wall on the island that did not tell you who was winning.

At the front, under the oldest rows, stood Eli's portrait. He had sat for a proper academy photograph, straight-faced, in a collar. Kade had never seen a less honest picture of him.

Below it, on a square of blue cloth, lay a bronze strip. ELIAS NAVARRO. There were no dates on it. The mounting pins on the back had not been trimmed, so that it sat slightly tilted, like something set down for a moment.

He leaned toward Julian. "Why is it loose?"

"The Hall doesn't mount a name until the recovery finding is final."

"Then why hold this now?"

"His family asked that nobody be made to wait for an investigation before they could be in a room together." Julian kept his eyes forward. "They also asked the Hall to keep its record accurate. So the people gather and the bronze waits."

It ought to have sounded like procedure. It sounded like the professor who had said *suspended is not rejected*, and like a card that printed *on return* first.

Kade wanted the loose name to mean something. He wanted the device that had gone quiet under the lab to mean something. He could feel hope trying to build a working system out of two incomplete measurements, and he stopped it. An unmounted name meant that an investigation was open, and that was all it meant.

Sera had read the strip over his shoulder.

"Good," she said. "The Hall waits for evidence. Somebody round here should."

Priya set the rotor beneath the portrait, next to the academy crest. It looked ridiculous there. Kade nearly laughed, and for the first time that day laughing did not feel wrong.

The room filled. He had somehow pictured this grief as the size of room 814. Engineering took three rows. Meridian took two. Owen Park sat among the Vector Analysis students. Professor Amadi was on the aisle. Instructor Hale came in late and stood by the doors. Two maintenance technicians sat together in work gray, and one held a battered sensor housing in his lap with both hands, as people hold a dish they have brought to someone's house.

"Who are they?" Kade whispered.

"Facilities," said Sera.

"Eli knew facilities?"

She gave him a look. "Eli knew every person on this island who could unlock a panel."

In the third row sat a woman in a mainland municipal-response uniform. "He wrote to her about the stabilizer," Sera said. "He wouldn't stop."

Kade looked at the dishonest portrait. He had known that Eli had a life outside their room. He had not expected it to fill a hall.

The doors closed. There was no tone and no announcement. Everybody stood, and the minute began.

He counted fifteen seconds and then lost the count. The silence filled with everything he had been keeping out of reach. Eli labeling noodles. Eli reciting a load table in his sleep. Eli holding one end of the rescue capsule in the qualifiers for five seconds, and telling it afterward as if it had been an hour. Eli on the wrong side of a gray door.

He opened his eyes. The minute ended, and no one looked relieved.

Dr. Sen spoke first. Kade had expected *brilliance* and *potential*, and had his anger ready for them. She spoke instead about a student who filed safety paperwork only on the second request, and who had once corrected a faculty equipment specification in the middle of his own disciplinary meeting.

Laughter went round, low.

"Elias Navarro believed that rules owed people reasons," Sen said. "This made work for a number of us. He also believed that if a system failed the people who depended on it most, it was not a finished system. I have not found the flaw in that."

The Systems Lab professor went up next, and he knew her. She had brought the tool case with the orange handle to 814. The program gave her name as Professor Kim. She held up a connector like the one in the specimen bag.

"Mr. Navarro rejected this part from his own design because it cost forty-three credits more than an equivalent in industrial ceramic. I told him forty-three credits was nothing at laboratory scale." She looked at the portrait. "He told me laboratory scale was where expensive mistakes went to become policy."

The woman in the municipal uniform was Captain Ortiz, and she had never met Eli. "He sent me nine messages," she said. "The first was a question about stabilizing a collapsed bridge deck. The next seven were corrections to my answer." She waited for the laughter. "The ninth was an apology for the seven."

Ortiz went over to the technician, took the battered housing from him, and carried it to the front. "He wanted rescue equipment that an ordinary department could afford. He asked me who gets protected first when the good gear is scarce. It's the best question anyone's asked me in ten years, and I didn't have a whole answer." She put the housing down beside the rotor. "I hope somebody finishes asking it."

Sera stood at the lectern and looked irritated that people were looking at her.

"Eli thought every problem was technical until proven otherwise. He was wrong about that constantly." People smiled. "But he also thought a thing could be made better if somebody cared enough to find out why it broke. I've been offered the accelerated track for the Response Corps."

Kade looked up. She had not told the House that. He could see that she had not planned to tell a hall.

"I'm taking it," Sera said. "I want to be the person who knows what to do when a building turns into a problem. He wanted to build what that person would need." Her hand closed on the strip at her wrist, and her voice cracked once. "I hope I get good enough to break every single one of them for him."

The room laughed, and a good many of them were crying as well. Sera sat down fast. Kade kept his eyes on the portrait for a while, to give her some privacy.

Mara told them about the three hours Eli had spent proving that a training algorithm shortchanged left-handed users, after faculty had called the gap statistically insignificant. "He did not accept *insignificant* easily," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not, and she held the lectern so that nobody further back than the second row would know. "He also never knew when to stop explaining. So I'll stop."

Julian did not speak. He had arranged the seating and the program, and he had let Priya check both.

Kade was not going to speak either. He told himself so while a technician described a tool returned two weeks late and cleaner than when it was borrowed, and while Owen Park reported, without expression, that Eli had once spent a whole lunch proving Owen's calculator rounded the third decimal wrongly. Then Priya described being lectured on combat tactics, after the forty-seven-second fight, by a boy several thousand places below her.

"He was wrong," she said. "Mostly."

Every story made Eli more present and harder to reach. Kade's body began making its old plan without him. It noted the doors and the corridor, the air outside, and the distance he could put behind him. Hale was standing by the doors. Hale would let him by without a word, and nobody in the room would think less of him. That was what made it dangerous. There had been nothing to stop him at the shuttle gate either.

"If anyone else would like to say something," said Sen.

Kade stood up.

His legs had gone before the rest of him agreed. The walk to the lectern was longer than any arena floor. When he got there he took hold of both edges and found that he had nothing to say.

The room waited. Ordinarily he would have put a joke into a silence like this one before it turned dangerous. This one already was, so he left it alone.

"Eli broke things," he said.

A small laugh moved through engineering.

"He called it testing. That was generous of him." He swallowed. "He was the first person here who didn't care why I'd been sent. He didn't care what the zero meant. He cared whether I'd eaten. He cared whether I'd studied, and whether my footwork was terrible."

"It was," said Mara, from the second row.

"It was. He built a model of my footwork before he knew me well enough to know if I'd mind."

"He knew," Sera muttered, and the laugh was bigger.

Kade nearly lost his voice then. He waited until it came back.

"I've spent most of my life on one idea. If you don't need anybody, nobody gets a lever. Nobody can leave a hole." He looked at the empty chair Meridian had kept on the aisle. "I thought that was being smart. Three nights ago I thought this proved it."

His throat hurt.

"I was wrong. Needing people isn't weakness. It's just expensive."

There was a small, pained laugh. Kade breathed.

"Eli was worth it."

He could have stopped there. But the housing sat beside the rotor, and somebody had asked that the question be finished.

"He wasn't done. He wanted to build things for people who can't afford Asterion's answers." He found the three rows of engineering. "So if any of you are thinking of turning his proposal into a memorial project, where there's a plaque and everybody makes speeches and nothing ever ships, he would hate that. He'd file a complaint. Professor Kim would have to countersign it."

Professor Kim put a hand over her mouth.

"I can't build any of it. I'm not going to stand up here and promise his work for him. I'm just asking. Build something useful."

That was all he had. He went back to his seat on legs that felt borrowed.

For the last minute of the ceremony Mara took his hand. He did not pretend not to notice. He turned his own over and held on.

---

The Hall did not empty quickly. It seemed that was a custom too. People stayed in the courtyard with small sandwiches and told the stories that had not been fit for a lectern. A technician swore Eli had named three service robots, and then denied it when the staff began using the names.

Kade stood by the courtyard wall with a sandwich he was not eating. So he was two steps away when Captain Ortiz walked up to Sera and held out the housing.

"Navarro said you break emergency equipment."

Sera took it carefully. "Only when the design deserves it."

"This one does."

Up close it was older than it had looked, and one corner had been rebuilt in gray polymer. Sera turned it over. "What happened to it?"

"Bridge collapse outside Denver. It came through the first impact and failed while we were monitoring aftershocks."

"Battery?"

"Connector. Proprietary and discontinued. We had six housings and four good cables."

Sera's attention sharpened. "So you were moving cables between positions."

"Until a locking collar cracked."

"Then you had three sensors."

"We had two. One cable went under the west slab with a responder on the end of it."

Kade could no longer hear the rest of the courtyard. Sera ran her thumb along the cracked collar. "Was the responder recovered?"

"Alive."

Her shoulders came down a little. Ortiz tapped the ceramic piece set into the rebuilt corner. "That's his. He wanted to standardize a replacement and publish the pattern so any department could make one. I told him we could buy new housings quicker than we could certify an unofficial part."

"He'd have hated that."

"He sent me a cost comparison nineteen lines long. So I asked him if he'd ever stood next to a responder who was trusting a part that might fail under load." She turned the housing. Something had been etched very small along the seam, and Sera tipped it to the light. TEST TO FAILURE. THEN TEST THE FAILURE. "He didn't argue. He asked what our certification rig would need. He sent plans for one. We couldn't afford to build it."

That was the other half of Eli's question, and Kade had never thought of it. Asterion could certainly make a cheap part. What nobody had worked out was how a department outside Asterion could afford to prove the cheap part was safe.

Sera handed the housing back. "I start Response assessment this month."

"Your assessment lead told me. Navarro only told me you'd find everything wrong with my department inside a week."

"I'd have to inspect it first."

Ortiz laughed, and took a plain paper card from her breast pocket. It had no academy seal on it. "Call when you want to spend a shift with people who can't replace a cracked housing by opening a laboratory account."

Sera did not take it at once. "Observing?"

"Working. Supervised and unpaid."

"Can I bring his connector plans?"

"If his family and his fellowship committee say so. Not before."

Sera nodded slowly. Kade saw that the answer mattered to her. Being missing had not made Eli's work public property. She took the card. "Then I'll ask them."

Ortiz left her with the housing's failure report open on her tablet. Sera read the report first and the card second, which convinced Kade that she would call.

He was still watching her read when Professor Kim reached him. She held out a data wafer with a green stripe down one edge.

"Navarro's fellowship proposal."

He kept his hands at his sides. "Why me?"

"His family controls what formally happens to it. His working group has technical access. You're listed in the working group as a collaborator."

"I'm not an engineer."

"No."

"Then why did he list me?"

She very nearly smiled. "Under *contribution* he wrote, 'Asks annoying system-boundary questions.'"

Kade laughed before he could stop himself. "What does collaborator mean, if I can't build any of it?"

"It means the committee can't strike out his cost assumptions without notifying you."

"Why would they?"

She looked very tired. "Because the quickest way to get his design through review is to swap the affordable parts for certified ones, and redefine the users as agencies with Asterion procurement access."

"So it would pass, and the whole point of it would be gone."

"He used nearly those words. In the second complaint."

"Captain Ortiz has one of his connectors."

"She has one prototype, made from plans he released to her department. She can keep that unit and test it. She can't distribute the pattern yet."

"So everybody owns a different piece."

"Everybody is carrying a different obligation," said Kim. "Who owns it is what the panel will spend three meetings arguing about." She held the wafer out again. "Nothing is disposed of for thirty days, and longer if his family asks. The fellowship is suspended, not closed."

It was the same word she had used in his doorway.

He took the wafer. "I don't know what to do with this."

"Good. Do nothing with it today."

"I'm not promising to finish it."

"You're not qualified to promise that." She did smile then, briefly. "You are qualified to ask whether the people who finish it kept the problem he meant to solve."

She went back to her students. Kade stood with the wafer in his palm. Eli was gone, and he had still managed to set Kade homework.

Across the courtyard Taren stood at the edge of things between his officers. He looked at Kade once and then away,. The anger was still there. Kade was too tired to pick it up. He understood, for the first time, that this was not the same as forgiving Taren, and that he did not have to pretend it was. He would not deal with it today.

After a quarter of an hour he touched Mara's sleeve. "I need air that isn't this air. I'm going up to the room."

She studied his face. "To sit in it or to pack it?"

"To sit in it."

"Then go."

---

He walked back alone. Classes had started again round the edges of the morning. It seemed like poor manners that the whole island had gone on working.

The card was still on the door of 814, and the third line still said ENDS: ON RETURN, OR DAY SEVEN. The wedge was still under the door. The strip still sat crooked on the bedpost, and the bed was as empty as he had left it.

He sat down at Eli's desk. He had not done that before. His own was a meter away, and all week he had gone round this one.

The air over it was dark where the application had hung. The tool case sat on the bare patch with its orange handle turned outward. He put the wafer down beside it and did not look for a reader. Today was not the day to turn grief into a project. He could remember the line from the notebook well enough without one. *People should not need to be rich, military, famous or lucky to survive the weird future we are building.*

"Annoying," he said to the desk.

His tablet chimed.

It was a maintenance notification, the gray kind that told you a filter had been changed. He ignored it. It chimed a second time from the same gray channel, and he frowned, because housing did not send those twice.

He opened it.

The window was plain gray, and he had seen it before. He had seen it over Eli's shoulder on the last afternoon. There was a short list of channels, and the bottom one had woken up. NULL-7. The sender field said only NULL.

The message was one word.

DUDE.

Kade stopped breathing.

He looked at it until the letters swam. *No.* He had not slept in two nights, and grief did things to people. Somebody could be cruel. A dying system could cough up an old packet. Null seven was a dead-letter box that nothing logged and nothing listened to. Eli's tablet had gone into the spine with him.

A second line arrived under the first.

DO NOT FREAK OUT.

He was on his feet, and the chair hit the bed frame behind him. His hands had begun to shake. It was not the Boundary tremor.

I AM SERIOUS ABOUT THE FREAKING OUT. THERE ARE SENSORS.

He looked up at the ceiling. The little housing sensor sat in its corner as it had all year. He had never once asked himself who could see through it. *Watched wrong*, Eli had said about the lab, on the last afternoon.

ALSO PLEASE NEVER TELL SERA I SAID DUDE INSIDE A FUNERAL WINDOW.

Kade sat down very slowly, like someone with nothing on his mind, and typed with both thumbs.

*Who is this.*

WOW. GRIEF HAS MADE YOU SUSPICIOUS.

His eyes blurred. He laughed, and got a hand over his mouth, because the sound came out broken. Then he made himself stop. He had refused for two days to build Eli out of a missing device number. He was not going to build him now out of capital letters and wanting. Sera had said that if something was there, it would still be there after he had done this properly.

*Prove it. Something that isn't on any network.*

The pause was long enough to hurt.

LUNCH, THE DAY OF. I LEFT YOU MY PROTEIN POWDER AND NOT MY SOLDERING IRON. YOU SENT ME A PICTURE OF A SHOVEL. NO WORDS. JUST THE SHOVEL.

Kade put both hands flat on the desk.

ALSO THE NOODLES IN THE COLD DRAWER DO NOT BECOME YOURS WHEN I AM IN CLASS. THIS IS LEGALLY A VERY LONG CLASS.

Nobody had seen the shovel but the two of them. It could be faked, he supposed, by someone holding Eli's tablet. But the second message was not information. No stranger would have thought to make that joke, and Eli could not have helped making it. It was not proof. It was evidence, and it pointed one way.

He had thought hope would be gentle, and it was not. It hit harder than the grief had, because grief had already shown his body exactly how much there was to lose.

*Eli?*

YES.

Then, quickly, one after another:

TELL NO ONE YET. SEN KNOWS. MARA CAN KNOW. SERA WILL MURDER ME, BUT SHE CAN KNOW AFTER WE MOVE.

*Sen knows.* Of course Sen knew. She had stood at that lectern and said *believed*, in the past tense, to a room with Eli's friends in it. Rage and relief went through him together. An hour ago he had told a full hall that needing Eli was worth what it cost. Now something had come to collect on that.

*Where are you.*

CLASSIFIED ALIVE. IT IS VERY INCONVENIENT.

*Why.*

This time the pause was longer.

BECAUSE THE PEOPLE WHO BLEW UP LAB SIX ARE STILL WATCHING.

He thought of the status traffic being copied to somewhere off the island, and the device that had stopped reporting before the coolant went. The pieces did not yet make a picture, and he would not force them into one.

*Did they try to kill you.*

The typing mark appeared, vanished, and came back.

MAYBE. WE NEED TO TALK SOMEWHERE THIS CHANNEL IS NOT A TERRIBLE IDEA. AND KADE?

*What.*

I HEARD ABOUT THE SPEECH.

His throat closed. *How.*

CLASSIFIED ALIVE, REMEMBER. There was a gap. WORTH IT IS A LOT OF PRESSURE.

Kade wiped his face on the sleeve of his one good shirt.

*Get used to it.*

THAT'S THE SPIRIT.

He laughed, and this time he let it happen, and kept it quiet for the sensor.

Then he thought about what to do, and most of his choices were his oldest habits dressed up. He could go to Sen's office now and break something. He could sit here alone with the secret, because carrying things alone had always felt like strength. But the message had said *Mara can know*, and Mara would not believe a word of it until she had tested it. That was exactly what it needed.

He put the tablet in his pocket and went to find her. He left the door on its wedge.

Behind him the blue strip stayed tied to the bedpost, and the bed stayed empty. That morning he had taken the empty bed to mean Eli was gone. Now it meant there was a system here that he did not yet understand.

# Chapter 11 — The Broken Floor

*Twenty-seven. Mom says three nights running the thing was about a bracket. She says she doesn't know what a bracket is and she's decided she doesn't need to. I looked it up. I'm with her.*

The challenge came up on the band at six fourteen, and his thumb was on it by six fifteen, and most of the minute in between had gone on reading one number.

PIKE, JONAH. 2,301. OPEN. NO FLOOR.

Not open the way Rusk had been open, a name in capitals for the whole bridge to read. Open to nobody. Any first-year with a cleared line could accept it, whatever their number, and nobody would, because two thousand three hundred one was a description of what happened to the one who did. The board paid the higher fighter almost nothing for winning one of these and took almost nothing from the lower fighter for losing. It only looked up if the lower fighter did something it had not priced.

Twelve seconds, the log said afterward, from the post to his thumb. He could account for every one of them.

The acceptance went green. Then a gray line came down over the green like a lid. FACULTY REVIEW REQUIRED. Under it, a room, and Hale's name.

---

Hale's office had a desk under match slates and injury forms and, in the middle of the paper, a bowl of wrapped peppermints that nobody in the history of the combat wing had been brave enough to take one from. The challenge sat between them on the desk glass. OPEN. PIKE, JONAH. 2,301. ACCEPTED. MERCER, KADE. 8,159.

Hale put one finger in the gap between the numbers. "Read them to me."

"Two thousand three hundred one. Eight thousand one hundred fifty-nine."

"Your eyes work. Why."

"He posted it."

"That's the mechanism. I asked why you used it."

Kade looked at the peppermints. "Are those for students?"

"No."

"Then this has become a hostile meeting."

Hale waited. He was better at it than Kade and had no reason to hurry. The clock on the wall did most of a minute before Kade understood that he was going to be the one who spoke.

"He overcommits his right side."

"That's a reason."

"And he doesn't plan for anyone below three thousand. He thinks the number does the planning."

"That sounds personal."

"It sounds observable."

Hale opened the challenge terms. Standard contact rules. No named opponent. No rank floor. Public gallery. Arena Four, which Pike had asked for by name, and whose stands held twice what the first-year rooms held.

"Why did he post it?"

"He enjoys civic access."

"Try again."

"His last win was quick and boring and the clip did badly. He wants a mismatch people will send each other."

"And you'd like to punish vanity."

"I'd like to use it."

Hale turned the desk glass around. Pike's last six bouts came up as load maps instead of footage, the way the combat office stored them: each floor in gray, the force in color, and on all six a hard red bloom gathered on the right side of the exchange where Pike's shoulder had gone through somebody. Clean. So clean they read through arena telemetry like handwriting. Kade's attention went to them the way water goes downhill, and he knew it while it happened, and could not entirely stop it.

Hale laid his palm across the maps.

"There it is."

"What?"

"The reason you haven't said." He did not lift the hand. "You don't need a match to learn that Pike drops his right shoulder. Navarro's model has had that since Tuesday. You requested Pike's archive load maps through this office three nights running, and I sign those, and I've been waiting to find out what a first-year on a yellow line wanted with the force profile of somebody he couldn't post to."

Kade said nothing.

"Director Sen's restriction. No attempt to reproduce the event in the teaching arena."

"I'm not reproducing anything."

"Then tell me what you are doing."

The maps were still under Hale's hand. Kade looked at the hand.

"Finding out whether I can recognize the conditions before anything happens."

"Recognition isn't intervention."

"I know."

"Say the rest."

At Northline an instruction like that had been the moment to stop cooperating. He disliked being walked to a sentence he could see from where he stood.

"If something starts, I stop."

"Something isn't a condition." Hale dragged a blank match card onto the glass. MERCER FIELD RESTRICTION. ABORT TRIGGERS. He turned the stylus around and held it out, handle first. "Write them."

Kade took it. He wrote VISUAL LAG, because Park had made him count doors. Then, after a moment in which he did not want to, HAND TREMOR. Then he sat with the third one, because putting it on a card was the first time it would exist anywhere but the base of his skull and one conversation in Bay One with the lights at half.

COLD LINE.

Hale took the stylus back and wrote a fourth underneath, in a hand that had filled out injury forms for twenty years. UNCOMMANDED TRANSFER ANOMALY.

"Any one of those, you disengage and signal. Open hand, above the head. I'll see it."

"If Pike lets me."

"If you can't disengage, you signal and I suspend the match. That's the part where your opponent and the arena crew and the people in your House matter more than how much you dislike stopping." He pushed the card an inch. "Confirm it or don't. If you confirm, I decide whether I believe you."

"Comforting."

Kade put his thumb on the field. Hale watched his face a second longer than the thumb needed, then applied a restricted-clearance seal, and the seal came up green with enough safety language under it to qualify as a short novel.

"Your success condition isn't winning."

"That's unnecessarily pessimistic."

"Your success condition is walking out with better information and no preventable injury to either fighter."

"That's hard to put on a highlight reel."

"Which is why Pike didn't write it." Hale picked up a slate as if the meeting were over, and it was, and then said, without looking up, "Mercer. You're allowed to find out an experiment was a bad idea before it puts you in a chair."

"That sounds inefficient."

"It's a skill. People practice it."

---

By seven fifty the pairing was entertainment. The student network had it up beside a live prediction model, and the model had done arithmetic on both of them and printed the result in a font that did not hedge.

PIKE. 91.8. MERCER. 8.2.

Eli stared at it over his cereal. "Eight point two is insulting."

"You think it should be lower?"

"I think they're underestimating variance."

"That's the kindest possible version of what you mean."

Taren dropped into the chair beside them without a tray. "People are taking bets."

Sera looked up from the far end. "Officially?"

"No. Which makes the odds more interesting."

Mara sat down across from Kade with tea and did not look at the number. "Why did Pike post it?"

"Publicity," Taren said.

Kade looked at him. "You know that."

"His roommate told somebody in Kepler, who told somebody in Meridian, who told me."

"Your chain of custody sounds invented," Sera said.

"All chains of custody sound invented when you say them out loud."

At seven fifty-five Taren looked up through the Commons glass at the bell, and half the room started the groan, and he said, "Fifteen months, sixteen days," and then, in the same voice, "and Pike," and the groan turned into something else.

Kade looked at the number again. Eight point two had decided what kind of story he was before anyone had thrown anything, and he minded, and minding a percentage was exactly the fuel Rusk had lit under him in front of four hundred people.

Mara had watched him read it. "If you fight the percentage, you lose."

"I'm not fighting the percentage."

"You're looking at it like it insulted your mother."

"It has no manners."

"Pike is enough problem for one match."

He closed the window. It took more than closing a window should.

"Fine."

Sera pointed her spoon down the table. "That was restraint. Actual restraint."

"Don't make it strange."

---

The route from Meridian to Arena Four went through the Hall of Standards, and he had walked it before without slowing, because it was a corridor with faces in it and he had always had somewhere to be.

Eleven bronze portraits in relief, larger than life, curved along one wall above eleven nameplates: the first publicly recognized Standard Eights, the founders' generation, the names every child in the country could recite by ten. Sen was fifth from the left, younger, with the same face. Behind each bronze a stone bay went back into the wall to hold it, and the bays had been cut by somebody who cared about symmetry.

Eli slowed. Kade nearly walked into him.

"What."

Eli pointed at the wall. Kade counted, because he counted things. Eleven portraits. Then he counted the bays, and got twelve.

The twelfth was not empty, exactly. A narrow maintenance panel filled it, newer than the stone around it and a shade wrong, the way a repair is always a shade wrong. Near its bottom edge sat a small construction code. Eli bent to read it.

LEGACY BAY 12. ACCESS SEALED.

"Why cut twelve?" Kade said.

Mara glanced over on her way past. "The hall's older than the display. They hung the bronzes later."

"That explains the stone. Not the arithmetic."

"Utilities, probably," Eli said, straightening.

"That's an aggressively boring answer."

"We're going to a fight," Sera said from ahead. "You can investigate suspicious masonry after Mercer survives it."

A guide in Sol gold came through from the east passage with a line of academy-track children behind her, ten or eleven years old, and recited the eleven names from memory without once consulting the wall. At each bay the children answered with the discipline. Structural transfer. Thermal control. Charge mediation. Biological stabilization. At Sen's bay the answer came loudest, because they had been drilled on it. Integrated transfer. Then the guide walked them straight from the eleventh bay to the arena passage, and nobody said anything about the twelfth, and one girl at the back trailed her fingers along the carved stone until the newer panel broke the pattern under her hand. Her hand dropped. She ran to catch up.

Kade looked at the panel a moment longer. A column on Amadi's calibration wall. A screw in a ring of medallions on his own House roof. A bay in the hall everyone walked through. Three twelves and eleven of everything else, and an institution that taught its history through what it repeated and, apparently, through where the tour did not stop.

He filed it with the other two and kept walking. Asterion had enough strange architecture that one asymmetrical memorial did not qualify as a mystery. Yet.

---

The prep bay under Arena Four had a glass wall onto the floor and a bench, and Eli had Pike's last six bouts running small above his tablet while Kade rolled his shoulders and pretended the roll was for the shoulders.

"He's won five of six."

"I counted."

"He hits harder than Shah."

"I noticed."

"You say I noticed in a way that makes me think you haven't emotionally processed the thing you noticed."

Sera, behind them with her boots on a second chair, said, "That was nearly insightful."

Eli brightened. "Thank you."

"It wasn't praise."

Mara said nothing. She stood at the glass watching Jonah Pike warm up on the far side of the floor, and his profile hung on the wall beside her for anyone to read. Strong Redirection. Moderate Amplification. Fast first step. Heavy right-side preference. Raises output when frustrated.

"Well?" Kade said.

"He does overcommit."

Kade smiled.

Sera pointed at him without moving her boots. "Don't smile like that. It makes me nervous."

"I've been practicing."

"Stop."

Mara turned from the glass. "He's also better than you."

"Helpful."

"I'm helping."

"You and Eli have merged."

She ignored it. "You accepted an open challenge from a fighter whose whole style is large, clean transfers. The kind you keep noticing. The kind in the column you told Navarro not to build on."

Eli's head came up. Sera's boots came off the chair.

Kade looked at Eli. "You told them."

"No."

"I have eyes," Mara said.

He leaned back against the wall. The match was about rank; true. Pike's overcommitment made it interesting; true. He had not said the third thing to anyone, and it was this: Pike made big, clean transfers, the kind that had started to show at the edge of his attention in every drill, and he wanted to know what it was like to stand inside one and watch. Sen had forbidden him to reproduce the event. He was not reproducing anything. Technically.

Sera was watching his face. "Kade."

"I'm not trying the wall thing."

"Good."

"I accepted a legal challenge."

"Both of those can be true while you're being clever in a direction that ends in a chair."

He looked at the floor.

The arena lights shifted above them, the ordinary blue going over to the white the broadcast rooms used.

"If the cold line starts," Mara said, "you stop."

He frowned. "How do you know about that."

"You told four people in Bay One what it felt like. You said cold, and you said it with your hand under your thigh." She said it flatly, an entry in a record. "You weren't in a state to remember which words you used. I was."

He decided he did not want to know what else he had said.

Hale's voice came through the prep speaker. "Mercer. Lane."

---

The calibration lane was a narrow glass corridor between the prep bay and the floor, with a rail at hip height and a technician who had done this ten thousand times. Kade put both hands on the rail. A low pulse went through the metal, asking his Conduit what it had, so the match could be capped to it.

CONVENTIONAL FIELD OUTPUT: 0.00. CONTROL CAP: NOT APPLICABLE.

The technician checked the sensor contacts. Then he checked them again.

"It's not broken," Kade said.

"I'm aware."

"You checked twice."

"I'm professionally cautious."

Across the glass, Pike had his hands on his own rail, and red climbed the calibration trace from the bottom, rose toward the line the match allowed, and stopped precisely under it, like a man parking to the inch.

REDIRECTION CAP: VERIFIED. AMPLIFICATION CAP: VERIFIED.

The gallery above made the same noise for Pike's numbers and for Kade's absence of them. The building had found two different ways to turn people into entertainment and was using both.

Hale came into the lane and read both displays. "Open challenge rule. Either fighter may withdraw after calibration without penalty if the declared profile differs materially from the verified one."

Pike looked through the glass at Kade. "His profile's still zero."

"Correct."

"Then mine doesn't differ."

"That's a very formal way to say you still want to hit me," Kade said.

"You accepted."

"I contain multitudes."

Hale ignored them both. "Mercer. Restriction card is active. Recite the triggers."

The gallery had gone quiet enough to be annoying.

"Visual lag. Tremor. Cold line. Uncommanded anomaly."

"Required response."

"Disengage and signal."

"If Pike prevents disengagement."

Kade glanced across the glass. "Complain to management."

Hale did not blink.

"Signal for suspension."

"Good. Pike. You release on the first suspension tone even if you have already begun a strike."

Pike nodded once, and meant it. Kade could see him mean it.

For a moment the match stopped being a percentage. It was three people saying out loud where responsibility changed hands, and the gallery, which had come for something else, had to sit through it.

Hale opened the lane.

Eli held a fist through the gap. Kade touched it. "Boring feet," Sera said. Mara said, "Don't chase the first exchange," and nothing else, and he stepped through the barrier onto the floor.

---

Arena Four was built to be looked at. The floor was the same white composite as the teaching room but three times the area, the red strip a long step inside a barrier that went up twice his height, and above the barrier the stands rose steep and full to a ring of lights. Sound came down from it, not across. He had read the schematics for this room after Shah, from the maintenance plan that hung off the Arena Safety Orientation, because it had diagrams and nobody read it. Structural ribs ran west to east under the composite. A damper sat beneath each third of the floor, west, center, east. The barrier's footings tied into the same subframe as the floor, so that the whole room was one built thing pretending to be a floor and a wall and some air.

He knew that. He was not supposed to be able to feel it.

Pike waited in the east circle. He was built like an upper-year who had been left in the first-year cohort by mistake, broad through the shoulders, heavy in the legs, with a calm face that had nothing to prove. The calm changed a little when he saw Kade.

"Mercer."

"Pike."

"You're lower than I expected."

Kade looked up at the board. MERCER, 8,159. PIKE, 2,301. "The numbers are visible."

Pike smiled. "I meant in person."

"That was nearly good."

The barrier sealed with its two-note tone. Hale read the rules from the rail with his hand flat beside the abort panel, not on it. Field caps. Controlled strikes. Nothing above the collarbone. Three points, and a boundary exit counted. Kade had heard it a dozen times. His heart went too fast anyway.

He did not look at Pike. He looked at the floor under Pike. Not the man. What the man would have to push against to become a problem.

Green.

Pike came, and the first exchange nearly proved everyone right. The first step was as fast as the file said and the strike behind it had a whole man in it, and it caught Kade on the shoulder and spun him half around, and he recovered before he fell, and only just. The hit went through him in a chain he could read only after it had finished. Shoulder. Ribs. Hip. Floor.

Pike did not rush the follow-up. He walked.

"Thought you were faster."

Kade breathed through the ache. "Thought you were quieter."

Pike came low. Kade checked it. High, and he blocked that late, and the force went down both forearms and told him exactly what Shah's numbers had been, next to these. Pike smiled again, and Kade saw the thing behind the smile. He liked control. Not hurting people. Deciding for them.

That was useful.

Kade circled left. Pike cut it off. Kade did the footwork Mara had put into him one shoe at a time: rear foot later, don't cross, choose before you plant. Pike's next strike missed by the width of a hand and Kade felt the satisfaction arrive, and Pike's shoulder arrived after it and erased it. He slid. His heel found the red strip on the west side of the floor and went over, and the board said one and nothing, and the stands made the low collective noise people make when a hit lands cleanly.

He heard no words in it. He reset on the circle and watched the floor.

Pike came again, and this time Kade saw the sequence before the strike existed. Weight onto the front foot. Hip turning. Shoulder committing. Force already begun before there was anything to hit with it. Not magic, not even Resonance yet. A body asking the floor for permission to become dangerous. He moved on the ask. Inside the line, past the shoulder, Pike's fist going by his ear with air on it, two fingers on Pike's forearm, and a pivot out that put him at Pike's back with a meter of clean floor. He had not redirected anything. Nothing cold. Good.

Pike's eyes narrowed. "Better."

"You people keep saying that right before you hurt me."

Pike stopped smiling. He did not get angry. He got interested, which was worse, and the file had told Kade what he did when he got interested. His output rose. He drove, and the drive took Kade back into the western third of the floor where the west damper sat, because Pike wanted him against the strip, and the load under Pike's feet was large and clean and shaped exactly like the red blooms under Hale's palm.

Kade had the strip two steps behind him. He knew it the way you know where the edge of a table is with your eyes shut. West damper under his heels. Ribs running east under the composite toward Pike. Barrier footings in the same frame.

For one instant Pike was not separate from the floor. Neither was he. Mat. Anchors. Lattice. Slab. Barrier. Two bodies. One connected answer to one physical question that nobody had asked yet.

The cold line drew itself from the base of his skull to the place behind his eyes.

There was time. That was the part he would never be able to say later in a way that made it smaller. Pike was loading. It was a long load, a big one, the kind that took most of a second to arrive, and in that second Kade's right hand hung free at his side, and the panel with Hale's hand flat beside it was thirty meters away and perfectly visible, and one sentence sat on a card with his thumbprint under it. Cold line. Disengage and signal.

Mara had said stop. Hale had said it in four words. Sen had said it in a sentence with a folder behind it.

He wanted to know.

The wanting and the card arrived at the same instant, and did not consult each other, and his hand stayed at his side, and he did the thing he had come here to do.

He did not think redirect. He did not think use it. He thought one word, at the place in the chain where his body was supposed to end and the next thing begin, the place a diagram gave a separate color because a diagram needed one.

*There.*

Pike's strike landed.

The sound was wrong. Not the flat crack of a body being hit. A deep structural report from underneath, the noise a building makes about itself.

The floor broke.

A fracture ran out from under Kade's front foot, split around an embedded sensor panel as if it had known the panel was there, and kept going west to the edge of the ring. The surface under him dropped several centimeters and stopped. Pike stumbled backward off a floor that had changed height under his lead foot. Kade did not move at all.

Red safety fields flashed up along the barrier. MATCH SUSPENDED.

Silence went through the stands. Then everyone spoke at once.

He looked down. He had not struck the floor. Pike had not struck the floor. The energy had gone there anyway.

The cold line behind his eyes went hot, and then it hurt, and his hands began to shake.

Hale was on the floor at a run, which Kade had never seen. "Both of you. Stay still."

"I'm fine," Pike said.

"That wasn't a request."

Kade tried to say the same and found his tongue half a beat behind the sentence. He decided not to.

Park came through the barrier seconds later with two technicians and looked at his face before anything else. "Symptoms."

"Pressure."

"Scale."

"Five."

"Hands."

He held them out. They answered for him. Her mouth did the small thing it had done in the teaching arena.

Sen came last. She did not hurry. He had seen her angry, and this was something else. Focused, the way Elena looked at a monitor when the numbers did not match what the patient had said. Sen looked at the crack. Then Pike. Then Kade. Then the sensor feed on a technician's tablet.

"What did you do?"

He swallowed. "I don't know."

Pike laughed once, with no humor in it. "Good answer."

Hale turned. "Pike."

"What? I hit him. The building lost."

They cleared the stands, which guaranteed the video, because Asterion students could smell forbidden footage through concrete.

---

Medical took two hours and found nothing.

No structural injury. No abnormal Conduit discharge. No measurable Resonant output sufficient to explain a floor, or a wall, or anything heavier than a cup. Park made him recite the day, the time, the room, his opponent, and his last five meals until the accuracy became insulting.

"Tuesday dinner was noodles."

"It was rice."

"That was Monday."

Park checked the record. Then she frowned.

"Victory," Kade said.

"Do not become smug about orientation testing."

"I take wins where they are."

The tremor faded more slowly than it had after Shah. That concerned Park. It concerned him too, and he did not make that part into anything.

Jonah Pike came in twenty minutes later carrying two sports drinks and a bruise darkening along one forearm.

Park did not look up. "This is not a social room."

"I'm delivering electrolytes."

"You may deliver one."

Pike handed Kade a bottle. "Peace offering."

"For breaking your floor?"

"You didn't break my floor." He leaned on the wall. "I watched the replay."

"Everyone has."

"No. The sensor trace. My coach had it up before they locked it." He shrugged. "My strike was normal."

"Encouraging."

"The load in the frame wasn't."

"Less encouraging."

Pike drank. "You scared?"

*No* came up, the old door-closing reflex. Kade looked at his hand. Two fingers were still moving on their own.

"Yes."

Pike nodded. "Good."

"You people have terrible encouragement."

"If you weren't scared, I'd be worried."

"He is already worried," Park said from the terminal.

"See," Pike said. "Medical consensus."

Kade laughed, which hurt, and Pike left a few minutes later. It did not make what had happened smaller. It made the world around it more ordinary, and that helped.

The door opened again before Park could restart her tests, and Hale came in with a woman in a gray facilities coat, white dust on one sleeve and an arena schematic on the tablet under her arm. Park moved between them and the bed.

"Five minutes."

"I need three."

"Everyone says that in medical."

"Facilities structural. I need the order of events before the recording teaches him a cleaner version."

Park checked his pupils again. "Three. If he loses a word, you leave."

The engineer set the tablet on the rolling table and did not play footage. She opened a blank plan of Arena Four.

"Where were your feet."

He marked them.

"Pike's."

Two more marks.

"Direction of his last strike."

An arrow.

"Your intended movement."

A shorter arrow, angled inside Pike's shoulder.

She expanded the plan down to the subframe, and Kade pointed to the western damper before she asked.

"That one took it first."

Her hand stopped. "The public schematic doesn't label the dampers."

"The maintenance plan does."

Hale's face tightened.

"The copy attached to Arena Safety Orientation," Kade said.

"Which no student reads."

"It had diagrams."

The engineer rotated the model. "The western damper took the first abnormal load. Then this rib. Then the sensor housing. Then the edge anchor." She lit each one in turn, a path in red going away from where his foot had been. "Pike's strike had enough energy in it to damage a sensor housing, if all of it arrived there. It should not have arrived there."

"But it did."

"That is why I'm in here instead of under the floor."

Park held up two fingers.

The engineer changed the display. A list came up beside the fracture. Arena Four closed for three days at minimum. Six combat sections relocated. Two team assessments with nowhere to go. A crew to lift the western third of the floor and check every anchor tied to the frame. The crack in the video had looked dramatic. The list looked expensive.

"Was anybody under it?"

"No. The service trench was clear."

His breath went out of him.

She met his eyes. "It's checked remotely before every match. That answer is procedure. It isn't luck."

He nodded. The distinction mattered more than he could have said with his tongue running at three-quarters speed.

"Will it happen again?"

"I don't know."

"Can you identify anything that started before the strike."

He could have said no. Hale was standing right there, and Hale already knew better.

"Pressure behind my eyes. Then the cold line."

Hale folded his arms. "One of your triggers."

"Yes."

"Did you signal."

"No."

"Why."

Because the strike had already started. Because he had wanted the answer. Because twelve seconds had been enough to accept the match and one second would have been enough to stop it, and he had spent the second on something else.

"I thought I could get through the exchange."

Hale let the sentence stand there and did not help it.

Kade looked at the list of relocated sections. "That was wrong."

The engineer closed the schematic. "That's what I needed."

Park pointed her out. Hale stayed.

"The match is recorded as no contest. No ladder movement for either of you."

Eight point two came back to him and looked very small beside an empty service trench.

"Okay."

Hale's eyebrows went up. "No argument."

"Would one fix the floor?"

"No."

"Then no."

"You're restricted from challenges and any unsupervised combat until Park clears you and Sen finishes her review."

There it was. The part he usually fought because somebody else had drawn it. This time the line had his thumbprint under it, on a card he had read and ignored.

"Understood."

Hale took a single peppermint out of his pocket and set it beside the sports drink.

"That was nearly mature."

"Is that one for students?"

"No."

He left it anyway.

Park did not clear him to leave until he could walk two laps of the medical floor without his eyes lagging his head. On the second lap he passed a glass room where structural engineers had already rebuilt the event in simulation. Pike's strike entering his chest. Then the predicted path: body displacement, foot load, boundary exit. Beside it the measured path, running red down through the lattice.

He stopped. Park stopped beside him.

"Do not interpret medical wandering privileges as research access."

"I'm standing in a hallway."

"You're standing in a hallway with intent."

The simulation ran again. He watched the totals along the bottom. Input. Output. Heat. Structural strain. No extra energy anywhere. No impossible multiplication. Only the wrong destination.

"I didn't make force."

Park looked at the screen. "No evidence you did."

"I moved where it counted."

"Maybe."

He watched the red path once more. Whatever he had done, it had not been a miracle that ignored physics. The bill had been paid in full, somewhere nobody had expected to receive it.

Park touched his shoulder. "Walk."

He walked. That was how worried he was.

---

Elena called before he reached the medical elevator, and he thought about letting it ring, and the thought lasted until her name came up a second time.

A hospital supply room. Shelves of sealed gauze behind her, a disposable cap still on her head, which meant she had stepped out of something to do this.

"Hi."

"Turn the camera to your face."

"Good to see you too."

"Kade."

He turned it. She looked at him the way she had looked at a generation of emergency-room patients until they confessed to the symptoms they had planned to keep.

"Pupils equal."

"Park says yes."

"Headache."

"Two now. Five right after."

"Nausea."

"No."

"Tremor."

He held up the hand. The last two fingers moved.

She was silent.

"Are you discharged?"

"Medically. Not politically."

"Explain."

He told her about the restriction and Sen's review. He left out the service trench, and then put it back in, because her face changed whenever he edited around an injury and he had learned to watch for it.

"Nobody was under the floor. They check it before every match."

"Good." There was no relief in the word yet.

"How did you get the video?"

"Darius sent it. A patient showed it to him first."

He leaned against the corridor wall. The clip had reached Northline before he had left medical.

"It looks worse from the spectator angle," Elena said.

"That camera's dramatic."

"It shows the floor dropping under you."

"Only a few centimeters."

"Do you want to keep giving me smaller units, or do you want to tell me why you accepted a fight against somebody six thousand ranks above you?"

The percentage answer was available. The style answer was better. Neither was the thing.

"His transfers are easy to read."

Her face went very still. "You used yourself as test equipment."

At exactly twenty hundred he walked into Sen's office, and the fracture covered one wall, paused, a white line going west across white composite.

Sen stood under it. No Park. No Hale. No tea. She pointed at the chair and he sat.

"Tell me exactly what you perceived."

He did. This time he left nothing out. Pike's weight becoming part of the floor before the strike. The lattice he had known was there without seeing it, west damper, ribs, footings, from a maintenance plan nobody read. The instant separate objects stopped being separate. The cold line. The second he had spent. The word *there*, which had not felt like an instruction so much as a selection, a finger on a diagram choosing which line to move.

When he finished she said nothing for a long time, and he let her, because he was learning that silence could be useful if he did not rush to fill it.

"You need a vocabulary," Sen said at last, "before you accidentally invent the wrong one."

He leaned forward. "That sounds promising."

"It is not."

"Recovered."

She looked at the frozen line on the wall, and then at him.

"Boundary architecture."

Two ordinary words, and she had said them the way you set something fragile on a table.

"Not a Standard."

"That wasn't my question."

"No."

She went to the wall and drew a rectangle around the arena with one finger. "Tomorrow we discuss what we know. Tonight I need you to understand one thing. The floor did not crack because you created force."

He looked at the replay. "Then why?"

Something cold went through him that had nothing to do with the anomaly. On a covered way, weeks ago, she had walked four steps and said yes, it had a name, and not from her, not today.

For the first time since Zero Protocol he had a mystery with edges. He was beginning to understand that edges were where the important things happened.

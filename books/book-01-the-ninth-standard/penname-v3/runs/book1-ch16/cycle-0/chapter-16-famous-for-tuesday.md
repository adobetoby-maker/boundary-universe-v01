# Chapter 16 — Famous for Tuesday

*Forty-two. Or three. Tessa says the count doesn't matter now you've got a bigger number. I said it matters to me. She said that's sad. It is. Boyd has the clip on the desk screen. He's watched you go off the rail eleven times. He says on the eleventh you nearly saved it.*

The Commons had a new sound at seven, and it took Kade half a slice of toast to identify it. Forty people reading the same thing on forty tablets and nobody saying so.

Taren stood at the glass with nothing to count. Fourteen months of Bell Watch had ended in one note the size of an arena, and the ritual had left a hole in his morning exactly its own shape. By the time Kade sat down he had filled it.

"You need a media strategy."

"No."

"You already have one. It's currently look uncomfortable and say something dry."

"It's served me."

"It has. We refine it."

Sera came past with coffee and did not stop. "Do not refine him."

"Thank you," Kade said.

"I'm protecting everyone else."

Eli had the tablet propped against the juice jug and was reading it the way he read telemetry, which was aloud and without mercy. "Six hundred thousand. This one says you're enigmatic."

"I sleep twelve feet from you."

"That has not improved clarity. This one has put your fall to orchestral music. This one has put your fall to different orchestral music. This one thinks you and Vey are secretly..."

Kade took the tablet out of his hand.

"I hadn't finished."

"You had."

The clip was already in the corner of the internal feed when he turned the tablet over, a boy going backward over a rail, forty frames long, looping. Somebody had put a caption under it since midnight. WHEN YOUR PLAN IS SO GOOD IT WORKS BETTER AFTER YOU LEAVE. He read it twice and almost liked it, which was worse than hating it, and gave the tablet back. Under the clip a phrase was climbing the trending column in capitals. ZERO TO 1904.

His band had lit three times before eight. Student Media, who had the tunnel version and wanted the long one. A national sports channel. A company that sold training gloves and described itself as performance-adjacent, which sounded to Kade like a word chosen by a lawyer with a headache.

"Cross said four thousand," Eli said. "On the roof. At midyear."

"It isn't midyear."

"No. He'll have noticed that."

Mara came down last, sat, and did not look at any screen, which he appreciated right up until Priya said, without looking up from her calf tape, "Vey turned down three of them."

He looked at Mara. "You were asked?"

"Yes."

"What did they want?"

"Whether you're as difficult in person as you look on the feed."

He waited.

"I declined to answer."

"That was kind."

"It was efficient."

---

Vector Analysis was Amadi at eight, and the room had rearranged itself for him without asking whether he wanted it to.

There was a seat saved. Not by Mara, who sat two rows down from his usual place with her stylus already moving. The empty chair was dead center, the best sightline to the wall, and a second-year he vaguely knew from the ladder board had one hand resting on its back like a man holding a door.

"Mercer. Here."

Kade stopped beside it. "Why."

"It's a seat."

"There are eleven others."

"This one's got the angle on the wall."

Kade looked at the wall. Then at the lens clipped to the second-year's collar, small and matte and pointed at the seat.

"Does the angle get better if I'm in it?"

The second-year's hand went over the lens. "That's for notes."

"Of course it is."

He took his own chair at the back. Mara did not turn around. "You're developing survival instincts."

"Fame has changed me."

Amadi returned last week's transfer-network proofs face down, moving along the rows without hurry. His came with a sixty-one on it and red in exactly the places it would hurt. Across the aisle the collar turned toward the number and Kade put his hand over it.

"Private academic record."

"You designed the House Trial relay."

"And apparently skipped two conservation steps here."

"Three," Amadi said, from the front, without turning.

"Thank you for the correction."

People laughed. Before the final, laughter in this room would have put heat up the back of his neck. This laughter was friendly, and friendly was more dangerous. It invited him to be the person they had come to sit near.

Amadi put a municipal flywheel network on the wall. Six storage nodes, two variable loads, one node dark for maintenance. Ordinary. No hidden operator, no disputed boundary, nothing elegant waiting under the wording. Six coupled equations and the discipline of carrying every term.

"Mercer. The island believes you can solve a system after falling off it. Show them the method that doesn't require a rail."

Forty faces turned. Mara kept writing.

He went up and took the stylus, and the last two fingers of his right hand did their small separate thing against the barrel, the way they had since the floor, and he closed his grip and made them stop. He looked for the strange angle. People expected one; he could feel the room expecting it, forty people leaning a quarter-inch toward the wall. So he redrew the dark node as an active condition instead of an absence, and Amadi tilted her head.

"Why."

"The dead node changes the distribution constraints."

"Correct. Which the conventional model already says with this zero." She tapped it. "Continue."

He continued. In the third equation he dropped a sign.

Mara's stylus stopped. Somebody near the front whispered, "Is that part of it?"

Kade looked at the sign. For one second he wanted to say yes. A planted error. The first move of something clever enough to justify forty people watching him write. He could feel how easy the yes would be and how well it would clip.

He wiped it.

"No," he said. "That was wrong."

The room changed. Not much. Expectation losing a little air.

"Again," Amadi said.

He started again and carried every term. It took nine minutes and nothing happened. The flywheels balanced, the hypothetical district kept its lights, and he went back to his seat with the stylus still warm in his hand and his fingers behaving.

Amadi faced the room.

"That is what right looks like. Nobody will clip it." She looked at the second-year's collar. "Recording in this room without the consent of every person in it is a conduct matter. Put it away or take it out with you."

The lens went away.

After class a professor who had marked him absent twice without learning his name stopped him to say the final had shown interesting systems intuition. Kade thanked him, because Elena had raised him correctly despite limited evidence. Two Atlas students wanted to review the match with him. Then the second-year without his camera caught him at the stair and invited him to a training group, the one Eli had applied to in his first month and received a form reply from in his second.

Kade said no, and the pleasure of saying it arrived sharp and immediate, and he disliked the pleasure enough to add, "If you want somebody who understands distributed control, ask Navarro. Directly. Not through me."

The second-year's face tightened. "We asked you."

"I noticed."

He walked before the answer could become a speech.

---

The attention reached Northline by lunch.

Darius sent a screenshot of the front-office screen with a regional sports segment on it and Kade's fall in a box in the corner. *Russell is pretending this is normal.* Then, a minute later: *Alvarez says turn it down, some of us are trying to fail math in peace.*

Then Tessa sent a photograph of the whiteboard in Room Four. MERCER, 1,904. Under it, in a different hand: STILL CAN'T FIX THE VENDING MACHINE.

That one changed the shape of the day. Less like being watched. More like being remembered by the right people.

At thirteen seventeen Darius called, and Kade answered expecting another clip and got a tilted view of the front office and half of Darius's forehead.

"There are reporters outside," Darius whispered.

Kade sat up. "What?"

"Two. One has news hair. The other keeps asking whether Room Four students are dangerous."

Behind him Ms. Alvarez crossed the office with a cardboard box. "Darius, if you are behind the filing cabinet streaming this, I will assign you fractions."

"This is not a stream."

"Then Mercer can hear me directly. Stop filming the windows."

Darius rotated the tablet anyway. Through the blinds Kade could make out a camera operator at the curb and a reporter under the faded blue sign, rehearsing a serious expression at nothing. His stomach tightened. Asterion had gates and a media office and a whole department whose job was saying no comment in several languages. Northline had Boyd and a roll of electrical tape.

"Did they talk to anybody?"

"Tessa told them you stole a county bus."

"I did not."

"They didn't believe her. She's taking it hard."

Alvarez took the tablet. Her face filled the screen. "They're leaving."

"How?"

"I explained that every student here is a minor and our consent policy is written in words short enough for television."

"You have a consent policy?"

"As of seven minutes ago."

He looked toward the blinds again. "I'm sorry."

"For what."

"They're there because of me."

"They're here because somebody sent adults with cameras to bother children at a school." Her voice did not soften. "Mercer. You are not responsible for every fool who learns your name."

He went quiet.

"But if a national network calls you, tell me before Darius answers the office phone and quotes a package rate."

From behind the filing cabinet: "That was private enterprise."

"That was fraud."

"The economy is complicated."

The call ended. He sat with the dark screen a while. Being remembered by the right people meant strangers could find them.

At fourteen hundred a message came in from a name he knew from a roof. Cal Reid. *Nice climb. Enjoy the week.*

He showed Taren, who read it once.

"He's congratulating you and predicting your irrelevance in six words. Don't answer him."

Kade looked at him. "That's surprisingly mature."

"I have my moments. Don't tell Eli."

---

He gave Student Media the long version because he made the mistake of thinking an internal outlet would be less strange.

The studio was small and looked out over the western training fields, and the third-year across from him had prepared well enough to make him nervous. A drone hung three meters off, silent.

"Last in the first-year cohort to 1,904 in under a month," she said. "Was the first number wrong?"

A month ago he would have said yes, because the alternative hurt. He thought about Park's control pass, and the word Mara had handed him in place of the one he wanted. Unmeasured.

"It measured what it measured."

She waited. "That's a very Asterion answer."

"I'm adapting."

"So it was accurate."

"At the things they tested. Probably."

"And incomplete."

He looked at her. "Also probably."

"Does that apply to people?"

That was annoyingly good. He looked at the fields. "Most numbers do."

"Even 1,904?"

"Especially."

She moved down her list. "Your role got more valuable after you were eliminated. Does that bother you?"

He laughed before he could stop it. "For about three seconds."

"And then?"

"Then I worked out that was the point. If the plan only works while I'm on the floor, it isn't a very good plan."

She sat back, and he heard what he had done.

"That's the clip, isn't it."

"Yes."

"Tragic."

The national sports request was different. Their producer wanted a remote segment called FROM ZERO TO HERO, and Kade declined it on the title before the message finished loading. Taren pointed out it would reach twelve million people. Sera asked whether twelve million people would help him pass Vector Analysis. Taren, to his credit, admitted they would not.

Mara said nothing until it was done. Then: "Good."

"You had an opinion?"

"Obviously."

"Why didn't you say it?"

"Because it was yours to make."

It should not have mattered. It did.

---

The first open floor after the interview drew a crowd.

Bay One's gallery was a narrow strip behind armored glass that on an ordinary afternoon held injured students, bored friends, and people waiting on a court. This afternoon every seat was taken and people stood two deep against the back wall. Instructor Grae looked up at the glass, then at Kade.

"Popular."

"Temporary."

"So is oxygen. Still affects performance."

Grae paired him with Mara. Four silver zones came up on the court between them. Score by crossing two in sequence, stop the other operator doing the same, and nothing on the card was in play: no structure, no redefinition, conventional pressure and footwork and timing. The bruise on Mara's forearm had gone yellow at the edges. She rolled the sleeve down over it and took her mark.

He shifted left. She took center. Behind the glass the gallery leaned toward the court as one body, and he could almost hear what they wanted. A trap. A reversal. Something that would look inevitable afterward.

He gave up the open right zone and cut behind her, trying to turn her hold on center into a route around the outside, and she pivoted once and put a compact pulse through his lane. His foot crossed the outer line. The court sounded its tone, and she had two zones before he was back.

"One-nil," Grae called.

The second exchange lasted eleven seconds. He feinted the same outside route, doubled back, and tried to pull her pressure across both center zones at once. She did not follow. She stepped into the space he had just given her.

"Two-nil."

Whispers moved through the gallery. Mara went back to her mark.

"You're fighting the version of you they came to watch," she said.

"He seems demanding."

"He's losing."

The third exchange he looked at the four zones. Only the four zones. She took center again and this time he did not try to punish it. He let her have the stronger position and took the two adjacent routes she could not cover at once, and she closed one, and he crossed the other, and then he did nothing. He waited. She adjusted, and he moved on the adjustment, and the court chimed.

"Two-one."

The gallery made a small disappointed sound. The point had been clean and patient and nearly impossible to cut into anything with music under it. Kade smiled.

Mara's eyes narrowed. "Why are you pleased."

"I was boring."

"Don't get attached to it."

She won five to three and he made her work for the last one. When the gallery emptied most of it left without coming near him. They had come for the boy who fell off a ramp and won a final from behind glass, and a conventional loss did not support the story. Grae handed him a towel.

"Useful afternoon?"

"I learned oxygen is temporary."

"Asterion education remains unmatched."

---

He took the sponsor meeting because Elena had sent two lines that morning saying money was real even when attention was foolish, and he had read them four times.

The representative met him in a glass room off the arena complex with a perfect smile and his history in front of her, better organized than any teacher who had actually taught him had ever managed. Northline. Suspensions. Zero. Last place. House Trial. Every bad day he had ever had was a bullet point now, with a market value.

"We think your story connects," she said.

Behind her the slide showed his face beside three silver words. BREAK THE BOUNDARY.

The room tilted half a degree.

"Who gave you that word?"

She blinked. "Which?"

"Boundary."

"It's campaign language. We've run versions of it for years."

He looked at the screen. Coincidence, probably. He put it somewhere he could find it again and made his face do nothing.

"What would you want from me?"

"Product appearances. Training content. A short personal feature. We'd build around the outsider angle. Alternative school to Asterion, last place to..."

"I know the story."

She paused. He saw that she was not being cruel, and that somehow made it worse. She was doing her job. His worst mornings had simply become material.

"How much," he said.

She told him, and he stopped being offended for several seconds. It was enough to replace the car. Enough to take Elena off nights for a season. Enough that his mother would tell him to think before he was proud of it.

He looked at his face under those three words.

"Who owns the story?"

"The campaign?"

"My story."

"You do."

"Then I decide which parts you use."

"We'd negotiate approvals."

"That means no."

"It means shared creative control."

He nearly laughed. The next slide had Northline's front on it, the blue wall gone the color of an old bandage, behind his face.

"Did you ask Northline before you used that?"

She checked her tablet. "It's licensed news imagery."

"That isn't what I asked."

She had nothing for that. He closed the presentation and said he needed time, and she said of course, and he ended the meeting early anyway.

Taren was leaning against the corridor wall outside.

"How did you know when it ended?"

Taren lifted his wrist. "Your calendar."

"My calendar's private."

"House team schedule permissions."

"That sounds almost legitimate."

"Best kind." He fell into step. "How much?"

Kade told him, and Taren whistled.

"Considering it?"

"I don't know."

"Good." Taren shrugged at his look. "Money makes people answer too fast in both directions."

Another surprisingly mature answer. Kade was starting to find them suspicious.

They were crossing the arena plaza when a tour group recognized him and somebody asked for a picture. The refusal was in his mouth. Then one girl said, "I'm from a continuation school," and he stopped.

"Where?"

"Phoenix."

She was about fifteen and deeply embarrassed to have said it, and the others around her went quiet in a way he knew. The quiet after somebody gives strangers the information they need to sort you.

"Mine was an old county records building," he said. "Between a tire warehouse and a storage yard."

She looked up. "Ours was a grocery store."

"Better parking."

"The freezer's counseling now."

"Efficient."

Her mouth moved. An academy guide hovered a few meters off, unsure whether this was on the tour.

"My counselor showed us your fight," the girl said. "She said you started last."

"That happened."

"Did you know you were going to climb?"

"No."

It came out too fast to be impressive, which was good. He kept going.

"I thought they'd made a mistake letting me in. Then I thought they were proving something by keeping me last. Then I nearly left."

"Why didn't you?"

He glanced at Taren. At the training complex. At the Meridian tower against the sky.

"People made leaving harder than staying."

"That sounds bad."

"It was inconvenient."

She smiled properly then. "Did they fix everything?"

"Absolutely not."

The guide shifted. Kade lowered his voice.

"Don't let a school use being imperfect as an excuse to be careless with you. And don't decide you hate every open door because most of them closed."

She nodded slowly, and he hoped it was true. It was at least truer than the clips.

He took the picture, and another with the rest of them. The girl checked the first and grimaced.

"I look terrified."

"Cameras make people perform themselves."

"What does that mean?"

"No idea. My mother says it."

They took a third. After they had gone Taren said nothing at all, which was the correct thing, and Kade found he was grateful for it.

---

The contract arrived during dinner. Twenty-six pages.

He opened it in the Commons and read the first paragraph four times, understanding less each time. Taren, across the table with a strategy review, lasted almost a minute.

"You're making the face."

"I have one face."

"The contract one has more hostility in it."

Kade turned the tablet around. "Tell me what this means."

Taren looked. "It means you need the student advocate's office."

"You wanted a media strategy."

"Media is people. Contracts are traps with page numbers."

Eli leaned over the back of the couch, read upside down, and said, "Why do they need biometric performance data?"

Sera looked up from a response manual. "They need what?"

In thirty seconds it was a House problem. Kade objected. Nobody moved.

Mara took the tablet and scrolled to the campaign description. "They call you an authentic outsider seven times."

"I was hoping repetition would make it mean something."

"It makes it a product category."

Eli had a second copy open on his own tablet and was searching the permissions. "They can retain training telemetry for product research."

Sera held out a hand. "Give me that." She read. "No injury exclusion. If he goes down during sponsored training, they own the footage."

Kade looked at Taren. "How much hostility now?"

"Appropriate amount."

Taren moved to the seat beside him and opened a blank page. "What do you actually want?"

"Not this."

"That's a limit. It isn't an answer."

He looked at the compensation figure again. The number had not become less real because the language around it was ugly.

"I want Mom off nights for a while."

Nobody joked. He went on.

"I want Northline to get something if Northline's in it. I don't want them using Darius or Tessa. I don't want to explain every suspension I ever had so somebody can put sad music under it."

Mara set the tablet down. "Then those are conditions."

"They can say no."

"So can you."

It landed the way her one word had landed after the sports channel. Not advice about which way to choose. Permission to have the choice.

Taren wrote. No Northline images without the school's direct approval. No other students. No retention of medical or biometric data. No campaign ownership of the word Boundary.

"No zero-to-hero," Kade said.

Eli nodded gravely. "A moral necessity."

Sera pointed at the termination clause. "They can leave if your rank drops. You can't leave if they misrepresent you." Taren added mutual termination. Mara found an approval window that gave Kade three business hours to object to edited material, during school days, and said "Generous," and they made it three days.

When they were done the list of conditions was longer than the campaign summary.

"They'll never agree to this," Kade said.

Taren shrugged. "Then you'll know what the money was buying."

He sent it to the advocate's office. Relief did not come. Neither did disappointment. The money stayed real. So did the price.

---

He called Elena from the empty study room off the stair, for the glass door and the quiet.

She answered from the break room in scrubs with her hair pulled back and the vending machines glowing behind her.

"You look tired," he said.

"So do you."

"I'm famous."

"So I heard."

"You sound impressed."

"I changed your diapers."

"Cruel."

Her face softened a degree. "You okay?"

He looked around the room he had chosen. "I liked it for about six minutes."

"Being famous?"

"Being seen." She waited, and he went on. "Then people started acting like they knew me."

"That's the tax."

"Helpful."

"You called."

He smiled. Then he told her about the offer, and the number, and her eyebrows went up.

"Real money," she said.

"That's what you said this morning."

"And?"

"They want the Northline story."

"Of course they do."

"You don't sound offended."

"I work in emergency medicine. If somebody offers real money because the worst day of your life photographs well, I can be offended after I've read the contract."

He laughed, and then he told her about the licensed photograph, and her face changed.

"They used the school without asking?"

"Legally."

"That wasn't my question."

"Family resemblance."

She leaned toward the camera. "Remember something. The people who knew you before the number moved are information."

He looked through the glass door. Eli was outside it making faces, because he could see the call was serious. Sera came past and pushed Eli's face off the glass with one hand without breaking stride. Mara appeared farther down the corridor with books. Taren waved from behind her.

"Yeah," Kade said. "I think I've got some data."

Elena watched him a moment. Then, in the tone the ordinary things came out in: "Your father hated being photographed."

The references had grown rare enough that each one felt placed on purpose. He kept his face where it was.

"Why?"

"He thought cameras made people perform themselves."

"You say that." He heard his own voice go careful. "I said it to a kid from Phoenix today. I told her it was yours."

"It was his." She looked down at her hands as though she had set something in them. He put the sentence on the pile with the others. A man who hated notation. A man who had been to engineering school. A man who would not be photographed, smiling at somebody outside a picture beside a bench.

"What else did he say?"

"Kade."

He heard the warning. Not now. Not on this line. He let it go past him, the way you let a hot pan go past in a small kitchen.

"Okay."

"I love you."

"Love you too."

The screen went dark. He sat with it a few seconds. Then Eli's face came back to the glass, and he laughed and opened the door.

---

Fame lasted longer than Tuesday. On Wednesday a fourth-year broke an academy record. On Thursday a celebrity upper-year announced a transfer to an orbital program and the transfer display drew a bigger crowd than his interview had.

On Friday he left Meridian ten minutes early and took the central path to Vector Analysis instead of the covered route behind the library. The central path crossed two residence quads, the academy store, and the plaza where visiting media set up when there was something to set up for. Nobody stopped him. Two first-years ran past arguing about the record. Three upper-years stood under the transfer display comparing orbital programs. The barista at the plaza kiosk called him Mercer instead of the House Trial guy, and charged him full price.

He reached the room six minutes early. The center seat had somebody's bag in it. He went to the back.

Amadi returned the corrected flywheel proof with an eighty-four on it and one line underneath in her small, unhurried hand. EVERY TERM CARRIED. BORING. CORRECT.

He put it away before anyone could ask.

In the corridor afterward the second-year was waiting, with Eli, and without the camera.

"I asked him directly," the second-year said.

Eli adjusted his bag. "They're building a distributed sensor model and nobody checked whether the nodes agree on what time it is."

"Do they?" Kade asked.

"Not even slightly."

The second-year looked between them. "Is that a yes?"

Eli considered it with more seriousness than Kade thought it deserved. "It's a request for your error logs."

"I sent them."

"Then maybe."

The answer was Eli's. Kade kept walking.

At lunch he checked the clip. Three point two million. He refreshed. It did not move. He set the tablet face down, turned it over, and refreshed again. Still nothing.

Sera sat down across from him with soup and watched the third attempt.

"Are you trying to make the number go up through intimidation?"

"I'm testing the platform."

"Obviously."

He locked the screen. "I thought this part would be more of a relief."

"People leaving you alone?"

"People getting bored."

She waited. He looked toward the ranking wall, where footage of the orbital transfer had replaced his interview.

"A small part of me misses it."

It bothered him less once it was outside his head. She shrugged.

"Everybody likes applause."

"I thought you were going to say something wise."

"No. Applause feels good. Just don't confuse it with love."

He went quiet. She looked at him. "That one landed?"

"Unfortunately."

She took a fry off his tray. "Good."

He looked across the Commons. Eli was arguing with an instructor about drone regulations. Taren was telling three upper-years a story and somehow making all of them listen. Mara sat near the window reading, one foot hooked around the chair leg. Priya had joined Anton at another table and was walking him through something with her hands. Nessa had her eyes shut over a cup of tea, counting nothing.

Nobody looked at him because he was famous. They looked at him because there was an empty chair.

He picked up his tray and crossed the room.

---

He went up to the roof that evening, when the light was going and the wind had not yet come, and got down on the cold concrete and under the table.

The names were a ceiling. Struck ranks and new ranks beside them. Worse ones, honestly written. LEFT TWICE. CAME BACK TWICE. His own line was above his face, three weeks old now and no longer smelling of marker. KADE MERCER, 8,159. Under it, in his own bad capitals, HELD AT THE STEP.

The board said 1,904. Three million people had watched him go backward off a rail. Under here the number was still the one he had written with his own hand, and the House would let him put a line through it on a Meridian Night and not one hour before, and he lay there and found that he could wait to be told when. That was the part the audience could not reach. Not the rank. The waiting for the people whose table it was.

His band lit against the concrete.

*Forty-five. Alvarez says when are you coming. I said you don't go back to places. She said ask anyway. So. Asking.*

# Chapter 16 — Famous for Tuesday

*Forty-two. Mom says the break room has your fall on a loop with the sound off and the night shift does the arms. I said that's not a compliment. She said from nurses it is. Boyd's watched it eleven times and says on the eleventh you nearly caught the rail. He's lying, but it's a good lie.*

Eli was awake before the light, which had never happened, and he was drawing a curve.

Kade watched him from the pillow for a while before admitting to being conscious. Eli had a tablet propped against the wall and a second one flat, and on the flat one a line climbed from nothing, went nearly vertical, and then began, at the far right edge, to bend.

"You're plotting it," Kade said.

"Views per hour. Every hour since the horn." Eli did not turn around. "It's decay. Everything anybody looks at has a half-life, and yours is short, because the clip is forty frames long and the ending's always the same. There's nothing to argue about in it. Things people argue about last."

"How short."

Eli put his finger on the bend. "You're famous for Tuesday. By Tuesday night the new views are under a thousand an hour and by Wednesday somebody else has done something. I'll be within ten percent. If I'm not, I'll say so."

Kade lay there and found the number oddly comforting, and then found that it was not only comfort, and did not look at the second thing yet.

His band had lit three times in the night. Eli had already sorted them, because Eli sorted things. Student Media wanted the long version of the tunnel interview. A national sports channel wanted him on a remote segment. A company that made training gloves wanted a meeting and described itself as performance-adjacent, which was two words doing the work of a lawyer.

"Cross said four thousand by midyear," Eli said. "On the roof. You're at nineteen hundred and it's not midyear."

"He'll have checked."

"He'll have checked twice."

Vector Analysis seated thirty-two. Amadi counted forty-one without appearing to count, standing at the wall with her stylus capped.

"Nine of you are not on my roster," she said. "You are welcome to the subject. You are not welcome to the boy. Anyone who came for the boy may leave now with my blessing, and anyone who stays will be given a problem."

Six left. Three stayed and got the problem.

Kade sat at the back, in his own seat. A tablet stood propped against a bag on the row behind, lens up. He noticed it because he noticed things that pointed at him. Amadi did not mention it yet.

She returned the transfer-network proofs face down. His had a sixty-one on it and three red marks, each at a place where he had let the model carry a term he had not written. The proof was right. It was right the way a bridge is right when the engineer has walked across it and not shown anyone the load table.

"Mercer. You solved this."

"Yes."

"Say the third conservation step. Out loud. From where you sit."

The room turned. He could feel the shape of what it wanted. It wanted the wall trick, the boy who saw the strange angle, and it had leaned a quarter-inch toward him to receive it.

He opened his mouth to give it to them. He could feel it there, the dark node as an active condition, an elegant thing to say in a full room. And it was not the third step. It was a way around the third step, and he had taken that way around three times on the page, and the red marks were where the way around had cost him.

"I don't have it," he said.

"Correct," Amadi said. "You do not have it. You have a sixty-one and a reason, and both are accurate." She uncapped the stylus. "Kim. Third step."

Nessa said it. Four terms, in order, no angle in it. Amadi wrote each one as it was said and the network on the wall balanced, and nothing happened, and the three who had stayed for the boy began to understand what they had stayed for.

Then Amadi looked at the propped tablet.

"Whose."

The second-year on the row behind Kade lifted a hand halfway.

"Did the thirty-two people in this room consent to be recorded?"

"It's pointed at the wall."

"It is pointed at Mercer. I teach in the direction of the wall and I know where it is." She held her hand out. He put the tablet in it. She found the file, turned the screen so he could watch, and deleted it, and gave it back. "Conduct is a form. I would rather not fill it in. Next time I will."

The laughter that came after was friendly. Before the final it would have gone up the back of his neck like heat. Friendly was worse. Friendly invited him to become the person they had come to sit near, and the sixty-one on the paper under his hand was the only thing in the room that had not invited anything.

At the door the second-year caught him without the tablet, and had a card. A study group, distributed control, meets Thursdays, faculty sponsor. Eli had applied in his second week and received a reply that thanked him for his interest.

"Navarro applied to this."

"We get a lot of applications."

"You got one from the person who found the paragraph that won a final. Go and ask him. In person. You can use my name to get through the door, and then don't use it again."

"We asked you."

"I know. I was there."

He walked, and the pleasure of the refusal came sharp and then curdled, the way it did, and he took the curdle as the price.

Mara had not spoken while it was decided. Afterward, when the others had gone back to their tablets, she said, "That one was yours."

"I'd have said no for you. That's a different thing, and it's worse." She went back to her reading, and he put it beside the coffee, and the thing about not having finished finding out, and did not look at the pile too closely.

Hale's afternoon was a zero-field mat with a door, and on Tuesday the door had thirty people in it.

"The room holds twelve," Hale said. He did not raise his voice. He had a foam cup and a slate and had been struck for a living. "Everyone standing past the tape is on the mat and gets a partner. Everyone else is furniture and I don't teach furniture."

The doorway thinned to eight in about four seconds, and the eight stood against the wall and got out their tablets.

Then he paired Kade with the Atlas third-year who had put a flat hand on his chest on Sunday and the rail behind his knees.

The eight along the wall had come for exactly this and had not expected to get it, and the Atlas fighter, a tall, plain-faced boy with taped knuckles, looked at Hale with something close to reproach.

"Walk-backs," Hale said. "Line on the mat. One holds it, one walks him off it. Hands and feet and weight. Nobody lights anything, and I'll know."

There was nothing to redefine. There was a line, and a person heavier than him who knew how to use it.

The first rep the Atlas boy put a palm on his shoulder and leaned, and Kade held for a count of two and then found that his rear foot had lifted without consulting him, and went backward off the line at a walk. The wall made a sound like a crowd at a race hearing the wrong name.

"That's a foot," Hale said. "Not a boundary. Again."

The second rep he tried to be clever and turned the shoulder, to give the push somewhere to go, and the Atlas boy simply went with him and walked him off sideways instead of backward. Faster. The wall did not make a sound this time. It was worse than the sound.

He stood on the line for the third with his hands open and did the only thing he had, which was nothing. Heel down. Weight not spent. He had held a table at a step once with a cup on it, and he held the line the same way, without an idea in his head, and the Atlas boy leaned and leaned and then took his hand away and stepped back.

"That's a hold," Hale said, and wrote something on the slate that had no drama in it at all.

"You let me have that," Kade said, low.

"No. I'd have said." The Atlas boy rolled his shoulder. "They wanted me to put you over something."

"There's nothing in here to put me over."

"I noticed." He looked along the wall, where the eight were putting their tablets away. "Sunday was a good push. It was the right push. I'd do it again."

"I know. I'd stand there again."

That was all of it. Hale ran the other pairs and did not come back to them. The last two fingers of Kade's right hand did their separate thing on the walk to the water, and he put it on Park's card in his head, where it went. When the eight along the wall left, none of them came near him. They had come for a rematch and been given a drill, and a drill was not a story, and he was glad of it in a way that had a hole in the middle.

"Nobody record that," Hale said, to the room at large. "It's not worth the storage."

He went up to the roof when the light was going, before the wind, and got down on the cold concrete and under the table.

# Chapter 8 — The Ladder

Fourth. Rusk came long again, the same lane, the same reach, so plainly the same that a first-year could have read it from the back rail. Kade read it. He also wanted the equalizer more than he had wanted anything inside a building, and the wanting and the reading arrived at the same instant and did not consult each other. He went in. Rusk was not on the end of the reach. He was already turning, and he took Kade's committed step and made it heavier, and the strip came up to meet him.

Three and one. Match.

Rusk lifted both arms to the room, not cruel about it, a man who had asked for precisely this and been given it. Kade stood inside the paint with his ribs talking and made himself take the whole thing without dividing it into parts he could argue with. Rusk had not tricked him. Taren had said don't. Eli had drawn the line. Mara had taught him to make the decision before the retreat, and he had, at a desk, an hour before the tone, because he wanted the climb to keep being public and had told himself that was the same as wanting to climb.

Rusk came over with his hand out. "You read the post."

"Every word."

"Then we both got what we asked for." He shook and let go. "Good fight. Go ice that."

No villain in it anywhere. Only a choice, and he had made it.

Eli was in the corridor with the tablet against his chest like something that might get confiscated.

"Rename the column," Kade said.

"Which."

"Selection."

"To?"

He looked back at the doors and the noise still coming through them. "Who is this for."

Eli's face did something careful. "That's a bad name for a column."

"Learn it."

Taren came down the corridor. "For the record, I said today."

"You said today."

"I'd like that carved somewhere."

Sera, behind him: "I'll carve it."

"All of you. Go away."

Mara came last. She stood and waited, and he knew she would wait as long as it took.

"I fixed the wrong decision," he said.

Her eyes sharpened. "Which one?"

"The one inside the fight."

A pause. Then she nodded, once. "Better."

That was all, and it was enough, and he did not know when it had become enough.

---

The next open challenge arrived the following morning. Higher rank. Cleaner profile. He wanted it before he had finished reading the name.

The model said rest. Sera, passing with a towel, said rest without being asked. His ribs said it in their own vocabulary. He declined.

Nothing happened. No crowd, no applause, no line rewriting itself on the Commons panel. He went to mathematics and got a vector wrong in a way that was at least new. It felt less like winning than winning did. It also felt like learning, and he had not known those could be two different things.

He called at twenty hundred with an ice pack on his ribs and the tablet angled so the ice pack was out of the frame. Elena was in the medication room, in dark blue, with a dispensing cabinet behind her that chirped every time somebody keyed it, and she looked at his face, then at the edge of the frame, and waited.

He moved the tablet back.

"How bad?"

"Three-one."

"I meant the ribs."

"Also three-one." He got the list out before she could ask for it. "Bruised. Medical cleared them. Full range, no fracture signs, no event, no neurological anything. It's in the record. You've got it."

She studied him long enough to confirm the list was not improvised. "And today?"

"I declined one."

"Because of the ribs?"

"Because everybody with useful information said rest."

"Was it hard?"

He looked at Eli's tablet on the desk. The challenge he had not taken had expired an hour ago. Somebody else had taken it, and won, and the winner was up a hundred and nine places on the feed.

"Nothing happened."

"That wasn't the question."

He pressed the ice against the worst rib. "There were four hundred people at the one I lost. He has followers. I knew he wanted the audience. Everyone told me. I took it because I wanted the same thing if I won, and I told myself it was about getting better." He made himself finish. "Today's was cleaner. Higher. I wanted to prove yesterday didn't count. Enough to want to fight with these."

"Bruised."

"You spent five minutes calling them ribs."

The cabinet chirped behind her. She did not turn. "It was worse after somebody else won it."

"Yes."

She nodded. Not approval. Recognition. Then she leaned in. "Do you want the ranking?"

The first answer arrived fast and was a joke. He let it go past.

"Yes."

The word sat there with nothing in front of it.

"Then want it," Elena said. "Just don't make every hour prove you deserve to."

Somebody knocked on the door behind her, two knocks, and she raised one finger without turning around.

"I have to go. If the bruising spreads, medical. Not a photograph."

"Fine."

"And Kade. I'm glad you care about something enough to be stupid about it." A beat. "Less glad about the stupid."

"Yes, Nurse Mercer."

"Don't use the title while you're ignoring the ice."

The screen went dark. He set a twenty-minute timer. Before he slept he opened the model and wrote one line under the column that used to be called challenge selection. *Winning isn't the only way a fight gets expensive.*

---

He found version nine at one in the morning, because Eli was still awake with it and the glow was on the ceiling.

Twenty-three columns. Opponent tendencies. His own reaction time by hour. Failed approaches. Distance control. Fatigue. One called CROWD TAX. One called MERCER GETS CLEVER HERE, which Eli described as painfully predictive.

Then Eli did something he had not done before. He turned the tablet face down on the towel with the drone parts, and left his hand on it.

"There's one more. I want you to decide before you see it, not after. I can delete it without showing you."

Kade looked at the hand. "Show me."

TRANSFER OPPORTUNITIES. It sat apart from the others, with a heavier rule above it.

"The model predicts where force goes. Every fight, it guesses, then it checks itself. Mostly the guess is fine. In four of your fights it's wrong, by a little, in the same direction." Eli said it steadily and did not look away. "I didn't go looking. It's a leftover. The model found it, and I didn't delete what the model found, and you should know it's there and that I know it's there."

Priya's fight first, the forty-one seconds Kade had told him to keep. Then three later bouts. Tiny moments. A stumble that traveled farther than a stumble should. A block that put an opponent sideways instead of back. A collision where Kade had felt less than the acceleration said he ought to. None strong enough to flag. None a room would have stopped.

All of them shared one thing, and he knew it before Eli said it. In each of those moments he had not been thinking about force at all. He had been thinking about what counted as connected to what.

"Do you want it gone?"

The question was real. Sen had forbidden him to try to reproduce the event. She had not forbidden anyone to notice anything. He had spent too much of his life treating technical permission as if it were the moral kind, and he now had a week and a half of evidence about what he did when a thing he wanted came with a warning attached.

"Keep the observations."

Eli nodded.

"Don't build anything on them. No drill. No test. No standing me somewhere to see what happens."

"I won't."

"Promise."

Eli sighed, and looked at the column, and Kade watched him want it, the way he had wanted the six frames, and watched him put it down. "I promise."

Kade held the look one second longer and nodded. Trust, apparently, was partly asking somebody to stop before they had done anything wrong, and partly believing them when they said they had.

---

The review came two days later, where the gray line had put it.

Hale met him beside the lower arena with Park and a tablet full of everything the two of them had written about him, in the order they had written it.

"No neurological events," Park said.

"No unsupervised field activity," Hale said.

"No prohibited experiments." Park scrolled. "Hands quiet, fourteen days."

He looked between them. "That's praise with a lawyer in the room."

"It's clearance," Hale said.

"For what?"

"Keep competing. Same conditions. The field line stays where Sen put it."

He tried not to smile and did not manage it, and Hale saw.

"Don't make me regret it."

"That seems like a hard thing to promise."

"Mercer."

"I'll try."

Hale looked at him, and Kade heard what he had said. Ms. Alvarez had put that word on him at the door of Room Four, and he had refused it for eight years because it was hers. It did not sound like hers now. It sounded like something he had picked up off a floor and decided to keep.

That evening the board ran its weekly rerating, the way Eli had said it would, weighing every opponent by what they had done since. Larson had won twice more. Mills had climbed. Mason was three hundred higher again. Even the loss to Rusk had one clean point in it and no penalties. The number came up in the corner of Eli's model in the color the model used for facts.

8,971.

Ten days of ranked access. Fifteen hundred places, and a little more, in under two weeks. Nowhere near relevant. Nowhere near good. He found that oddly comforting. There was room above him that did not need a miracle to reach, only stairs, and he already knew where the stairs were.

*Twenty-two. Mom read me the new one. She said it like a temperature coming down. I'm not going to explain that. You know how she sounded.*

---

Mara stopped beside him at breakfast with an apple in her hand.

"Rank."

"You've got the same board I have."

"I asked you."

"Eight thousand nine hundred seventy-one."

She nodded once. "You're learning."

He waited for the qualifier. It did not come.

"Was that praise?"

She bit the apple. "Don't spoil it." And she went.

Eli watched her go, and opened his mouth, and Kade pointed at him without looking, and he closed it.

At seven fifty-five Taren looked up through the glass and called it. "Fourteen months, twenty-eight days." Half the room groaned. Sera's napkin arrived where it always arrived.

On the way out Kade passed the panel by the Commons door. It had cycled to the duty rota and back to the ladder, and the drawn glass case was gone. Not struck through, not argued with. Gone, and the card with it. His line had moved out of the frame somebody had built for it, and nobody puts glass around a thing that moves.

He looked up at the bell, and then down the page to his own number, and for the first time since the plaza glass he did not see ten thousand people stacked on top of him. He saw the next name in the band, and the one past it, and the one past that. A ladder was only a system with an order to it. He had always been good at systems once he stopped pretending he did not care where they led.

# Chapter 20 — Run

He went to the transit desk first, before the bag, because he did not trust a door he had not tested.

The landing complex at twenty to six belonged to the cleaning carts and the gulls. The glass wall looked out on a pad still wet from the night, and beyond it the Pacific was the color of a knife left in the sink. Enrollment Officer Cole was behind the transit desk with a manifest and a coffee, and he looked up at Kade and then at Kade's empty hands, and did not comment on either.

"Mr. Mercer."

"Is there a hold on my record?"

Cole did not ask why. He touched his screen, read it for the length of time it took to be accurate, and said, "No. Your mandatory evaluation ended when your provisional enrollment became active. You are not under travel restriction."

"So if I wanted the mainland."

"There is an academy shuttle at seven-oh-five and another at twelve-ten. Both connect at San Diego. The desk requires your band and a departure time." A pause. "It does not require a reason."

The seven-oh-five was on the pad. He could see it from where he stood, a gray shape with its door open and a man in a vest walking around it with a torch. He could be on it. He had nothing with him but a jacket and a band and a notation book in the jacket, and for a second that seemed like the correct amount to leave with. Take nothing, owe nothing, be gone before the place could put a number on the loss.

"Twelve-ten," he said.

Cole slid the slate across. Kade laid his wrist on it, and the form filled itself in from the inside. Name. Medical clearance, with Park's initials on it. Emergency contact. A line for destination beyond San Diego, and he typed his mother's city without letting himself stop at it.

The desk printed a narrow strip. ASTERION TO SAN DIEGO. TWELVE-TEN. GATE EAST TWO. SEAT ELEVEN.

"That's a seat."

"You asked for one." Cole went back to his manifest. "The desk will cancel it if you don't present. Nobody will come looking. That is not indifference, Mr. Mercer. It is the schedule."

He put the strip in his jacket, against the notation book, and walked back over the Central Bridge with six hours in his pocket. He had gone back on the first day to read the stencil at the threshold, the number and the word PERSONS, and had liked the island for it before he knew one other thing about it. He did not look at it now.

---

Eli was awake. Not asleep-awake, the way he was most mornings, but upright at his desk with two mugs and the look of a person who had been arguing with a document since midnight and was losing on points.

"Don't talk to me," Eli said. "Talk to me. No. Read something."

He held out his tablet without looking round. Kade took it because it was easier than not, and stood in the middle of the room reading while the green duffel stayed folded in thirds on the shelf above the drawers, where it had lived since August.

SYSTEMS LAB FELLOWSHIP. APPLICATION. NAVARRO, E.

Under it, a stabilization unit the size of a lunch box, drawn clean. Not arena gear. The paragraph under the drawing said what it was for in the kind of language that had been sanded until it could be read by a committee. Field stabilization for civilian response. Rescue crews. Rural clinics. A unit that cost less than a used car and could be carried by one person down a stairwell.

"You didn't tell me about this."

"You've been busy. Your family has a second file. It takes up the room." Eli still had not turned. "Section four. Limitations. It's due at eight and I've rewritten it nine times and it's gone from honest to legal and I can't get it back."

Kade read section four. It said the unit held a stabilized field for three minutes after physical shock and then failed safe.

"What does fail safe mean?"

"It means it fails safely."

"To who?"

Eli turned round.

"You've written it for the committee," Kade said. "The committee's going to fund it or not. The person who's going to hold it is a nurse in a corridor with a beam on somebody. She reads *fails safe* and she thinks it keeps going. Does it keep going?"

"No. It drops the field. Cleanly. Over about a second."

"Then say that. Say *the field drops in one second and the unit says so out loud before it does.* Does it say so?"

Eli looked at his drawing. "It could."

"Then it should."

Eli took the tablet back and typed with two fingers, fast, and then stopped, and only then saw what Kade was doing, because Kade had taken the duffel down and shaken it open and the mismatched thread on the repaired seam was catching the light from the window.

Eli looked at the bag for a while.

"Right," he said. "Okay."

That was all. No argument. Kade had been braced for an argument and had one ready, and it stood there in him with nothing to push against.

"Three shirts," Eli said. "Not four. You never wear the gray one. Charger's under the notes."

Kade packed. Three shirts, two pairs of jeans, the eleven-dollar shirt with the tag finally off it, the charger from under six weeks of Mara's coupled-field notes, which he squared in the center of the desk where she could not miss them. Two academy training sets, because they were his by page eleven of a contract he had read every line of. The notation book stayed in the jacket, with the strip.

"There's a form," Eli said. "For leaving. It stops the House holding your bed and takes you off the shelter count. I'm telling you it exists. I'm not telling you to fill it in."

"I'll fill it in from the mainland."

"Then for a day and a half you'll be a person the clinic thinks is on the island who isn't." Eli went back to section four. "That's not a guilt trip. It's a number I know. I'd want to know it."

Kade zipped the bag. It weighed almost nothing. It had weighed almost nothing coming in.

"Eight o'clock," Eli said, to the screen. "If you're on the island at eight, come and tell me if I fixed it. If you're not, I'll assume you were right and I'll put your name in the acknowledgments and spell it wrong."

"Eli."

"Don't. I've got a deadline and you've got a seat, and if we do this properly one of us is going to miss one." He drank from the wrong mug and made a face. "Go. I'll be here at eight either way. That's the whole difference between us. I've never once been sure you would be."

---

The bag went on his shoulder and he went down the back stair to avoid the Commons, and it was still not yet seven when he came out under the bell into a courtyard with nobody in it.

Sera was on the Central Bridge.

Not waiting for him. She had a stopwatch on her band and a laminated map folded small in her other hand, and she was walking the bridge at a pace that was not quite a run, counting under her breath, and she saw him come up onto the deck with the duffel and did not break her count. She finished it at the far threshold, stopped the watch, and wrote a number on the map with a grease pencil.

Then she looked at him.

"Forty-one seconds," she said. "Bridge crossing, unloaded, no wind to speak of. Response wants muster-route times for the application. They want them from an applicant, not from the drill book." She looked at the bag. "You're loaded. Walk it with me. I need a number for that too."

"I'm not a drill."

"Everybody's a drill. Walk."

He walked. She walked beside him, half a step back, the way she stood at a fire door with the boots flat, and the watch was running.

"Where," she said.

"Home."

"That's an address. Where."

He did not answer.

"Fine. Why."

"Sen lied to me. My father was on this rock. Everybody with an office knew before I did. Do you want the whole list, because I've been making it since Tuesday."

"I don't want the list. The list is free. Anybody can have the list." They came off the bridge and onto the covered way, and she checked the watch and did not write. "I want to know what you're doing with it."

"Leaving."

"No. You're leaving *before*." She said it flat, the way she said an evacuation time. "Before somebody here matters enough to lie to you. Before the House gets a say. Before Eli's application comes back and you have to be in a room with him when it's yes or no. You've found out that a woman in an office is a liar, which is the least surprising thing that has ever happened on this island, and you're using it to get out ahead of the thing that actually scares you."

"Which is?"

"Being here when the bill comes."

The landing complex was in sight at the end of the covered way, glass and steel and the flowering thing he still had no name for.

"Don't tell me what I'm scared of."

"I'm not telling you. I'm recognizing it." She checked the watch. "I left my first placement during a fire drill. I was fourteen. A drill is the one time in a building nobody is counting heads, because everybody thinks somebody else is. I walked out the muster gate with the count still going and nobody noticed for six hours. I told myself for a year that proved something about them."

"It did."

"It proved I'd found the one minute they couldn't see me and used it." She let that sit. "That's what you're doing. You've found the one minute nobody's stopping you. It's not the same thing as being free."

The bag strap had found the place on his shoulder where the bag always found him.

"Second placement, I stayed a month," Sera said. "Kitchen fire. Grease pan, then the curtain. Two adults with keys on their belts and a binder of procedures, and they stood there and looked at each other for exactly as long as it takes a curtain to go. The kids didn't. A boy I'd never spoken to took the extinguisher off the wall because it was nearer him than any of the adults, and I got the little ones out the back because I'd counted the back door on the first day. I count doors. That's why."

"That's why Response."

"That's why I decided I was done being in rooms where the person with the keys is automatically the person who knows what to do. Response doesn't care where I went to school. They care whether I can get forty people out of a building that's coming down and lose none of them." She looked straight ahead. "That's mine. It's not about you. I'm saying it so you know I've got somewhere to be that isn't standing in your doorway."

"You're not in my doorway."

"No. I'm walking you to the gate. Because nobody walked me to mine, and I've thought about that more than I'd like."

They reached the glass doors. She stopped the watch and looked at it and wrote the number on the map. Then she folded the map and put it in her pocket.

"I'm not coming in," she said. "I've got the west route to time and I'm not going to stand at a window watching to see what you do, because that makes it about me, and it isn't. You've got a seat. Use it or don't." She looked at the bag one more time. "If you get on, message me from the far side. Not for me. For the count. I'd rather know the number than search the water."

She went. He watched her go up the west stair at that not-quite-run, and she did not look back, and he understood that it had cost her not to.

---

He sat on a bench by the glass with the Pacific in front of him and the bag between his feet, and he had five hours.

The terminal filled and emptied with people who had ordinary places to be. A family put a second-year on the seven-oh-five with a food container she had clearly been told to eat on the way. An upper-year slept sitting up with his hand through the strap of his own duffel. The seven-oh-five left. Nobody looked at Kade to see whether he was on it.

His hands wanted work. He took out the notation book and found the exercise from last week, the one Eli had written the hash strings behind in pencil.

He owed a Sol second-year a model.

He had walked out of Amadi's room on a word, and the partner who labeled her gauges before she touched them had finished the frame alone, and he had let the debt sit for two days as one more line on a list. He drew the frame. Four members as drawn. Two supports. The load at the top. Then, because he had had two days to stop believing the drawing, he drew the cover plate as a member, a rectangle with four bolts and a stiffness of its own, and let gauge three read what it read.

The numbers came out. Close enough to say that whatever was behind the plate had been carrying a third of the load the paper gave to member two, from the beginning. The paper had never been wrong about the physics. It had been wrong about what was there.

He sent it before he could improve it. *You finished the frame. Here's the model. The plate's a member. I should have drawn it Tuesday.*

She answered inside a minute. *Check the sign on support two. It's opposite. You always do that.*

It was opposite. He fixed it and sent it again, and she sent back a single mark that meant received. A debt incurred walking out of a room, paid from a bench while walking out of an island. In August he would not have paid it. He would have let the room close behind him and called the room the problem.

He put the book away and looked at the pad, and the twelve-ten came in over the water, low, and settled, and the five hours were gone.

---

The line at East Two was short. The sleeping upper-year, awake now. Two first-years with a case between them. A woman in a coat that was too warm for the island and exactly right for the mainland.

An attendant with a slate said, "Mercer, seat eleven," and he held the strip up before he had decided to, and the reader chimed, and the green square beside eleven turned solid on her screen. Then the covered walk, and the aircraft door, and the seat.

He sat in it. Eleven was a window. The bag went under the seat in front, the belt went across, the cabin smelled of cold air and somebody's coffee, and nobody, at any point, had put a hand on his arm.

No wall. No official. No director with a reason. No friend in the doorway. Sera had walked him to the glass and left. Eli had told him the time and gone back to section four. Cole had printed the strip. The whole island had let him go with less resistance than a supply closet.

He had come across a bridge with his father's name in his pocket and eight years of leaving rooms behind him, ready for a fight, and the fight had not come, and there was nothing in the seat with him but the choice.

He called his mother, because that was the promise, and because he wanted to do it from inside the thing rather than from a bench beside it.

She answered from her own kitchen with a mug in her hand, and looked at the seat back behind him, and the window, and knew.

"You're on it."

"You hear it first. That's the deal."

"I'm hearing it." She set the mug down. "Are you coming home?"

"I've got eleven minutes to decide. They're loading a case."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know."

She did not fill it. She let the eleven minutes be his, and he had never in his life been so grateful to be given nothing.

"I'm going to tell you two things," Elena said, "and neither of them is stay and neither of them is go. If you land here tonight I'll be at the rail with my coat on from a shift and I'll be glad. If you get off that aircraft, I won't think you picked that place over me. And if you come home because you've done the sum and it isn't worth what it costs, I won't call it running."

"What would you call running?"

"Leaving before you've found out what it costs."

Sera had said it on a bridge. Now his mother. He had apparently been handed a diagnosis by two women who had never met, and it was the same diagnosis, and it was an awful thing to be that legible.

"I found out," he said.

"Then say it."

He looked out the window. The pad, and the covered walk, and the glass, and beyond the glass the covered way that went up to the bridge with the stencil at the threshold. He could not see the bell from here. He could feel where it was.

"A stabilizer that says out loud when it's about to drop the field. It's due at eight. I told him what was wrong with it and I want to know if he fixed it." The words came out in the order they were stacked, not the order he would have chosen. "Six weeks of somebody's notes on my desk where she'll see them and know what it means. A woman timing bridges for a job she wants more than she wants me to stay, who walked me to the gate anyway. A rival who'll be improving while I'm on the mainland and won't slow down for it. A bell I've heard ring once."

"That's the cost."

"That's the cost."

"And the other column?"

"Every person with an office on this rock knew my father before I did. One of them sat in a waiting room and told you to be quiet. That doesn't go away because I've got friends."

"No," his mother said. "It doesn't. You get to hold both."

Behind him the attendant said something to the woman in the coat about the door, and there was a change in the sound of the cabin, the way a room changes when it is about to be sealed.

"I'm not coming home today," he said.

"Then I'll see you when you do."

"Mom."

"Yes."

"I'm still angry. At her. At you."

"I'd be worried if you weren't. I haven't asked you not to be." She picked the mug back up. "You're going to go and stand in front of her."

"Not alone. Somebody as far as the door." He had not known that was the plan until it was in the air. "You've heard it first. That's both."

"That's both. Go on. Get off the aircraft before they make it a thing."

He ended the call. He unbuckled the belt. He pulled the bag from under the seat, and stood in the aisle of an aircraft that was about to leave, and the attendant looked at him with the slate in her hand and no expression at all.

"I'm not traveling."

She did not ask. She touched the screen, and the solid square beside eleven went hollow, and she stood aside from the door.

"Mind the step, Mr. Mercer."

He minded it. He walked back up the covered walk with the bag on his shoulder and stood at the glass and watched the door close on the twelve-ten without him in it, and watched it lift, and turn over the water, and go. Nobody in the terminal looked up.

Cole did not look up either.

"Seven-oh-five tomorrow," Cole said, to the manifest. "And twelve-ten. And the day after."

"There's one every day."

"Yes. That is what a schedule is." He made a mark. "Whether you are on this island is still the one line on it that belongs to you. I said that on your first night. I would not have said it if it were going to change."

"You watched me get on."

"I watched you get off. That is the part I have not seen before." Cole turned a page. "Go and eat something."

---

He messaged Mara from the bridge. SPIRE. NOW. YOU STOP AT THE DOOR.

She was on the reception bench when he got there, tablet dark, and she took in the bag and the strip in the pocket and said, "Sera told me you'd be on the twelve-ten or you'd be here. She didn't say which."

"She was timing the west route."

"She timed it twice. I watched her from the carrel." Mara did not move to stand. "The screen still says security session. The visiting officials are in the long room. You won't get in."

"I'm not going in. I'm going to sit."

He sat on the far end of the reception bench with the bag between his feet, in front of a desk that had told him yesterday to come back Thursday. It was the oldest thing he knew how to do. In Room Four you sat in the chair the system put you in and did not leave it, and eventually the system had to look at you, because you were on its floor and it could not file you.

Twenty minutes. Then thirty. People who were not from the island went between doors with folders, and one of them looked at the duffel and looked away with the blankness of a person who has been told not to notice things. Mara read. He did not.

At the thirty-eighth minute Sen came out of a door at the corridor's end with two people behind her, and saw him, and her face did nothing. She said something to the two people. They went back in. She came down the corridor alone and stopped in front of the bench, and looked at the bag, and then at the strip.

"You have a seat."

"I had one."

"There's a stairwell," Sen said. "I have nine minutes before they notice I'm not in the room. It's not an office and I can't make it one."

Mara did not get up. That was the agreement. He followed Sen through a plain door into a concrete stair that smelled of paint, and she sat on the third step as if she had done it before, and he stood, because he wanted to be standing.

He put the export on his band and turned it toward her. The movement order. Twenty-three days. A roster with the three digits on it. PROJECT CONTINUITY. MEDICAL OVERSIGHT: A. SEN. CONSENT STATUS: REAUTHORIZED.

"In the doorway. I asked you if anyone had done it before. You said no."

"I lied to you."

No shape around it. It went in further than a defense would have.

"You sat four hours in a waiting room and told my mother to keep quiet. You told her it was for me."

"I told her the official story was the safest story I knew how to give you. I still don't know a safer one."

"That's a careful sentence."

"Yes."

"Was he Zero?"

"He was."

"What happened to him?"

Sen looked at the paint on the wall for a long moment.

"I can't tell you that. Not yet."

"Of course."

"I know how that sounds."

"No. You don't."

She held his eyes. "You're right. I don't."

That stopped him where nothing else had.

"I made decisions about your family," she said, "under conditions you did not create and without your consent. Some of them protected you. Some of them did harm. I am not going to tell you which is which, because I don't fully know, and I'm not going to ask you to believe that I meant well, because meaning well is not evidence."

"Then what is."

"What I can show you." She glanced at the door. Seven minutes.

He thought of a frame with a cover plate bolted over one bay. Gauge three reading what it read. A drawing that had never been wrong about the physics and had been wrong about what was there.

"You've got a drawing of me," he said. "This school has. Every gauge on me reads. Footwork to four places. And there's a plate over one bay with my father behind it, and I'm not asking you to take the plate off. I'm asking for the drawing with the plate on it. Every cover. Where it is. Who bolted it. What they wrote down as the reason."

Sen was very still.

"You want the withholding, not the withheld."

"I want the shape of what I'm not allowed to see. If a gap's got a name on it, I can stand in front of the name. If it hasn't, it's just a hole, and you decide when I've earned the edges."

"For records held under academy authority," she said slowly, "that's something I can do. I can release your complete file. Every version of the Zero Protocol order. Every routing. And an index of what has been withheld, with the withholding authority against each entry. The sources stay redacted. The identifiers and the authorities do not."

"And the records before I got here."

"That search already exists."

He stared at her. "You searched for me."

"After your structural event. I needed to know what people outside this academy already had."

"The Directorate," he said. He had the word from a friend on a step with a sandwich, and he used it the way he tested a shoulder before a punch.

She did not say yes. She did not say no. She said, "I needed to know what they already had before they came to ask me for more."

"And the person being searched didn't get the list."

"I believed showing it to you would expose people I was still protecting."

"Do you still believe that."

Sen opened her tablet and separated two documents without answering, and he understood that the answer was the documents. One carried the academy seal. The other ran off the bottom of the display, identifiers and authority codes and blocks marked WITHHELD, and she pushed both to his band and he opened them before the light had faded. The file began with Cole's first report from a stairwell at Northline. The index began years before that. No answers. But every hole had a shape now, and every shape had a name beside it claiming the right to keep it empty.

"Did you ask anyone to stop me leaving?"

"No."

"Nobody. Cole. The House."

"Not at my direction."

"That's careful."

"It's exact."

He could work with exact. He could also check it, and she knew that.

"Why did you get off?" she said.

"That's mine."

"I know." She looked at the bag. "Do you?"

The anger came up and went down far enough for him to hear the question under it, and he thought of a hollow square on an attendant's slate.

"I got off for them," he said. "Not for the school. Not for you. I'll stay long enough to stand in front of every name on that index. That's the size of it."

"The answers may cost more than you think."

"Everything on this rock does."

Three minutes. She stood. At the door she said his name, and he turned.

"I'm sorry."

He waited for something useful to arrive with it. Nothing did.

"Noted," he said. Not forgiveness. A line in a column, with a source beside it, that she could be held to.

Mara stood when he came out. She looked at his face and did not ask, and walked beside him to the bridge, and he told her on the way, all of it, because she had built the first row, and when he finished she said only, "Page fourteen is still wrong," and he laughed, once, out over the water, and it surprised them both.

---

Eli was in the room at ten past six with the tablet face up and section four on it.

"Did I fix it?"

Kade read it with the bag still on his shoulder. *The unit announces field loss audibly two seconds before the field drops. The drop takes one second. This is a design limit, not a fault.*

"You fixed it."

"Good. It went in at seven fifty-two. I put you in the acknowledgments and spelled it right, which I want noted, because I considered the alternative." Eli finally looked at the bag. "You came back for the shelter count."

"I came back for the stairwell."

"Which one?"

"Both."

He put the duffel on the bed and took the first shirt out, and the second, and Eli leaned on his desk and did not help, which was a kind of help.

Shirts in the drawer. The cable. The training sets. Mara's notes were still squared in the center of the desk where he had left them for her to find, and he moved them back to the corner, where they lived, and sent her one line.

MAY I KEEP BORROWING THESE?

UNTIL THE NEXT REVIEW. YOU HAVE HEARD ABOUT PAGE FOURTEEN.

The strip was in his pocket. He took it out and looked at it. Seat eleven. Twelve-ten. A hollow square somewhere on a slate in the landing complex where a solid one had been. He turned it over, and the back was blank, printed for nothing, and he wrote on it in the notation-book pencil.

ACADEMY FILE. RECEIVED.

INDEX. RECEIVED.

EVERY PLATE HAS A NAME ON IT.

"That's an aggressive use of transit paperwork," Eli said.

"She's a structures person."

"You made the Director a list on a boarding pass."

"She knows where I got it."

He put it under the edge of Eli's projection, where he would see it from the bed.

Then the drawer, and under the paper map, face down where it had been since the first night, the square of paper in his mother's chart-print. *You are allowed to leave. You are also allowed to stay.* He had needed the first line for four months. He turned the paper so the second line was on top, and put it under the pencil.

The duffel was empty on the bed. He folded it in thirds, the way it had lived since August, and put it back on the shelf above the drawers where he could see it from the pillow.

Eli watched him do it. "Not under the bed?"

"I want to see it."

"Every morning."

"That's the point. It's a shelf. It's not a door."

Downstairs the Commons was mid-dinner and nobody applauded, and he was grateful. Sera looked up from a plate and a laminated map with grease-pencil numbers on it, and said, "West route, loaded, forty-nine seconds. You're slower than the bridge," and went back to eating, and that was the whole of what she said about it. Anton slid a plate across without a word.

Taren's face went unreadable for half a second and came back easy, and Kade saw the half second and put it where he had put the sandwich on the steps. Later.

Mara, from the far end of the table, said, "Did she give you anything?"

"The academy file. And an index of everything she won't give me, with a name on each line."

"And the answers?"

"Still shut. But I know where the bolts are."

He sat. He ate. Tomorrow at seven-oh-five there would be a shuttle, and at twelve-ten another, and none of them would look for him. He had wanted a wall so he could push against it. There was no wall. There was a shelf, and a bag on it he could reach from his bed, and a table of people who had each refused to make his leaving easy or his staying free.

That was not trust. He was not sure yet what it was.

It was enough to be here at eight.

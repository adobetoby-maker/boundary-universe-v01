# Chapter 20 — Run

The green duffel came down off the shelf above the drawers at six in the morning, and it was still folded in thirds the way it had lived there since August, and Kade shook it open and the handler's tag was still through the handle with the mismatched thread. He left the tag on. It would save somebody printing another.

Packing took eleven minutes. Most of that was socks.

Three shirts, two pairs of jeans, the eleven-dollar shirt with the tag finally off it, the charger, the notation book. Two academy training sets, because they were his now by page eleven. When he pushed the top drawer shut the room looked exactly the way it had in August, except that the bag was full, and the second bed was not bare.

Eli sat in his desk chair with his feet on the rail of it and watched the whole thing without offering to help, which was a kind of help.

"You know this is a terrible plan."

"We agree."

"Good. I like to establish common ground before I lose an argument." Eli tipped the chair back. "Where."

"Home."

"Home is eight hundred miles away."

"I know where it is."

"Do you? Because you've packed for somewhere closer. You packed for the hall."

Kade did not answer that. He went round the room from the outside, checking for anything he might have mistaken for his. Two library books under Eli's chair. The chipped Meridian mug on the sill belonged to the House kitchen, and had come up here in October full of something Sera had made. The second blanket had appeared from a supply closet when the island turned cold and he had never learned whether it was issued or merely arrived.

Mara's coupled-field notes were on his desk, six weeks of her handwriting with his pencil marks in the margins where he had been wrong. He squared them in the middle of the desk where she would see them. He put the pencil in the bag.

Eli watched the notes go down. "You're returning objects."

"They're hers."

"They've been in this room six weeks. They have tenancy." He let the chair drop onto its front legs. "Is there a form?"

"For what?"

"Leaving. There's a departure form. It tells the House to stop holding your bed and your rotation, and it tells the clinic to stop counting you for shelter capacity. It's dull. It exists." He waited. "Are you filling it out?"

"No."

"Then you're not leaving. You're disappearing with luggage."

"That's an administrative distinction."

"Administration is how a hundred and eighty people know whether to search the water for you."

Kade's hand stopped on the drawer pull. Outside in the corridor somebody ran past late, and a door banged, and a first-year shouted that the lift had stalled again, and three voices told him to take the stairs. The House went on around the possibility of his leaving it without lowering its voice at all.

He opened the top drawer for the charger cable and found the House rotation sheet under the paper map, the print already going soft at the folds. Thursday. KITCHEN CLOSE. MERCER, with KIM struck through beside it, because Nessa had a placement interview Thursday and he had said yes without thinking about it, because it had cost nothing to say.

He took the cable and left the sheet.

"Who's got Thursday?" Eli said.

"Somebody."

"Excellent. I'll tell the administrator somebody has volunteered. We'll put the contact as *elsewhere*."

Kade shut the drawer harder than it needed.

He had come to this island carrying almost nothing. Leaving should have been the easy direction. It was not, and that annoyed him more than the lies had, and he looked for somewhere to put the annoyance and found, over Eli's desk, the thing that had been hanging there for weeks.

A projection. A stabilization rig the size of a lunch box, drawn in wire, with a rough ring of components around the core picked out in red.

"What is that?"

Eli glanced up at it as if it had been somebody else's. "My application."

"For what."

"Systems Lab fellowship."

Kade stood with the cable in his hand. "You didn't tell me."

"You've been busy. Secret family history takes a lot out of a room." Eli reached up and turned the model. "I want to build stabilization hardware that costs less than a car. Not arena gear. Civilian. Rescue crews. Rural hospitals. A clinic that can't buy one Conduit subsystem worth more than the building it's in."

Kade looked at him.

Eli looked uncomfortable. "What."

"Nothing."

"That was suspiciously sincere. Don't make it strange."

"You made it strange. You've got dimensions." Kade came closer to the projection. "Why are those red?"

"They're not failing. They're the parts I can't make cheap. Commercial field sinks hold a surgical tolerance for six hours, and precision is what you pay for. A rescue crew doesn't need six hours. They need a thing that survives being dropped down a stairwell and buys them three minutes."

"Three minutes for what?"

"Lift a beam. Restart a pump. Walk somebody past a live field. Depends who's holding it." He expanded the power assembly. Two replacement designs unfolded beside it, both crossed out. "The fellowship lets me break versions with the lab's money. Ideally one stops breaking before they notice the pattern."

There was a date in the corner of the projection. Five days.

Eli had built a future over his desk in the same room where Kade had just packed his past into one bag, without a word, in the evenings.

"Put the stairwell in the application," Kade said.

"I did. In respectable language."

"That's dishonest."

"It's called grant writing."

Kade nearly smiled. Then the strap on his shoulder reminded him what it was for.

---

Sera stepped into the doorway and stopped in it, and looked at the bag, and then at him.

"No," she said.

"No what?"

"No exits before breakfast. You packed like an orphan in a documentary."

Eli pointed at her. "Thank you."

Kade shifted the strap. She was not blocking the door. She was occupying it, with intent, one shoulder on the frame and both boots flat, the way she stood at a fire door during a drill.

"You're not going to stop me."

"Walk around me." She let that sit. "First I'm going to make you say it out loud. Why."

"Because Sen lied to me."

"That's a reason. What does it cost you?"

He had expected her to argue with the reason. "Cost?"

"You get the reason for free. Everybody gets the reason. I want the other column." She did not move. "Sen lied. Fine. What do you lose by leaving over it?"

"Nothing I can't get on the mainland."

"Say it anyway."

"Vector Class." It came out before he chose it. "The notation. I'm eleven weeks into something I couldn't read in September."

"Next."

"My father was on this rock."

"And it costs you."

"The desk under the stair. Eli's got hash strings for cards I can't read from eight hundred miles."

"Next."

"My mother got sat in a waiting room and told to be quiet, for me."

Sera's face changed, slightly. "That one matters. What does it cost."

"Nothing. She's at the other end of the trip."

"Then say the name of the woman who did it, and say where she lives."

He said nothing.

"Next," Sera said. "Everybody on this island knew more about your life than you did."

"Enough of them."

"Cost."

He held the strap and did not answer, because the answer was standing in the doorway and sitting in the chair, and saying so would have made it a thing that could be used.

Sera let the silence run long enough to make the point and then said, quietly, "You always do this?"

"Do what?"

"Leave before the place gets a vote."

He turned. "Don't psychoanalyze me."

"I'm not. I'm recognizing myself." She looked past him at the window. "My first placement lasted nine days. I told everyone the school was useless." A shrug. "It mostly was. The second lasted a month. I left because a counselor lied to me."

"That sounds reasonable."

"It sounded reasonable. That's the trick of it." Her thumb found a pale seam in the doorframe and worked at it. "I liked people there. There was a girl who waited outside my door every morning because she couldn't stand walking into breakfast alone. I taught two kids how to get onto the laundry roof without tripping the window alarm. There was a maintenance man who kept the good cereal back for Fridays. When I left I told myself none of that counted, because the counselor lied. It made the bag lighter."

"Did you tell her? The girl."

"I left a note."

"That's not telling her."

"It isn't." No defense in it. "She mailed it back. No message. My own handwriting in an envelope." Sera looked at the zipper he had drawn shut with so much care. "I've still got it."

Kade pictured the envelope and did not let himself invent what was in it. The counselor's lie had been reason enough. It had not made the leaving cost nothing.

"Why'd you stop?" he said.

"Stop what?"

"Leaving."

She thought about it, honestly, the way she thought about a route. "I got tired of letting the worst person in every building decide whether I got to keep the good ones."

That went in under the strap somewhere.

"And because I want something," she said.

"The Response rotation."

"That's the door. Not the room." She said the next part flatly, so it would not sound like a speech. "Disaster Response Corps. They don't care where I went to school. They care whether I can get forty people out of a building that's coming down without losing one."

Eli said, from the chair, "That's why you know every evacuation route in Meridian."

"You noticed."

"I contain multitudes."

She ignored him. "First placement had a kitchen fire. Grease, then the curtain. The staff froze. The kids didn't. I got done, that day, with rooms where the person holding the authority is automatically the person everybody expects to know what to do." She looked at Kade. "Maybe Sen deserves it. Maybe the school deserves some. But don't confuse being angry with being free."

"You think I should stay."

"I think you should decide whether you want to, after you're done being angry. Not before."

That was worse advice than an argument, because it was correct and it did not tell him what to do.

He sat on the edge of the bed with the bag still on his shoulder, as if putting both feet on the floor without it might count as staying, and Eli said, "For the record, if you go, I'm keeping the desk," and Kade said, "That's my desk," and Eli said, "Possession is complicated under abandonment law," and Sera's mouth moved at one corner.

The bag slid to the floor. He did not unpack it.

Ten minutes later the door opened again, and it was Mara, who took in the duffel, and Kade, and Sera, in that order.

Sera said, "Provisional."

Mara nodded once, as if that were a rank.

"Do people file reports on me now?" Kade said.

"Only the dangerous ones." She held out her band with a message open on it, and he read it without taking it.

DIRECTOR SEN. AVAILABLE AT YOUR DISCRETION. NO TIME SET.

Yesterday the administrative floor had been Thursday. This morning the Director had a gap in a security session with his name on it.

"She sent that to you?"

"To you. You're not checking messages." Mara lowered her arm. "I brought it because you'd have read silence as management and an order as control. This is neither. It's a door with no clock on it."

"That's irritatingly fair."

She looked at the bag, the squared-off shirts in it, the rotation sheet he had left in the drawer with Thursday on it, and did not ask.

He picked the bag up.

"You said decide after," he told Sera. "I'm not after. I'm still angry. So I'm going for a walk."

Eli looked at the duffel. "With luggage."

"I want to know if it feels different when I actually reach the door."

Sera considered him, then stepped out of the frame.

"Good."

He stared at her. "You're letting me go."

"I was always letting you go."

That was irritating enough to prove her point, and he went past her with the bag.

---

Meridian's common room had Nessa and Anton in it arguing about orbital construction placements over a brochure, and Anton saw him through the open door.

"Mercer. Tell her orbital welding is not a personality."

"Tell him vacuum certification doesn't make him interesting."

Then they both saw the bag. Anton lowered the brochure.

"Switching rooms?"

"Possibly islands."

Nessa's eyes narrowed. "For how long?"

"Haven't decided."

The room did not go quiet. A card game went on under the window. Somebody at the long table asked for a power adapter. Nessa tapped the projection off and stood.

"You've got Thursday close."

"Eli mentioned."

"Because you traded me. I've got the placement interview at four."

"Ask Anton."

"Prototype shop," Anton said. "Final materials test. Can't move it."

"Somebody will cover it."

"Who?"

The same question twice in one morning. Kade looked at the duty board by the door, the week in its narrow grid, kitchen close and floor reset and first-year escort, his name three times, and KIM struck through under Thursday with MERCER written in over it in his own hand.

"If I withdraw, the House reassigns it."

"Have you withdrawn?"

"Not yet."

"Then the House thinks you're coming back."

No accusation in it. Just the practical problem he had made by existing on a schedule. He pulled the stylus from the slot. He could write OPEN and make his absence somebody else's Thursday. Instead he wrote, small, under his name: CONFIRM BY 18:00.

Nessa read it. "That's not coverage."

"It's a deadline."

"For you?"

"Apparently."

Anton turned the projection back on. "If he's here at six, you win the argument."

"Which one?"

"All of them. It's safer if he's back by five fifty-nine."

Nessa gave Anton a look that promised a vacuum-related accident. Kade put the stylus back. Neither of them had asked why he might go. Neither had offered to drop what they wanted so he could leave cleanly. They had only required his choice to touch the same world everyone else had to live in.

He crossed the courtyard under the bell. Priya was on the training floor teaching a first-year how to load acceleration without telegraphing the direction, and did not look up, and he was grateful for it. Then Central Bridge, with the number and the word PERSONS stenciled at the threshold. He had gone back to read that the first day. He had liked the place for it before he had known anything else about it.

The landing complex smelled the way it had in August. Salt, cut grass, fuel, and the flowering thing he still had no name for.

Enrollment Officer Cole was behind the transit desk with a manifest, because of course he was.

"Mr. Mercer."

"Officer Cole."

Cole's eyes went to the bag and came back. "Departure?"

"Maybe."

"The desk requires something more specific than maybe. It requires your band and a departure time." Cole had said that sentence to him once before, in a stairwell, on the first night. He said it now as if he had been keeping it in a drawer.

Kade set the bag down. "Next civilian flight to the mainland."

"Twenty-one minutes. Academy shuttle to San Diego. Commercial connections after that."

"Can I take it?"

Cole looked at his screen for the length of time it took to be honest. "You are not under travel restriction." He slid a blank transit slate across the desk. "Band."

Kade laid his wrist on it. The slate filled itself in. Name. Medical clearance, with Park's initials on it. Emergency contact. One line for destination beyond San Diego.

He typed UNDECIDED. The field went amber and sat there.

Cole waited.

He changed it to the name of his mother's city, and the field cleared.

"Bag on the plate."

He lifted the duffel onto the scale. Eight point four kilograms. Everything he had carried onto the island, plus two training sets and a cable, and the tag still through the handle.

"No hold luggage. Seat fourteen."

"You've assigned me a seat."

"You asked whether you could take the shuttle." The desk printed a narrow strip and Cole set it beside the band. ASTERION TO SAN DIEGO. GATE EAST TWO. BOARDING 11:42.

Kade picked it up. The paper was heavier than the bag.

"What about Zero Protocol?"

"Your mandatory evaluation period ended when your provisional enrollment became active. There is no hold on your record."

"So I can just leave."

"Whether you are on this island," Cole said, "is still the one line on the schedule that belongs to you. I told you that the first night. It has not changed." He looked at Kade for a moment longer. "You appear disappointed."

"I'm not."

"Good." Cole went back to his manifest.

Kade stood there with the strip in his hand. He had walked across the island expecting a wall, and there was no wall. No official. No director. No friend between him and the aircraft. Seat fourteen existed whether he sat in it or not. The choice had just become real.

---

He sat on a bench by the glass with the Pacific in front of him and the bag between his feet.

A family said goodbye to a second-year going home for the weekend. Two first-years argued about luggage weight. An upper-year slept with his head on his own duffel. Ordinary departures, none of them his.

He let himself have the mainland. A ticket. His mother at the arrivals rail with her coat still on from a shift. Darius sending something rude. His room, the box on the closet shelf, Northline in the morning if he wanted it. Safety by subtraction. Something in his chest loosened at the thought, and he did not pretend otherwise.

Then the other half came, the column Sera had made him look at.

A red ring of parts over Eli's desk. A manual with one boot on the table. Six weeks of Mara's handwriting squared on his desk. Julian making him hold a plan long enough for it to be trusted. Priya wanting a rematch and wanting him worth one. A rotation sheet with his own name written over Nessa's. The bell over the courtyard that he had heard ring once and had felt in his teeth.

He had always treated leaving as removing a problem. For the first time he could see what would be removed with it, and it had names.

He called his mother, because that was the promise. She answered on the second ring from her own kitchen, and looked at the glass behind him and the bench, and knew.

"You're at transit."

"You said you hear it first."

"Are you coming home?"

He looked through the window. The shuttle was on approach, low over the water. "I don't know."

She did not say anything to fill it. No demand, no relief he had to pay for.

"Do you want me to tell you to stay?"

"Don't tell me to stay."

"Do you want me to tell you to come home?"

He thought about it. "Don't tell me to leave either."

"Then what do you want?"

"I want everybody to stop lying."

"That's not a travel plan."

"I know."

She looked tired, and did not hide it. "Kade. If you come home, I'll be glad to see you. If you stay, I won't think you chose that place over me. And if you leave because you've decided it isn't worth what it costs, I won't call that running."

"What would you call running?"

"Deciding before you find out what you actually want."

Sera had said it in a doorway two hours ago. Now his mother. Apparently everyone in his life had formed a committee, and had not told him, which was typical.

The shuttle came down onto the pad. Boarding began. A short line formed at East Two, the sleeping upper-year in it without being fully awake, the second-year presenting her band. The reader chimed for each of them.

Kade stayed on the bench.

An attendant checked the slate in her hand. "Mercer? Seat fourteen?"

He held up the strip before he had decided why.

"We're ready for you."

The line had cleared. Beyond the gate a covered walk ran straight to the aircraft. No barrier. No test. Twenty steps.

He bent the strip across his thumb. "One minute."

She glanced at the clock. "You have one."

He looked at the gate. Then at his mother.

"I'm not coming home today."

"Then I'll see you when you do."

It hurt and helped at the same time, which was what she was for.

"Mom."

"Yes."

"I'm still angry at you."

"I know. I haven't asked you not to be."

"And at her."

"I'd worry if you weren't." A pause. "You're going to her."

"After this. Not alone. Somebody as far as the door." He had not known he was going to say it until it was out. "You've heard it first. That's both."

"That's both," she agreed. "Love you."

"Love you too."

He ended the call. The attendant was still waiting, and he crossed to East Two with the bag on his shoulder, and for one second the strip hung over the reader.

He set it on the counter instead.

"I'm not boarding."

She cancelled the seat. The green square beside fourteen went gray.

"Safe day, Mr. Mercer."

No question. No attempt to make it mean anything for him. The gate closed without him, and he sat until the shuttle lifted off the pad and turned over the water, and only then picked up the duffel.

Cole watched him come back past the desk.

"Mr. Mercer."

He stopped.

"On your first night I told you to find the shelter, and then find it again with the first way closed." Cole did not look up from the manifest. "You found the pad today. Then you found it again."

Kade stared at him. Something near the corner of Cole's mouth moved and came under control.

"Progress," Cole said.

Kade laughed, once, despite everything, and it surprised them both.

---

He messaged Mara from the bridge. SPIRE. NOW. YOU DON'T COME IN.

She was on the bench outside the Director's reception when he got there, tablet dark in her lap, and she looked at the bag and the strip in the side pocket and said only, "I'll be here," and he was inside Sen's office twenty-seven minutes after the shuttle left with the duffel on the floor beside his chair.

Sen looked at the boarding strip sticking out of the pocket.

"You nearly left."

"I had a seat."

"Why didn't you?"

"That's not why I'm here."

She nodded. Fair.

He put the export on her wall. The movement order. Twenty-three days. A roster with 000 on it. PROJECT CONTINUITY. MEDICAL OVERSIGHT: A. SEN. CONSENT STATUS: REAUTHORIZED.

"You lied to me. In the doorway, when I asked if anyone had done it before."

"I did."

No qualifier. It hit harder than a denial would have, because there was nothing in it to push against.

"You knew my father."

"I knew him."

"You sat in a hospital waiting room for four hours to tell my mother to be quiet."

Sen's face tightened. "I told her the official story was the safest story I knew how to give you."

"That's a very careful sentence."

"Deliberately."

"Was he Zero?"

"He was."

He had known since the card. It still went through him.

"What happened to him?"

She looked at the wall for a long moment. "I can't tell you yet."

"Of course."

"I know how that sounds."

"No. I don't think you do."

Sen held his eyes. "You're right."

That stopped him where the rest had not.

"I made choices about your family," she said, "under conditions you did not create and without your consent. Some protected you. Some harmed you. Both are true."

"Then why would I trust anything you say now?"

"You shouldn't. Not automatically."

He took the boarding strip out of the pocket and flattened it on her desk, because his hands wanted something to do that was not shaking.

"Who decides when *yet* is over?"

"For information held under academy authority, I do."

"So the person who lied decides when I've earned the truth."

"That's the structure. It isn't a defense of it."

"Change the structure."

Her fingers were still beside her tablet. "Some records aren't under academy authority."

"I asked about the ones that are."

The ventilation lifted a corner of the strip. He put one finger on it.

Sen opened a form. "I can release your complete academy file. Internal routing, access history, every version of the Zero Protocol order."

"That's the file about me since I got here."

"Yes."

"I want the file about me before I got here. Any reference. My name, my mother, Northline, hospital records, aptitude projections. If something's withheld, I want the record identifier, who withheld it, and the stated reason. No invisible gaps. Every gap has an owner."

Her eyes went once to the edge of her display, where a restricted-records indicator sat, and came back. Not a refusal. The arithmetic before one.

"I can't guarantee an index for systems I don't control."

"Then guarantee it for yours."

She read the demand off the wall as if it had come from somebody with authority over her, and perhaps that was the point. "For Asterion-controlled systems, I'll provide the records and a withholding index."

"When?"

"The academy file now. The historical search already exists."

He stared at her. "You searched for records about me."

"After your structural event. I was trying to find out what the Directorate already knew."

The word sat in the room. He had heard it yesterday on the forum steps from a friend with a sandwich, in the same easy voice Taren used for everything, and Sen had just said it as if it were a thing a person could be trying to stay ahead of.

"And you didn't think the person being searched deserved the list."

"I thought showing it to you would expose sources we were still protecting."

"Do you still think that?"

Sen looked at him for several seconds. Then she separated two documents from the wall. One carried the academy seal. The other was mostly identifiers and authority codes and blocks marked WITHHELD, and it ran off the bottom of the display.

"The sources stay redacted. The identifiers and the withholding authorities do not."

She pushed both to his band, and he opened them before the light had faded. The academy file began with Cole's first report from Northline. The index began years before that. No answers. But the gaps had shapes now, and every shape had a name beside it that claimed the right to keep it empty.

"Don't send Mara with messages again."

"I sent it to you."

"She brought it because you knew I'd ignore it."

"She brought it because she chose to."

He studied her face. "Did you ask anyone to stop me leaving?"

"I did not."

"Nobody. Cole. The House."

"Not at my direction."

"That's careful."

"It's exact."

He could work with exact. He could also verify it, and she knew that.

"Then why stay?" she said.

"That's mine."

"I know."

She looked at the duffel. "Do you?"

The anger came up and then went down far enough for him to hear the question under it. He thought of the green square going gray.

"I chose to stay." He stood. "For Meridian. For the classes. For answers."

"The answers may cost more than you expect."

"Everything here does."

He picked up the bag. At the door she said his name, and he turned.

"I'm sorry."

He waited for something useful to arrive with it. Nothing did.

"Okay," he said. Not forgiveness. Not acceptance. An acknowledgment that the words had been said in the room, and could be held to.

Mara stood when he came out. She looked at his face, and did not ask, and walked beside him to the bridge, and he told her on the way, all of it, because she had built the first row.

---

The Commons was mid-dinner when he came in with the duffel. The noise dipped. Not everybody knew why he had it. Enough did.

Sera looked up from her plate. Eli stopped talking mid-sentence, which never happened. Taren's face went unreadable for half a second and then came back, easy, and Kade saw the half second and put it where he had put the sandwich on the steps. Later.

He went up to eight fourteen. Eli followed three steps behind without asking.

He put the bag on the bed and took the first shirt out. Then the second.

Eli leaned on his own desk. "No speech?"

"You survived the first one."

"Good."

Shirts in the drawer. The cable. The pencil back in the desk. Mara's notes still squared in the middle of the desk where he had left them for her. He sent her a message.

DO YOU NEED THESE BACK?

Her answer came before he had put the band down.

THAT IS NOT THE QUESTION.

He looked at it a while. Tried again.

MAY I KEEP BORROWING THEM?

UNTIL OUR NEXT REVIEW. YOU ARE STILL WRONG ON PAGE FOURTEEN.

He put the notes beside the projection. Nothing in the exchange required either of them to pretend the day had not happened. It only said what came next.

Seventeen forty-six. He opened the House rotation on his band. Thursday, kitchen close, waiting for confirmation. Fourteen minutes inside the deadline he had written on a board for himself.

He pressed CONFIRM.

Nessa answered before he could close it. GOOD. ANTON IS STILL WRONG ABOUT VACUUM CERTIFICATION.

I DID NOT AGREE TO ARBITRATE.

TOO LATE. YOU CAME BACK.

One Thursday did not bind him to this island for good. It bound him to Thursday. For now, that was an honest size for a promise.

The cancelled strip was still in his pocket. He set it by the pencil and turned it over, and the back was blank, printed for nothing. He wrote three lines on it.

ACADEMY FILE. RECEIVED.

HISTORICAL INDEX. RECEIVED.

EVERY WITHHOLDING HAS AN OWNER.

Eli read over his shoulder. "That's an aggressive use of transit paperwork."

"She likes structures."

"You made her a list on a boarding pass."

"She knows where I got it."

He propped the strip against the base of the desk projection with the departure time facing the wall and the three lines facing him.

Then the drawer, and under the paper map, face down where it had been since the first night, the square of paper in the print she used for charts. *You are allowed to leave. You are also allowed to stay.* He read the second line, which was still the hard one. He put it under the pencil, face up.

He had not stayed because the school had earned it. He had stayed long enough to make demands of it.

Not because he trusted Sen. Because Mara had brought him evidence instead of hiding it. Because Eli had a future over his desk that did not need him in it. Because Sera had recognized the ugliest version of his oldest habit and refused to let him call it strength. Because Meridian noticed whether he showed up, and had said so, with a stylus, on a board.

Because leaving was a choice now, and not a reflex.

He folded the empty duffel in thirds, tag and all, the way it had lived on the shelf where he could see it every morning, and slid it under the bed instead.

Not thrown out. Not forgotten. Just empty, and not where he had to look at it.

Downstairs nobody applauded, and he was grateful. Sera slid a plate across without a word. Eli said, "I kept your desk." Mara, from the far side of the table, said, "Did you talk to her?"

"She gave me the academy file. And an index of what's withheld, with names on it."

"And?"

"She admitted the lie. The answers are still shut. But every gap has an owner now."

Taren looked down at his food.

Kade saw it, and noted it, and put it with the rest. Later.

For now he sat. He stayed. He ate.

For Kade Mercer, that counted as a commitment.

# Chapter 27 — The Empty Bed

The housing coordinator had a checklist, and the checklist needed a roommate. So at ten past seven a second-year climbed to the eighth-floor landing and told Kade he was wanted in his own room.

He had been on the top stair all night. A woman with a tablet was standing at the door of 814, and she might go in without him if he let her.

She had not gone in. Her toes were a hand's width short of the sill.

"I'm sorry to do this today," she said. "There are four things I have to ask before anybody can put a hold on the room. Dependent devices, hazardous materials, living organisms and perishables. You can answer from the hall, if that's easier."

"It isn't," said Kade, and went past her.

Eli's half was too tidy, and it took him a moment to work out why that was wrong. Eli tidied when he was nervous. He had been nervous about Friday all week, so twenty-three cables hung coiled on their hooks, each under a label only Eli could read. The bench mat lay square to the desk. In the exact middle of it sat the drone rotor he had promised to fix three weeks ago, still unfixed, like a patient who had been given an appointment.

"Dependent devices," the coordinator said from the doorway.

"Nothing that needs him. The workstation's asleep."

It was not quite asleep. The fellowship application still hung over the desk in pale letters, exactly where he had left it. LOW-COST FIELD STABILIZATION FOR CIVILIAN RESPONSE. Eli had written to himself in the margin. *Version seven still too expensive. Stop pretending rare materials are free just because Asterion buys them.*

"Hazardous materials?"

"There's a discharged battery stack under the desk. Drone cells in the wall locker, in the fire bag. One soldering iron." The iron stood on the windowsill beside the tub of protein powder. At lunch yesterday Kade had been willed one of those and forbidden the other. "Does protein powder count?"

"Not under housing policy."

"It should."

It was out before he could stop it, and for about one second the room sounded the way it had on Tuesday. The coordinator looked down at her tablet.

"Living organisms?"

"Only me."

"Perishables."

He pulled out the cold drawer between the desks. It held half a box of noodles, two nutrient drinks, and a piece of fruit that had crossed over from food into research. Every item wore a strip of tape. MINE. ALSO MINE. The noodles said, KADE, THIS DOES NOT BECOME YOURS WHEN I AM IN CLASS.

He shut the drawer with his knee. "Some. I don't want them cleared."

She marked it. "A hold doesn't oblige you to keep spoiled noodles. Most people need that said twice." She lowered the tablet. "Housing can't place the hold, Mr. Mercer. Your House does. Somebody from Meridian files it and somebody who lives in the room accepts it. Until that happens, my system wants me to enter this bed as something. I'm not going to enter it as anything before noon."

"What happens at noon?"

"I come back up and hope there's a card on the door." She hesitated. "You're also entitled to a temporary single while this is open. It's one form, and it's in your housing folder now. People take it. Nobody thinks less of them."

She went. Kade stood between the two beds. Eli's blanket had a dent in it the shape of a tablet. On the desk stood the mug, WORLD'S MOST ADEQUATE ENGINEER. Kade had bought it as a joke after the House Trial, and Eli had drunk from it every day since. He hated that buying a mug now felt like one of the important things he had done that year.

He knew how to make himself unavailable. It came back as easily as a language he had grown up speaking. He could shut the door and answer in the smallest words he owned, and need less from everyone. It had always worked before. It would not work in here, because Eli had spread too far into the room.

He went downstairs to find out how a person got a card for a door.

---

The common room was already arguing about it.

A sheet of paper a meter wide had been pinned to the corkboard, ruled into hours from now until the memorial. Julian Cross stood in front of it with a marker. Priya stood in front of Julian.

"You are not Meridian," she said. "I say this with respect for your rank and none whatever for your handwriting. This is our room and our floor. Give me the pen."

"I've only done the meals."

"You've done the meals, the faculty calls and the housing contact, and you've put yourself down for four of them. Pen."

Julian looked at the marker as though it had been found on him. He gave it up.

Kade read the sheet over her shoulder. One row was for meals, one for faculty, one for housing, and one for the door during visiting hours. Near the bottom was a row headed EIGHTH FLOOR, IF WANTED. Six people had signed against it. A seventh name had been written there in neat small capitals and struck through with one stroke of Priya's thicker pen. The name was HOLT.

"Mercer." Anton Bell had his list of names from last night in one hand and a pair of kitchen scissors in the other. "Housing's been up. Come on."

The bell cabinet was a tall cupboard under the stairs, where the rope for the old bronze bell came down through the ceiling. On the shelf beneath the rope lay a roll of blue cloth. The outer turns had faded almost gray.

"Seven-day hold," said Anton. He measured a length against his forearm and cut it. "If somebody in this House goes missing, or dies, or gets evacuated or arrested or transferred overnight, their room isn't touched for seven days."

"That's a specific list."

"We're a specific House."

"Why seven?"

"I asked that in my first year. Nobody knew. It's long enough that housing can't turn a person into a vacancy before their friends have found out what happened to them. That's how it was told to me." He took a card from a box beside the roll. It had been printed a long time ago, with three lines on it. HOLD PLACED BY. ACCEPTED BY. ENDS: ON RETURN, OR DAY SEVEN. Anton wrote his name on the first line and pushed the card along the shelf.

Kade looked at the third line. *On return* was printed first.

"It's always been printed like that," Anton said, watching him. "Don't read anything into it."

"I'm not."

He was, and he made himself stop. Hope had an edge, and he was not going to pick it up by the wrong end.

"What am I accepting?"

"Seven days of being the person housing talks to. You stop people turning the room into a shrine, which he would hate. And you leave it at least twice a day."

"That last part's written in by hand."

"It's the oldest handwriting on the card."

Kade signed. Anton laid the strip of cloth across his palm. The edges had been cut a minute ago, but the weave was soft as an old shirt.

"Everyone gets the same roll," Anton said. "I thought that was cold, my first year. Then I worked out how many people had cut from it before me." He shut the cabinet. "Bedpost, at the foot. There isn't a special knot."

Julian was waiting at the bottom of the stairs with his hands in his pockets, relieved of his pen.

"She was right," he said. "My father says leadership is only what people call control when the result is good. Every list I write makes me feel better, and it's the wrong sort of better." He looked at the cloth. "I can organize meals. I can't fix this. I seem to need to say so out loud."

"Welcome to the group," said Kade.

Julian gave him a tired smile. "My House does three days."

"Meridian does seven."

"Seven's better."

Every House on the island, it seemed, kept a custom for people who did not come back on schedule. Kade did not like what that said about Asterion.

Upstairs he put the card on the door. Then he knelt at the foot of Eli's bed and tied the cloth round the post. His fingers had not been steady since the gray door, and it took three attempts. The knot sat crooked. He left it crooked.

A gray rubber wedge lay on the floor behind the door, printed on Eli's machine in October and labeled DOOR. Kade stood with his hand on the door for some time. He knew what he did with closed doors. He kicked the wedge under it and left it open a hand's width.

---

Sera was sitting on his floor when he came back from his second departure of the day, which had been to the end of the corridor. It was her hour on the sheet. She had a paper notebook on her knee, fat with use and held shut with a rubber band. She was not opening it.

"It was on a bench in Lab Five," she said. "Responders brought it up with everything they cleared. It isn't mine to read. But he owed me a page by Friday, and I'd like to know if he wrote it."

Kade took the notebook. There were colored tabs down the side, and one said PROJECT. He opened it there and kept his thumb clear of the others.

The first page had a heading printed large enough for anybody. WHY CIVILIAN STABILIZATION MATTERS. Under it, in ordinary handwriting: *Because people should not need to be rich, military, famous or lucky to survive the weird future we are building.*

He read that three times. He had known what Eli wanted. He had not known how much. The pale application over the desk stopped looking like paperwork. It was a future, and it had not happened yet.

Farther on a line had been added in a different pen. *If version eight works, ask Sera to break it. She will enjoy that too much.*

The last written page was smeared, because it had been copied off the back of a hand. He turned the notebook round and held it out so that she could see that page and no other.

*GO / NO-GO. One trip equals what? Set at placement. Must be wrong in the right direction. Send to S before Friday. She will mark where it kills people. (Enthusiasm.)*

Sera went on looking at it after she had finished reading.

"I walked into the assessment office at eight," she said. "Like the commander told me to. They've offered me the accelerated track for the Response Corps."

"Sera. That's enormous."

"Probably."

"You are terrible at being happy."

"He's gone, Kade."

The sentence filled the room. Kade looked at the dent in the blanket.

"Not located," he said.

She shut her eyes. "Don't."

"I know what it means. I'm keeping it accurate, that's all."

She breathed out slowly. "Part of me was glad. In that hall, with the glass still white and him behind it, a stranger told me I was good at this, and part of me was glad. It hasn't stopped being glad since. What sort of person is that?"

Kade thought about the week after the House Trial. He had liked the applause, and been ashamed of liking it, at the same meals.

"I don't think one cancels the other," he said. "I think you get both, and you carry them around together. Like a stretcher."

Her face eased by a degree. "Look at you, growing."

"Please stop."

She very nearly smiled. At the door she stooped and put two fingers on the crooked knot, quickly, as someone might touch a friend's shoulder in passing. She did not retie it.

When she had gone he read the enthusiasm page again and laughed once, out loud, in the empty room. Then he put both hands over his face. The laugh hurt more than crying would have. He cried as well, and did not try to stop it.

---

Asterion went back to class the next morning. The east engineering wing was sealed, but breakfast was at seven and Vector Analysis at eight, and the ranking boards would update at noon as they did every day. Kade knew that ten thousand students could not hold still for one missing boy most of them had never met. It felt like a betrayal anyway.

Taren was on the landing, standing well out from the wall with his hands in plain view.

"You'll have seen the sheet," he said. "Priya was right to cross me off. I wanted you to hear that I asked."

Kade had a dozen answers, and none of them were true yet. "I don't know what to do with you. I'm not going to work it out this week."

"I know," said Taren, and gave him the stairs.

Professor Amadi did not mention Eli. She looked at the empty seat beside Kade's and said, "Navarro's assignments are suspended," and began the lecture. Halfway through the hour she set a three-person coupling problem, and Kade turned to his right with a sentence already started, and there was a chair there.

Owen Park had looked at the chair as well. He moved his tablet over.

"We're two. Come and be a third."

The refusal was ready before Kade had chosen it. *Don't fill the place. Don't make it any less empty.* He looked at the thought properly, and it was stupid. Forty minutes beside Owen Park would not rub anybody out.

He carried his chair across.

The rig had three linked field plates in a row and a load that travelled across them. One operator held the center plate. One moved power between the two outer plates. The third watched the instruments and had the abort. Kade took the center without asking, because that was where he always stood. Owen's partner, a quiet girl from Sol, took the outer plates. Owen took the instruments.

The load started across. Kade steadied the center and waited for the low stream of numbers from his right, the running talk that told him where the load would be before it got there. There was silence on his right. The load reached the first seam and the left plate sagged. Kade threw power after it, late, and all three plates went red.

DISCONTINUITY: 0.31 SECONDS.

Amadi reset the rig from her desk and said nothing.

"You stopped," said Owen. "Just before the seam. You were waiting for something."

"For the call."

"There's no call in this exercise. Instruments watch and abort. It's on the sheet." Owen was not unkind about it. "Whoever used to do that for you wasn't doing it because the sheet said so."

It had been Eli. He had never believed the edge of his own job was a reason to let a system fail quietly.

They ran it again, and it was worse. Kade tried to hold the center and track the load himself. It was too much at once, and the wire behind his right eye pulled tight. The girl saved the second seam with a rough overcorrection that kept the system alive and wrecked their efficiency. The run closed amber.

"I can't talk and read at once," Owen said. "I've tried. I go quiet or I go wrong. So don't build this round a voice you haven't got."

Kade looked at the three stations. He had been trying to rebuild Tuesday's team out of different people. He made himself look at the people he had.

"What are you good at?"

"Holding a value. Give me a number and I'll keep a plate on it until the building falls down."

"And you?" he asked the girl.

"Timing. I row."

"Then I'm in the wrong seat." Kade stood up. "Owen takes the center and holds it by the numbers. He won't drift and he doesn't need to be told anything. You take instruments, and you don't give me figures. Count me in like a stroke. I'll take the outer plates, because the one thing I can do is feel where a transfer wants to go. I'll move on your count."

"That leaves nobody calling the seam," said Owen.

"Nobody here can. So we build it not to need one."

On the third run the girl counted, low and even, and Kade moved power on her beat, and Owen's center plate sat at its value like something bolted down. The load went over the first seam and nothing flashed. At the second, her count stretched by half a beat, and Kade stretched with it without being told.

The load ran off the end. It was not an elegant score, but the run had been stable from one end to the other.

Owen saved the configuration. "Navarro would hate this. There's no model in it anywhere."

"He'd have built a model of it by dinner, and then said he'd agreed with you from the start."

The girl laughed, briefly. People could be nothing like Eli and still help him build something that worked.

His tablet chimed as he packed up. Amadi had sent a revised workload with two extensions and one office hour, no penalty. Beneath it she had written a single line. *Grief does not excuse missing assignments. It entirely justifies asking before you miss them. You would have heard pity in anything softer.*

He looked up. She was already marking. He disliked how right she was.

---

Two people were waiting outside 814 that afternoon, and they had not gone in past the card.

One was a systems officer in a gray incident jacket. The other was a professor from Systems Lab, who had a dented steel tool case at her feet with orange tape wound round the handle.

"I take things away," the officer said, "and she leaves things. The academy likes a faculty witness for both. Eli's tablet went into the spine with him, so I'm here for the desk dock and the workstation core."

He unplugged them carefully, and copied down each cable label without trying to read it. The pale application over the desk went out. Kade had not known that it would.

"What are you looking for?"

"Network activity before the incident."

"He used a dead maintenance channel that night. Null seven. There's an open ticket for it from March."

The officer wrote that down. "Anything else?"

"He found the lab's status traffic being mirrored off campus."

"We have that. Some status packets were duplicated to an unauthorized endpoint. That much I can say."

"Was it the Directorate?"

"We haven't attributed it."

"That isn't what I asked."

"I know. It's the answer I've got." He coiled the last cable. Then he added one thing more. "There's also an unresolved device identifier from the lower lab."

Hope was moving before Kade could stop it. "What does that mean?"

"A device stopped reporting before the main coolant failure. It never came back on the recovery network."

"His tablet?"

"Different hardware. It doesn't match anything in the registry, so I can't tell you what kind." His face stayed professional, and was not unkind. "Don't turn an incomplete log into a story, Mercer."

"What if the story's already in there?"

"Then the evidence for it will still be in there tomorrow."

One identifier could be a sensor, or a maintenance controller, or a piece of somebody's broken drone. Kade would not let it be Eli. He had worked out at a gray door what that kind of wanting was for. Hope without evidence was only another way of getting out of the room.

The officer left with his sealed bags. The professor lifted the case onto the bare patch of desk and pressed its broken latch twice with her thumb, as though it might have reconsidered.

"He mended this latch on everyone's case but his own," she said. She held out a slate. TEMPORARY CUSTODY OF STUDENT PROJECT MATERIALS. "If nobody signs, academy storage collects it at five. It doesn't assign ownership. He listed you as his emergency room contact."

It was not an emergency contact, which would be his mother. It was an emergency *room* contact, because in Eli's view a room could have an emergency too, and somebody ought to be on file for it. Kade nearly smiled, and it hurt.

He read the form to the end. "This doesn't say what's in the case. I'd be signing for whatever anyone says was in it."

She looked at him more carefully. "Yes. He filed three complaints about exactly that. I countersigned the second." She opened the lid. Every tool lay in a fitted slot, except for one narrow gap, and a panel inside the lid woke to show forty-two entries with serial numbers. "So he wrote his own."

Below the last entry Eli had left a note. *If this case is coming back because I am dead, missing, expelled, or finally arrested for unauthorized fabrication: the ten-millimeter torque key is not lost. It is in Lab Five, being useful.*

Kade knew where. It was in an orange tool roll, and he had handed Eli that roll himself.

"I'll sign his list. Not yours."

"I'll attach it as the controlling inventory." She turned the panel toward him. His hand went unsteady halfway through his surname, and she took a close interest in the latch until he was done. Then she shut the lid, and turned the orange handle outward where a person could grab it.

"His fellowship review is suspended," she said at the door. "Suspended is not rejected."

"Does the difference help?"

"Nothing helps today. But it's his, so I'm keeping it accurate."

That helped more than comfort would have.

---

At dinner Meridian left Eli's chair empty. Nobody had arranged it. His plate stayed in the cupboard and not at his place, because the House apparently had a rule about that too. Kade sat down next to the gap, and Mara sat on his other side.

His tablet had been buzzing against the table since the soup. The academy bulletin wanted a comment, and so did two mainland news services. A fourth offered payment for an exclusive account of the Lab Six tragedy. He turned the tablet face down. It buzzed again.

"I could sort that," Mara said. "Into people who need an answer, people who believe they're owed one, and people who ought to be introduced to Sera."

He slid it across. She did not wake it.

"Give me the limits first."

*All of it, I don't care,* was in his mouth, because deciding cost effort and he had very little left. But that was exhaustion answering for him, and she would hear it.

"Today's messages. Not my mother's thread and not Darius's. Don't answer anybody. Just sort."

She worked without narrating it. The media went behind a filter and the buzzing stopped. She paused once. "The training office wants to know about your evening slot."

"Cancel it."

"Tonight's, or the term's?"

The question irritated him, and that told him it mattered. "Tonight's."

She cancelled one session. Near the end she tilted the screen so that he could see a preview without opening the thread behind it. It was from Darius. *No answer needed. Your mom ate. I checked.*

Kade swallowed. "Which pile is that?"

"You tell me."

"People who need an answer."

She flagged it, locked the tablet, and put it on the table between them. She did not keep hold of it.

Across from them Anton was asking the table, quietly, whether the House wanted to bring something of Eli's to stand beside the name tomorrow. "Nothing gets decided tonight," he said, and looked at Kade. "Nothing comes out of that room unless you say so."

"Not the mug," said Kade.

"Then not the mug."

For ten minutes the long table was quiet. Then Priya began complaining about a conditioning instructor, and Anton told her she was wrong, in detail. Nessa told a story about a second-year who had field-locked himself inside a storage room and tried to pass it off as meditation. Somebody laughed.

Anger went through Kade like a current. Then he remembered Eli laughing in every room where anything had ever gone wrong, and the anger came apart in his hands. He laughed at the second-year. The chair beside him stayed empty while he did it. Under the table Mara's shoulder rested against his, and she did not move it away.

---

The old instinct came back after midnight, and this time it brought a form.

*If caring can do this, care less. If belonging makes a room hurt this much, get a smaller room.*

The smaller room was in his housing folder. TEMPORARY SINGLE OCCUPANCY REQUEST. It had five fields, and three of them had filled themselves in. He sat on the floor with his back against his own bed and completed the fourth. Under the bed frame, by his hip, lay the duffel he had folded away the night he came back from the shuttle gate. At some point in the last hour he had pulled it out and unfolded it. He did not remember deciding to.

Mara had gone at eleven, because he had asked her to sleep. Sera had response training at six. Darius had sent three terrible jokes and one line that said only, *I'm here.*

None of it made the hole any smaller. It did not make the world any smaller either, and the world was what he was trying to shrink. The fifth field wanted a reason. He typed, *Can't*, and stopped. If he took the single, somebody else would have to answer for the card on the door. Anton would do it without a word of complaint. That was the trouble with these people.

At six minutes past midnight he left the form open and called Darius.

The picture shook, showed a ceiling tile, and settled on half a face behind the Northline security desk.

"Hey," said Darius, and left it there. Behind him the Room Four vending machine hummed, as it had through every disaster Kade ever carried into that building.

"Why are you at school at midnight?"

"Night guard called out. Also your mom was sorting a kitchen drawer by frequency of use, and I left before I got a category."

"She does that when she's scared."

"I know. She's scared, and she's trying to be useful. She ate. She called the academy twice and got what you got." Nothing at Northline had become less ugly while Kade was away, and it was a relief. "So tell me something about him I can't stand."

"What?"

"All I've got is the good parts. Engineer, funny, sent me six pages correcting the battery math in a joke."

"Your math was wrong."

"See, that's a start. Give me another. I can't miss a guy who's all good parts. That's a poster."

"He labels his food like it's disputed territory."

"With you in the room, that's just sense."

"He talks in his sleep before deadlines. Once he recited a load table for eight minutes. I recorded it, because I thought he was awake and being strange. He found it and deleted it." Kade looked up at the dark place over the desk where the letters had been. "He wants to build rescue gear ordinary people can afford. I told you that, and I thought it was a hobby. It isn't. He knows what county services are allowed to spend. He's designing for one nurse on a night shift, and he's never met her. He'll explain her purchasing limit to you whether you ask or not."

"Sounds exhausting."

"Extremely."

"Good," said Darius. "Now I've got an actual person to miss."

That went straight through him. Kade pressed the heel of his hand against his mouth. Darius stayed on the screen. He did not promise that Eli would be found, and he did not warn that he might not be.

When Kade could talk again, he said, "I don't know what to tell my mom."

"Tell her you're alive."

"She knows I'm alive."

"Parents are inefficient. They require updates."

Kade opened her thread. *Still here. Ate dinner. Memorial tomorrow. I'll call you after.* He added *I love you* and sent it before he could tidy it. Behind the thread, the housing form was still waiting for its reason.

He deleted *Can't*. Then he deleted the form, and pushed the duffel back under the bed with his foot. It had not been folded, but it was out of sight.

"What was that?" said Darius.

"Nothing. A room I'm not taking."

Darius nodded as if that were a complete sentence. "Want to hear why the vending machine no longer accepts credit?"

"That sounds like your fault."

"It's a story with multiple valid interpretations."

It was a terrible story. The call ran twenty-seven minutes, and Kade did not once look to see when it would be over.

At twenty to one the door moved on its wedge and Sera came in with a bowl. She set it on his desk. She looked at the corner of canvas under the bed and did not remark on it. Then she sat down on the floor beside him with her boots out in front of her.

"I nearly asked for a single," he said.

"I know. The coordinator told Anton it was in your folder. He's had someone walking past this door every hour."

He looked across at the crooked knot. "What if this is the part I was right about? Needing people gives them a way to hurt you."

Sera was quiet for some time.

"Well, yes," she said.

He turned his head.

"What did you think the other choice was? Some arrangement where you get to have people and nothing's at risk? I looked for that for years, in worse buildings than this one. It isn't anywhere." She got up. "Memorial's at ten."

"I can't do it."

"You can. You don't want to. Those aren't the same, and you know it."

She was at the door when he said her name.

"The investigators have a device that dropped off the network before the coolant went. It isn't in any registry. Do you think it means anything?"

Her face went very still. "I think they have incomplete data."

"That was careful."

"It was meant to be. Don't use hope to get out of grieving, Kade. If something's there, it'll still be there after you've done this properly."

She went out, and left the door on its wedge.

He opened his messages. The unsent line was still sitting there. *Spine C. They came in by spine C, like you said.* He did not send it and he did not delete it. He would not let it become a prayer, and he would not let it become an ending.

He stayed awake until the window went gray. He did not tell himself that none of it mattered. He did not make a miracle out of a missing device number, or take the empty bed as proof that caring had been the mistake. The room was too large. He left it that way, with the card on the door and the blue strip crooked on its post.

When it was light he ate the cold bowl, because somebody had carried it up eight floors. He put on his one good shirt.

Downstairs, somebody was setting out chairs. He checked that the wedge was under the door, and went down to help.

# Chapter 2 — Zero Protocol

By the time the parking-lot lights came on, Kade Mercer had written his confession twice.

The first version filled the back of a district worksheet and read, when he went over it, like a boy explaining why the window was already broken when he got there. He crossed it out. The second version was four lines. *I was told to identify the boundary of the active system. I drew it through the steel ball. The interface accepted it. I drew it around nothing, and everything went off.* He signed it, because the rooms he was expecting liked a signature, folded it once, and put it under his hand on the desk.

They had moved him out of Multipurpose Two at one o'clock, when two district technicians arrived with a roll of orange tape and sealed the door the way custodians sealed a bathroom with a burst pipe. Station Seven was still lit when the door closed on it. He had checked.

Room Four was where the district put him, because Room Four was where he went. The heater shuddered. The clock above the door had stopped at eleven-oh-six, and nobody had reset it. On the wall display the two boxes were still up, the small one around the number and the big one around the list, because Ms. Alvarez had not cleared it and nobody else knew her password.

She was at her desk with a stack of district paperwork she was not reading. Darius sat two rows back with the last of the pretzels, present on the theory that his chair was enrolled even if he was not. Vice Principal Russell passed the door at intervals with his phone against his chest, the way a man carries a baby he is afraid to wake. *Remain on campus pending authorized review,* he had read off the screen at twelve fifty-eight, and when Kade asked what was being reviewed, him or the country, Russell had not laughed and had not answered.

Kade knew this room. Not Room Four, the other one, the one that always came next: somebody with a folder, a sentence that began with *we understand you*, and a new word for his file on the way out. The only unfamiliar quantity was the size of the thing he had broken. His previous record was a sprinkler head.

His phone had settled into a steady hum against his hip. The messages that mattered had come from Elena. *The charge desk just got a call from your school. Do not sign anything. Do not explain anything. I am coming.* He had written back that it was a test thing and it was fine, and she had answered in under a second. *You do not get to use fine today.*

She got there at two forty.

He heard her before he saw her, the fast flat slap of clinic shoes on hallway tile, and then she was in the door in navy scrubs and a gray coat, badge on backward, hair coming out of its knot. She stopped on the threshold and looked at him the way she looked at everything that came through the double doors at St. Vincent. Head. Hands. Front of the shirt. Color of the face. Then she crossed the room and took his chin in cold fingers and turned it toward the window.

"Nobody hit me," he said.

"I can see that."

"You checked anyway."

"I check everyone." She let go. "Get your bag."

Russell had come to the door. "Mrs. Mercer, I'm sorry, we've been asked to keep him on campus."

"Show me."

He showed her. She read it on his phone twice, holding the screen the way she would hold a chart she did not trust.

"This says *requested to remain*," she said. "It does not say he can be held."

"It came from a federal office."

"So do tax forms." She handed the phone back. Then she looked at the window, at the two dark vehicles that had been parked in the maintenance spaces since one-thirty with nobody in them, and Kade saw her do the arithmetic he had already done. Whoever had sent the cars had sent them to wait.

"Kade," she said, "if we go home, do you think they stop coming?"

"No."

"Then they come to the house." She set her bag on Darius's desk without asking him. "I would rather meet them here, where there are windows and a vice principal. Sit down."

"I was sitting."

"Sit down differently."

---

She checked him properly once Russell had gone. Penlight from her coat pocket, left eye, right eye. Hands out, fingers spread. Follow the light. Squeeze. She had done this to him at nine after a bicycle and at thirteen after a fight, and both times she had waited for the jokes to run out and then finished.

"Headache."

"Behind the eyes. It's going down."

"Since when."

"Since the seventh section." He watched her write that on the back of her hand, where she wrote things at work when there was no paper. "It's not bad."

"You don't have a scale. You have nothing, and you have lying on the bathroom floor." She clicked the penlight off. "Vision. Numbness, face or hands."

"Fine. No."

"Did you lose time?"

He had been ready for the others. "What?"

"The proctor told Russell the room emptied around you. Nine kids pushed their chairs back and you didn't turn your head. That isn't concentration. Concentration hears chairs." She sat in the desk in front of him, backward, arms across the top. "Did you lose time."

He thought about the timer in the corner reading eighty-seven minutes when he had been certain of more. About the proctor standing where he had not been standing a moment before.

"I don't know," he said. "That's a real answer. I'm not being cute."

"I know when you're being cute. Tell me the rest."

So he told her. Not the sections that had been school. The one that had not. The sphere on its cable and the chime that said *incomplete* until he stopped drawing around objects and started drawing through them. The selection handle coming free. The word, white on black, there and gone. The gray field with nothing in it, and the line he had put around the nothing because it was the only thing left to do.

"And then it gave me a score," he said. "Three zeros. Not one. Three, centered, like they were proud of it. And then the lights went, and every screen in the country said *terminated*, and mine didn't. Mine kept the zeros up. Like it hadn't heard."

Elena did not say anything.

He watched everyone's face, so he saw it: nothing a stranger would have caught. Her right hand had been turning her badge lanyard between two fingers the way it did when she was listening. On the word *zeros* the fingers stopped, and then they started again, a beat late.

"Mom."

"I heard you."

"You did something with your hand."

"I did a great many things with my hand today, most of them to people who were bleeding." She stood up. "Have you eaten?"

"Darius gave me pretzels."

"Darius gave you salt." She looked at Ms. Alvarez, who had not once pretended not to listen. "Is there anything in this building with protein in it?"

"There's the machine," Alvarez said. "And there's my desk drawer, which is better than the machine."

Elena went to the desk drawer. Kade sat with his four-line confession under his palm and filed a hand that had stopped on a word in the place where he kept things he was going to ask about later.

---

The afternoon went the way afternoons in institutions went, which was slowly and then not at all. Russell came in at four to say district counsel had told him to say nothing, and said it at length. The light in the windows went orange and then went.

At six fifty-one two more vehicles pulled into the lot behind the first two. Doors opened. Kade counted four people and then two, because two of them stayed by the cars.

The two who came in were not what he had built in his head. The man was fifty or so, thick through the shoulders, gray at the temples, and tired in the specific way of somebody who had been awake since a phone rang. Dark suit, no tie, a narrow hard case in one hand. Behind him, a woman in charcoal with nothing sewn on it anywhere. No name, no bar, no patch. She let her eyes go around the room once, corners first, the way his did. They finished on him and stayed.

"Vice Principal Russell." The man had a voice built for being understood over distance without being raised. "David Renn, International Resonance Authority. This is Lieutenant Commander Nia Vale, Federal Resonance Security. Which of you is Mrs. Mercer?"

Elena did not stand. "You've been in the parking lot for five hours."

"Two of my people have. I've been on an aircraft." He set the case on Alvarez's desk. "I'd like to speak with your son. You're welcome to stay. So is his teacher. Mr. Bell, I'm afraid, is not."

Darius looked at Kade. He had no joke ready, which was how Kade knew he was frightened, and it unsettled him more than the cars had.

"Go," Kade said. "Take the pretzels. Text me every insult you think of."

"I've got like forty."

"Space them out."

Darius took the bag and went. At the door he looked at the woman with no name on her uniform, and she looked back, and he decided against whatever he had been going to say.

Renn lowered himself into the desk in front of Kade's, which was built for a fourteen-year-old and complained about it. Vale did not sit. She had gone to the wall, where the display still showed the cargo tram and the list under it, and she was reading the list. FRICTION. TRACK GRADE. LOAD SECUREMENT. BELL.

"Whose is this?"

"His," Alvarez said. "I wrote it. He said it."

Vale said nothing. Something changed in the way she stood.

Kade slid the folded worksheet across the desk before Renn could start. He had always preferred to go first.

"That's what happened," he said. "I broke it. That's how."

Renn unfolded the paper and read four lines. He read them again. He handed it over his shoulder to Vale without looking.

"Mr. Mercer," Renn said, "you didn't break anything."

"Every testing center in the country went dark."

"Yes."

"Nineteen minutes."

"Yes. And when the network came back, every candidate in this building had a result except you, and yours said *authorized review*, which is why you've been sitting in this room since one o'clock. None of that is damage." He folded the confession along its crease and set it on the desk between them. "You reached the end of the examination. Nobody has done that in a long time."

Kade had spent seven hours preparing to be blamed. He had not prepared for anything else, and for a second he had nothing to say, which happened to him roughly never.

Elena filled it. "Explain what that means. In words a nurse can use."

Renn opened the case. Inside was a plastic sleeve, sealed along the top with a numbered tab. He tore the tab. It made a sound like a ticket. The sleeve had two words printed across the header in red, and Kade read them upside down before Renn turned them around.

ZERO PROTOCOL.

"The Aptitude Ascension Examination doesn't score to zero," Renn said. "It can't. The lowest result a candidate can produce by answering badly is somewhere in the low hundreds. Three zeros isn't a score. It's a route. When the examination meets a specific pattern of responses, it stops scoring the candidate, closes the session on every connected machine so the pattern can't propagate, and hands the candidate to a restricted review process. That process has a name. You're looking at it."

"It has a name," Kade said, "so somebody built it."

"Yes."

"On purpose. Years ago."

"Yes."

"For what?"

Renn's face did the thing. Kade had watched adult faces do it his whole life, the smoothing-over, the small internal meeting where they decided whether he was old enough for the sentence they had. He hated it more from this man than most, because this man plainly knew.

"For a category of candidate the public examination was not designed to evaluate," Renn said.

"That's a description of a hole."

"It's the description I'm able to give."

Vale spoke from the wall. "It's a failsafe." Renn turned his head, and she did not stop. "The section your son reached hasn't been in the public examination specification for decades. It was supposed to be unreachable. It stayed in the code because removing it would have meant explaining to a great many people why it was there. The last time a candidate produced a valid route through it, the receiving program was classified, and so was the candidate."

"Commander."

"He wrote a confession, David. He's owed a noun."

Elena said, very level, "How many decades."

Vale looked at her, then at Kade. "Three."

Thirty-one years. He had read it off the hallway screen that morning with pretzels in his hand. Three decades put the last valid route right at the start of everything. He did not know what that meant. He knew it meant something, and he knew from the way Renn's jaw set that he was not supposed to have done the subtraction.

"Don't build on that," Renn said.

"Too late."

"Kade." His mother, quietly. He glanced at her and stopped, because she had gone pale around the mouth, and it was not the tired pallor of a double shift. It was the other kind.

"You said valid," Kade said to Vale, because Vale was answering. "So I didn't guess."

"The branch can tell guessing from the other thing. You didn't guess."

"Then what did I do?"

Vale opened her mouth, and Renn said her name a third time, and she closed it. Not sorry. Filed. Kade recognized the look because he wore it.

"Is he safe?" Elena said.

"We have no evidence he isn't."

"That isn't what I asked. What do you think he is?"

Renn folded his hands. "We think your son may have an atypical Resonance profile."

"Atypical how."

"We don't know."

"Dangerous to him."

"No evidence."

"To anyone else."

"No evidence."

"Then why," Elena said, "are there four vehicles outside a school with forty-two students, two of which have been empty since lunch?"

It was Vale who answered. "Because the last time this happened, the answer was classified, and nobody in this room knows yet whether that was for your son's protection or somebody else's."

The heater kicked on. Nobody said anything for a while.

Renn took a second folder out of the case and set it on Kade's desk. It was heavy, the kind of paper stock nobody at Northline had ever ordered, and the crest on the cover was silver and raised, and Kade knew it the way he knew the shape of his own hand. It sat in the corner of every broadcast the whole country stopped for.

Asterion.

He did not touch it.

"No," he said.

"You haven't opened it."

"I don't need to. Somebody put the wrong name on something." He pushed it back across the desk with one finger. "Give it to whoever applied."

"Nobody applied. Asterion has invoked a classified admissions authority tied to the route you produced. There isn't an application. There's a statute."

"I'm not Resonant."

"Six hours ago I would have agreed with you. I don't currently know."

"I got zero."

"You did not get zero," Renn said, with the first edge Kade had heard in him. "You got routed. The number is the only part of today you've been allowed to see, and you are treating it as the whole story because it's the part that hurts least."

That landed somewhere Kade had not braced. He covered it. "So what is this? An offer?"

"No."

"Then what happens if I don't go?"

"Then within seventy-two hours Commander Vale's office arrives at your home with a different folder, under federal Resonance safety statute, and the conversation has fewer chairs." Renn said it without threat, the way Elena told a waiting room the wait was four hours. "Evaluation is mandatory. I'd rather it not feel like it."

"It feels like it."

"I know."

Elena reached across him, took the Asterion folder, and opened it. She uncapped the pen from her coat pocket, the one she signed medication counts with, and began writing on the inside of the cover in the square print she used for charts.

"He leaves tonight with me."

"Yes," Renn said.

"No surveillance inside my home. Not a car on the street, not a device, not a person walking a dog who doesn't own a dog."

Vale said, "Agreed."

"No press inquiries routed through your offices to us. If a reporter finds this house, it's because a reporter did work."

"Agreed."

"No medical testing of any kind that is not an emergency without his informed consent and mine, in writing, in advance, for as long as he is a minor." She looked up. "That includes anything you would like to call a baseline."

Renn hesitated.

It was less than a second. It was enough. Elena's pen stopped, and her eyes stayed on his face, and Kade watched her put the hesitation somewhere she would be able to find it again.

"Non-emergency procedures require consent," Renn said.

"Sign that."

She turned the folder around. He read what she had written and signed under it, and Vale signed under him without being asked, and Elena capped the pen and put the folder in her bag.

"He'll receive transport instructions by message," Renn said. "Departure inside seventy-two hours. He can bring what he likes within reason."

"What's reason?"

"There's a list."

"There's always a list," Kade said, and stood up, and for the first time all day nobody stood between him and the door.

---

Darius was on the curb at the edge of the lot, hood up, back against the tire warehouse fence, where he had clearly been for an hour. He got up and looked from Kade to the crest sticking out of Elena's bag.

"That's the school," he said finally.

"That's the school."

"You in trouble?"

"They've been very clear that I'm not. They said it a lot."

"That's how you know."

Elena had gone ahead to the car. Darius lowered his voice, though the men by the vehicles were forty meters off.

"Alvarez said something while you were in there. She said kids who go to that place don't come back to places like this." He shrugged inside the hood. "She said it like she was betting."

Kade thought about a vending machine that paid out for respect.

"Tell her she loses," he said.

Darius looked at him a second longer than the sentence required. Then he put his fist out, and Kade met it, and Darius said, "Text me when they give you a cape," and walked off toward the bus stop with the pretzel bag still in his hand, and did not look back, which was how Darius said the things he did not say.

---

Elena drove with the radio on low, which she never did. A man's voice was explaining that federal officials had not identified a cause for the shutdown, that the disruption appeared to have originated in the western region, that every affected candidate would receive a result within seventy-two hours. He said it three ways. None of them included a name, or a school, or a seventeen-year-old in a hoodie with a repaired cuff. For once Kade was grateful to be nobody. It would not last. Tonight it was true.

She turned it off at the second light.

"Say it," he said.

"Say what."

"Whatever you've been driving with."

She kept her hands at ten and two. That was how she drove when she was too tired to trust herself. "I've watched a lot of people sign themselves out against medical advice," she said. "Do you know what they have in common?"

"They're all nurses' kids."

"They start explaining why they're leaving before the doctor is in the room. By the time somebody comes in with an actual answer, they can't hear it, because they're already halfway to the parking lot in their heads." She glanced at him. "You slid that folder back before he finished saying the name of the school."

"It's a mistake."

"Then it's a mistake with four cars and a statute, and you can find out how it happened from the inside."

"You have been telling me since you were nine years old," Elena said, "that nobody has ever given you a real chance. You said it in Russell's office. You said it to two different social workers. You said it at fourteen with your arm in a sling and you meant every word." Her voice did not rise. It did the opposite, which was worse. "And tonight a real chance walked into Room Four with a federal seal on it and you tried to hand it back before it could look at you."

"That's not a chance. That's a summons."

"It's both. Most chances are." She stopped at the light. "You are allowed to be angry that it came like this. I'm angry that it came like this. You are not allowed to tell me you don't want it. You are bad at that lie, and I have known you since before you had teeth."

He had a dozen answers. Not one would survive being said aloud to her, in this car, at this light, and he knew it, and she watched him know it.

"What if I go," he said, "and it's a filing error."

"Then you come home."

"Then I'm the kid Northline sent to Asterion by accident. Russell tells that story at retirement parties."

"And if it isn't an error?"

He did not answer. The light changed and she let the car roll and did not push. She left the silence where it was until it told on him.

"Then I'd have to want something," he said, "where people could see."

"Yes."

"I don't do that."

"I know. I've watched you not do it for seventeen years." She turned onto the avenue, away from home, and he noticed and let it go for a block. "You told me once, and you were maybe ten, that if you never wanted anything then nobody could take it away from you. I thought it was very sad and very clever and I hoped you'd grow out of it. You grew into it instead."

"That's not fair."

"I'm your mother. Fair is your father's department." She said it lightly, the way she said all his father's departments, and then her mouth tightened at the corner and she did not say anything for a while.

"You passed the house," he said.

"I know where the house is."

---

The store was the discount place beside the empty pet shop, with a third of its sign burned out so it introduced itself to the parking lot as VAL E MAR. Elena parked under the working part of the sign and shut off the engine and did not get out.

"I'm not shopping," he said.

"You're not. I am. You're going to read that packet start to finish while I do it, because you've been carrying it for an hour and you haven't looked past the crest, and that is not like you."

She was right, and he resented it, and he took the folder.

He read under the dome light with the door open a crack so it stayed on.

Page one said MANDATORY EVALUATION in letters big enough to read from the next car. Page two was transport. Page three was the packing list: two formal academy sets, three training sets, two kinds of shoes, a weather shell, something called an approved field underlayer, and an optional practice uniform priced like the electric bill for a season. His stomach did the small drop it did whenever a number had his mother's name on it, and he kept turning, because nobody hid anything on page three.

Section sixteen began with mandatory equipment, spent two paragraphs on the academy's trademarks, and ended in a sentence set in type a size smaller than everything around it.

He read the sentence. He read the page number. He got out of the car.

He found her in an aisle with a black collared shirt over one arm, holding a pair of gray trousers up to the light. She had a cart, and in the cart was a rolling suitcase in hard blue plastic, and he decided to fight one battle at a time.

"Put the pants back."

"I'm not having this conversation in a store."

"Page eleven." He held the folder open on top of the shirt on her arm so she had to look. "*Required clothing and safety equipment during provisional jurisdiction remain the property and financial responsibility of the academy.* They issue it. All of page three. They put the list where you'd see it first and the sentence where you wouldn't."

She read it. She flipped back to page three, looked at the practice uniform and its price, and read the sentence a second time, moving her lips slightly, which she only did when she was memorizing something for later use against someone.

"They wrote a shopping list for families who can't afford it and made it free eight pages later." She put the trousers back on the rack with more force than trousers required. Then she looked at the shirt on her arm, and did not put it back.

"They issue clothes," he said.

"They issue their clothes. You are walking onto that island in one thing that is yours that isn't a hoodie with a cuff you fixed with a stapler." She set the shirt in the cart. "Eleven dollars. Do not make this a principle."

He let her have it. He had a bigger principle in the cart.

"Now the suitcase."

"The suitcase has wheels and a zipper that closes, which puts it two features ahead of what you own."

"The duffel closes."

"The duffel closes if you hold the slider with pliers and think good thoughts. It has been missing a tooth since March."

"So I'll fix it."

"You've been going to fix it since March."

He lifted the suitcase out of the cart and set it on the floor, where it stood on its four bright wheels between them, and he made himself say the true thing, because she was going to wait until he did, and he did not have another hour of waiting in him.

"It looks like somebody who's going to stay."

Elena looked at the suitcase for a while.

"All right," she said.

He had braced for the argument and nearly fell over the lack of one. "All right?"

"You're going for an evaluation. You're not enrolling. You're not consenting to anything past the door. And you don't have to prove any of that by carrying your socks in a bag that doesn't close." She took the suitcase by the handle and rolled it back to the shelf herself. "Fix the duffel tonight. Actually fix it. If it fails on that island in front of those people, I don't want to hear about it."

"You'll hear about it."

"I know I will."

---

Elena made soup, because in her house soup was a treatment plan and not a food, and he ate it because arguing with soup had never once worked.

His phone lay face down on her side of the table. He had handed it over before she asked, which she noticed and said nothing about. It lit twice with numbers he did not know and once with Darius asking whether they'd let him keep his hoodie, and nobody turned it over.

After the bowls were in the sink she went into the hall and stood at the closet with the door open and did not reach up. There was a box on the top shelf, black, with a lid that did not quite fit. He had opened it exactly once, at eleven, and put everything back in the same order, and never asked to see it again, because it was hers.

"You don't have to," he said.

"I know I don't." She brought it down anyway and set it on the table beside the Asterion folder, and the two of them sat on the wood together, one silver and one scuffed, and she looked at them like a woman who had not decided which was the emergency.

She took the lid off.

A watch that had stopped a long time ago. A mechanical pencil, silver, the kind you refilled. A plastic badge on a clip from an industrial systems company, the photograph too small to tell anything from. And two photographs, the one that had lived in a frame in the front room his whole life, and the other one, which had not.

The other one was Aaron Mercer at twenty-three or twenty-four, standing beside a bench with equipment on it that Kade could not identify, smiling at somebody outside the picture. Dark hair. Narrow face. People had been telling Kade since he could remember that he had his father's eyes, and he had never found a single use for it.

Elena held the print by its edges.

"Your hand," Kade said. "In Room Four, when I got to the number. I asked, and you gave me the people who were bleeding."

She did not pretend she had forgotten, which he was grateful for.

"I've been a nurse a long time," she said. "Things stop my hands. I don't always get told why, and I'm not going to invent a reason for you tonight."

"That's not no."

"No. It isn't."

"What did you hear in that room tonight that you'd heard before?"

She looked at him, and for once he could see her decide not to lie and also not to answer, which were, he was learning, two different operations. "I don't know what connects to what. I'm not going to guess in front of you. Guessing in front of you is how you end up on an island drawing conclusions with a pencil."

"That was one time."

"It was every time." She set the photograph back in the box, on top, facing up. "Your father worked for an industrial systems contractor. He did. That part is true."

*That part.* He heard it and let it go past him without touching it, the way you let a hot pan go past in a small kitchen. Grabbing it now would burn them both, and he could see her hands.

She put the lid on the box. She did not put the box away.

At nine forty-one the phone on her side of the table lit and stayed lit, and she turned it around so they could both read it.

*Transport confirmed. Departure fifteen thirty, day after tomorrow. Destination: Asterion Island. Status: Mandatory Evaluation. Zero Protocol.*

He read it three times. The words did not change.

"Go fix your bag," his mother said.

---

The duffel had been hers before it was his, green canvas from a grocery-store promotion older than he was, one handle re-sewn in thread that did not match, and a slider that had been sliding off the end of the track since March. He took it to the kitchen table, where things in this house got fixed, and moved the box of his father's things eight inches to make room, and did not open it.

Needle-nose pliers from the drawer. He worked the slider off the end of the coil and found the problem where he had known it would be: the top plate had spread a hair, enough that the teeth slipped past instead of meshing. He squeezed it closed a fraction at a time, because the difference between a slider that worked and one that jammed forever was thinner than the metal. It took eleven tries. Then the teeth caught, and he ran it the length of the bag, and it closed, and he sat there with his thumb on it.

His mother had gone to her room, which was not the same as bed. The new shirt hung from the back of a chair with the tag still on. The Asterion folder lay open where she had left it, and MANDATORY was the only word on the first page big enough to read from across the table.

He had told Darius he was coming back, and meant it. He had told Renn no, and had not meant it, and the man had known, and so had Elena, and so, he was finding out, had he.

That was the part he sat with, at ten at night, with a bag that finally closed. Not the cars or the statute or his mother's hand stopping on a word. Underneath the anger and the wariness and the seven hours in a plastic chair, something had come up through the floorboards while he was busy performing indifference, and it was not going away because he told it to.

He wanted to know what the machine had seen.

He wanted it badly enough that it frightened him, and he understood, turning the pliers over in his hand, that the fear was not of the island. Nobody had ever once handed Kade Mercer a question he could not answer and then waited for him. The fear was of the answer. He unzipped the bag and started with socks.

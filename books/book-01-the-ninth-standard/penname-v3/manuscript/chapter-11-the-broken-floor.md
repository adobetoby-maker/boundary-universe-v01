# Chapter 11 — The Broken Floor

*Twenty-seven. Mom says you've started saying usual hour like it's a place. She wants to know who's in it. I said it's probably a person. She said that's what she's asking.*

At quarter to six the Meridian Commons belonged to the cleaning units and one second-year asleep on the long couch with a textbook open across her face. The ladder wall ran anyway. It had no reason to sleep.

He had come down because lying in the dark composing a shared-state bracket in his head was not rest, and because the wall had the open challenges on it, and he read them now the way other people read weather.

Most opens were sensible. A fourth-year posting to anybody within five hundred ranks. The line third from the top was not sensible.

PIKE, JONAH. 2,301. OPEN CHALLENGE. NO RANK FLOOR. POSTED 04:52. ACCEPTANCES: 0.

An open with no floor was a joke told by someone high enough to afford it. Beat somebody six thousand ranks under you and the ladder paid you almost nothing. Lose to somebody six thousand ranks above you and it took almost nothing away. The number only moved if the lower fighter did something the board had not priced, and the board priced most things.

He read it for nine seconds. The log said so later, and he would have guessed less.

The band's tone was small in the empty room. The second-year did not wake. The wall rewrote its line. ACCEPTED. MERCER, KADE. 8,159. Under that, before he had finished breathing out, a gray bar dropped across it like a lid. HELD. FACULTY REVIEW. A location, a time twenty minutes off, and Hale's name.

He looked at the textbook on the sleeping girl's face and thought that he should probably have consulted someone, and that he had known he was not going to, and that this was why he had come down at quarter to six.

---

Hale took the review in the upper gallery of Arena Four, with the empty stands going down in front of them and a maintenance unit polishing the floor below in long overlapping ovals. He was eating breakfast from a paper tray and did not stop when Kade sat.

"Five fifty-three," Hale said. "What were you doing awake?"

"Not sleeping."

"Predict him. First exchange."

Kade had watched enough to do this without trying. "Fast first step. Right shoulder, with everything behind it. He wants the first one to land so the rest of the match is a conversation about the first one."

"Second."

"He walks. He doesn't rush a follow. He wants you to know he doesn't have to."

"Third."

"Make him miss twice and he gets interested. Interested means his output goes up. It's in the file."

"It is in the file." Hale put the tray down. "Now say the thing that isn't. He's not beatable. Not by you, not this month, and you know it. So you're buying a loss. What are you buying with it?"

"Time," Kade said. "Inside a transfer that size. I want to know if I can feel it coming. Pike doesn't throw small ones."

Hale nodded slowly, the way a man nods when a number he already had comes out of a machine.

"Director Sen's restriction. You'll remember it, because I read it to you. No attempt to reproduce the event."

"I'm not attempting anything. I'm standing in a ring."

"You're standing in the one ring on this island where it's most likely to happen, calling that a coincidence, and hoping I'm slow." Hale turned his slate around. Not footage. The public archive's access log, with Kade's band identifier running down it in a column. "Pike's Kepler bout. Fourteen times since Thursday. Nobody watches a fight fourteen times to learn a man drops his shoulder."

There was nothing on the slate he could improve.

"Here's why I'm not declining." Hale took the slate back. "If I decline, you find another open in a week and you don't tell me, because I'll have taught you that telling me costs you the match. I'd rather have you in a room where my hand is beside the panel."

He pulled a form onto the slate. MERCER FIELD RESTRICTION. ABORT TRIGGERS. Two lines were already filled in Park's neat clinical hand. VISUAL LAG. HAND TREMOR.

"Those are Park's. Is there anything Park doesn't have?"

"Cold," Kade said. "A line. From the back of the skull to behind the eyes. It came before the wall, in the teaching arena. A second before. Maybe less."

Hale wrote COLD LINE under the other two, and then, in the same hand, UNCOMMANDED TRANSFER ANOMALY, and turned the slate to face him.

"Any one of those four. You disengage and you signal. Open hand, straight up, above the head. If he's on you and you can't get clear, the hand still goes up, I suspend, and he releases on the tone. Your success condition is not winning. It's walking out with better information than you walked in with and no preventable injury to anyone in the building. That includes him." He held the slate out. "Confirming is free. The part that costs is later, in the second when you'd rather not."

Kade put his thumb on the field. Hale watched his face, not the thumb.

Eli had the prediction up when Kade got back to the room, on the big display, so that the numbers were the first thing in the door.

PIKE 91.8. MERCER 8.2.

"They ran it with variance off," Eli said, without turning around. "Somebody in Kepler wrote it for a class."

"Is the real number higher?"

Eli turned around. "The real number is that there isn't one. He's fought six people this year and none of them were you. Nothing in the model knows what you do with the floor." He heard himself. "With footwork. Mara's footwork."

"Sure."

He looked back at the display. "That number will be on every band on the island in an hour, and I'd like it noted I said it was wrong before it was fashionable."

Kade looked at eight point two and knew exactly what it wanted from him. It wanted to be fought. He closed the window.

In the Commons the number had beaten him downstairs. Taren was at the long table with no tray and a first-year on either side of him, taking what he called an informal survey and Sera, from the far end, called a book.

"I'm not holding money. I'm holding sentiment."

"Sentiment at what odds?"

"Eleven to one. Sentimentally."

"Why did Pike post it?" Sera said, not to Taren.

Taren answered anyway. "His last win was nine seconds long and nobody shared the clip. He wants a mismatch that travels."

"Who told you that?"

"His roommate told someone in Kepler who repeated it at a Sol thing, and a Meridian second-year was at the Sol thing."

"You know it's suspicious when you can say it that fast," Sera said.

"Everything's suspicious out loud. That's why people whisper."

Mara was not at breakfast. His band said, in her narrow way: *Bay One. 0900. Shoes.*

---

She ran him for twenty minutes without saying Pike's name. Four steps and a turn, four steps and a turn, rear foot later, don't cross, choose before you plant. The ribs from the Rusk week were down to commentary. She corrected his shoulders twice by pointing at them.

At the end she stood in the middle of the mat and folded her arms.

"He'll walk after the first one. He always walks. Don't fill the walk with anything. It's a gift and you'll want to answer it."

"That's it?"

"That's the only thing about him that's worth a sentence. The rest is that he's better than you." She looked at him for a moment longer. "What are you actually doing?"

He did not answer.

"That's an answer." She picked up her jacket. "I'll be in the bay."

---

The way to Arena Four from Meridian went through the Hall of Standards, and he had walked it a dozen times as a corridor with faces in it. Today he walked it counting, because counting was what he did instead of thinking about a shoulder, and the hall gave him things to count.

Eleven bronze portraits in relief, curved along the inside wall, larger than any human face had a right to be. The founders' generation, the first public Standard Eights, the names every child in the country could say in order by ten. Sen was fifth from the left, thirty years younger and holding the same expression, as if the sculptor had been warned. Under each bronze, a plate. Behind each bronze, the wall stepped back into a stone bay cut to hold it.

He counted the bays out of habit and got twelve.

He stopped. Sera walked into his back.

"What."

"Count the bays."

Eli counted, because he could not hear the word without doing it. "Twelve."

"Portraits."

"Eleven."

The twelfth bay was at the far end, past Sen, past the last plate, and it had no bronze. A flat maintenance panel filled the stone, painted to match and not quite matching, newer than everything around it the way a repair is always newer. A small construction tag sat near its lower edge. Eli went down on one knee to read it aloud.

"Legacy bay twelve. Access sealed."

"Why cut twelve?" Kade said.

Mara had stopped a few paces on. "The hall's older than the bronzes. They cut the wall first and hung the portraits later."

"That explains the stone. Not the count."

"Utilities," Eli said, getting up. "There's a service riser behind this wall. I've seen the map."

"That's a very calming answer."

"I'm a calming person."

"Suspicious masonry after," Sera said. "Fight first."

He went. He put it where he had put a twelfth column on Amadi's calibration wall and a twelfth screw in a ring of medallions on his own House roof. Three twelves, and eleven of everything visible, on an island that had cut its stone before it decided what to tell the children.

---

The prep bay under Arena Four had a bench, a glass wall onto the floor, and Pike's public profile hanging beside the glass for anyone who wanted to be discouraged by it. Strong Redirection. Moderate Amplification. Fast first step. Heavy right-side preference. Raises output when frustrated. Wins, five of six.

Eli had his model open on the bench and was not looking at it. Sera had the chair with the best view of the door. Mara stood at the glass.

Across the floor Jonah Pike was warming up alone, in no hurry, with the ease of a person who had never once wondered whether the building was on his side.

Sera turned her chair to face Kade. "Tell me the third reason."

"What?"

"Rank. His shoulder. Those are two. You do things for three reasons and tell people two."

He looked through the glass, where Pike had begun a slow set of transfers against a training post, each one whole, each one arriving exactly where it was sent. Big and clean. He wanted to stand inside one and watch.

"I accepted a legal challenge," he said.

"And you're being clever in a direction that ends in a chair. I've watched people do that. It doesn't look like anything until it does."

Mara turned from the glass. She had not spoken since Bay One.

"The word you used," she said, "was cold. After the wall. Four people heard it. You said it with your hand under your leg so nobody would look at the hand."

He had not known that.

"If it goes cold, you don't get to be curious. You get to put your hand up. That's the whole arrangement."

Hale's voice came through the prep speaker. "Mercer. Lane."

---

The calibration lane was a glass corridor with a rail at hip height and a technician who had done this ten thousand times and would have preferred to do it ten thousand more without conversation. Kade put both hands on the rail. The metal asked his Conduit a question. The display above answered for it.

CONVENTIONAL FIELD OUTPUT: 0.00. CONTROL CAP: NOT APPLICABLE.

Across the glass, Pike had his hands on the other rail, and his trace came up red from the bottom, climbed to a line just below the cap the match allowed, and stopped there, parked to the inch. REDIRECTION CAP: VERIFIED. AMPLIFICATION CAP: VERIFIED.

Hale stepped into the lane. "Open challenge rule. Either fighter may withdraw after calibration, no penalty, if the verified profile differs materially from the declared one."

Pike looked through the glass. "Zero. Still."

"Still."

"Then I've got nothing to withdraw from."

"That's the politest thing anyone's said about it," Kade said.

Pike's mouth moved a fraction. "You accepted. I don't post for fun."

Hale ignored them both. "Mercer. Restriction card is active. Triggers."

The gallery got quiet enough that he could hear his own band tick.

"Visual lag. Tremor. Cold line. Uncommanded anomaly."

"Response."

"Disengage. Hand up. If he prevents it, hand up anyway. You suspend."

"Pike. You release on the first suspension tone. If you've started a strike, you release the strike."

"I know the tone," Pike said, looking at Kade rather than at Hale. "I've released on it before. It's not a trick."

He meant it. It was possible to see him mean it. For a moment the match was not a percentage or a clip. It was three people saying out loud where responsibility changed hands, in front of a gallery that had come for something else.

Hale opened the lane.

Eli's fist came through the gap and Kade touched it. Sera said, "Boring feet." Mara said nothing at all, which was worse, and he stepped onto the floor.

---

Arena Four was built to be looked at. The floor was the same white composite as the teaching room and three times its area, a red strip a long step in from a barrier that stood twice his height, and the stands rose steep above the barrier to a ring of lights, so that sound came down on you rather than across. Past the strip the bout was over. Not a point lost. Over.

He knew more about the floor than anyone in the stands. After the Rusk week he had opened the maintenance plan that hung off the Arena Safety Orientation, because it had diagrams and nobody read it. Structural ribs ran west to east under the composite. A damper sat under each third of the floor, west, center, east, and took what the surface above it could not. The barrier's footings tied into the same subframe. The room was one built thing that had agreed to look like a floor, a wall, and some air.

He knew that on paper. He was not supposed to be able to feel it through his shoes.

Pike waited in the east circle, broad through the shoulders and heavy in the legs, with the calm of an upper-year who had somehow been left in the first-year cohort. The calm shifted when he saw Kade up close.

"You're smaller than the clip."

"Clips lie."

"Not about me."

The barrier sealed with its two-note tone. Hale read the rules from the rail with his hand flat beside the abort panel, not on it. Field caps. Controlled strikes only. Nothing above the collarbone. Three points.

He did not look at Pike. He looked at the floor under Pike's feet. What the man would push against to become a problem.

Green.

Pike came, and the first exchange was the file read aloud. The first step covered more floor than a step should. The strike behind it had all of him in it, and Kade got his forearm in the way late and the force went straight through the arm into the shoulder and turned him half around. The board said one for Pike. He kept his feet. Only that.

Pike did not follow. He walked, exactly as Mara had said, three easy steps on a curve, letting Kade watch him not hurry.

Don't fill it. Kade stood in the walk and did nothing with it, and felt how much he wanted to, and did nothing anyway.

"Thought you'd be quicker," Pike said.

Kade breathed through the shoulder. "Thought you'd be quieter."

Low. He checked it. High, and he blocked that with both arms. Pike smiled, and behind the smile Kade saw the thing that made him dangerous. He did not enjoy hurting people. He enjoyed deciding for them. That was a man who would take an offer.

Kade circled left and Pike cut it off. He put Mara's feet under him, rear foot later, choose before you plant, and Pike's next strike passed him by the width of a hand. He felt the satisfaction arrive, and the shoulder behind the miss arrived after it and shoved him a long step toward the west side of the floor. He stopped with the strip a hand's width from his heel and did not look at it. He knew where it was.

He reset, back onto clean floor, and Pike let him.

The third time Pike came, Kade saw it whole before there was anything to see. Weight onto the front foot. Hip beginning to turn. Shoulder committing a quarter second before the arm had any reason to. Nothing to do with Resonance. A body asking the floor for permission to become dangerous. He moved on the ask. Inside the line, under the shoulder, Pike's fist going by his ear with air on it, two fingers on the back of Pike's forearm to borrow the direction, and a pivot that put him behind the man with a meter of floor to himself.

He had not redirected anything. Nothing cold. His own feet, and Mara's.

Pike turned around. "Better."

"People keep saying that right before something bad happens to me."

Pike did not get angry. He got interested, and the file had told Kade what interested looked like, and he had wanted it, and now he had it. Pike's output went up. It was visible in the way the floor took his weight. He drove, not fast, in a straight line, and the line pointed at the western third of the floor, because Pike wanted him against the strip with nowhere to put his feet, and under each of Pike's steps the load bloomed large and clean and exactly the shape of the six red maps Kade had opened fourteen times.

West damper under his heels now. Ribs running east beneath the composite toward Pike. Barrier footings sharing the frame. He had two steps of floor and then the strip and then the end of the match, and Pike knew that arithmetic as well as he did.

Pike loaded.

It was a big one, the biggest yet, the kind that took most of a second to arrive, and in that second the arena stopped being separate things. Mat. Anchors. Lattice. Slab. Barrier. Pike's weight going into the floor and the floor's answer going back up into Pike, and Kade's own weight inside the same exchange, one connected answer to one physical question that nobody had asked yet.

The cold line drew itself from the base of his skull to the place behind his eyes.

There was time. That was the part he would never manage to say later in a way that made it smaller. The panel was thirty meters away with Hale's hand flat beside it, perfectly visible. His right hand was free. He got it as high as his hip. He would remember that afterward more clearly than the strike, his own hand rising to the hip, the card with his thumbprint under it, Mara saying you don't get to be curious, and the hand stopping there, because he wanted to know, and the wanting had been in the room since quarter to six.

The hand went back down.

He did not think redirect. He did not think anything with a verb in it. He put his attention on one place in the chain, the seam where his body was supposed to stop and the floor was supposed to start, the line a diagram gave its own color because a diagram needed one, and he chose it the way you put a finger on a map.

*There.*

Pike's strike landed.

The sound was wrong. Not the flat crack of a body being hit. Something deep and structural, from underneath, the sound a building makes when it has an opinion about itself.

The floor broke.

A fracture ran out from under Kade's front foot, split cleanly around an embedded sensor panel as if it had known the panel was there, and kept going west until it met the edge of the ring. The surface under him dropped several centimeters and stopped. Pike stumbled backward off a floor that had changed height under his lead foot. Kade did not move at all. He was not sure he could.

Red safety fields came up along the barrier. MATCH SUSPENDED.

There was a silence with the whole stands in it. Then everyone in the stands spoke at once.

He had not struck the floor. Pike had not struck the floor. The energy had gone there anyway.

The cold line went hot. Then it hurt, in a way that had a shape, and his hands started to shake.

Hale was on the floor at a run, which Kade had never seen. "Both of you. Nobody moves."

"I'm fine," Pike said, from where he had caught himself.

"I didn't ask."

Kade tried to say the same thing and found his tongue a half beat behind the sentence. He let the sentence go.

Park came through the barrier gap with two technicians and looked at his face before anything else. "Symptoms."

"Pressure."

"Number."

"Five."

"Hands."

He held them out, and they answered for him, and her mouth did the small tight thing it had done in the teaching arena.

Sen came last and did not hurry. He had seen her angry. This was not that. This was the face Elena wore at a monitor when the numbers disagreed with what the patient had said. Sen looked at the fracture. Then at Pike. Then at Kade. Then at the sensor feed on a technician's tablet, for longer than at any of them.

"What did you do?"

He swallowed. "I don't know."

Pike laughed once, without any humor in it. "That makes two of us. I threw a normal one and the building fell down."

They cleared the stands, which guaranteed the video. Asterion students could smell forbidden footage through concrete.

---

Medical took two hours and found nothing to hold.

No structural injury. No abnormal Conduit discharge. No measurable Resonant output sufficient to explain a floor, a wall, or anything heavier than a cup. Park ran the tracking test with a stylus and made him follow it with his eyes and not his head, and the second time his eyes were late by an amount she wrote down without saying.

"What day is it?"

"The day I broke the floor."

"Date."

He gave it. She wrote that down too.

The tremor faded more slowly than it had after the teaching arena. That concerned Park. It concerned him too, and he did not make a joke out of it, and she noticed that he did not.

Jonah Pike came in twenty minutes later with two sports drinks and a bruise ripening along one forearm.

Park did not look up. "This is a treatment room."

"I'm bringing electrolytes to an opponent. That's in the sportsmanship module."

"You may bring one."

Pike put a bottle on the rolling table and leaned against the wall as if he had been invited to. "My coach had the sensor trace up before they locked it. My strike was in profile. Dead center of my usual. I've thrown that one a thousand times."

"That's reassuring."

"The load in the frame wasn't in anybody's profile."

"Less reassuring."

Pike drank. "You scared?"

*No* came up first, the old door-closing reflex, the one that had answered every adult at Northline before they finished asking. Kade looked at his hand on the blanket. Two fingers were still moving on their own.

"Yes."

Pike nodded. "Good. I'd have worried otherwise." He pushed off the wall. "For the record, I wanted a clip. I got one. It's not the clip I wanted." He stopped at the door. "Get it sorted. I'll take the rematch when there's a floor."

He was gone before Kade thought of anything to say to that. It did not make the morning smaller. It made the world around the morning ordinary again, and that was worth more than he would have guessed.

The next knock was Hale, and a woman in a gray facilities coat with white dust on one sleeve and a tablet under her arm. Park put herself between them and the bed.

"Five minutes."

"Three," the woman said. "I'm structural. I need his order of events before the replay teaches him a cleaner one."

Park looked at his pupils again. "Three. If he drops a word, you're done."

The engineer did not open footage. She opened a blank plan of Arena Four on the rolling table and put a stylus in his good hand.

"Your feet."

He marked them.

"His."

Two marks.

"His last strike."

An arrow.

"Where you meant to go."

A shorter arrow, angled in under the shoulder.

She peeled the plan down a layer to the subframe, and before she asked, he touched the western damper.

"That one first."

Her hand stopped over the tablet. "The public schematic doesn't show dampers."

"The maintenance plan does. It's attached to Arena Safety Orientation."

Hale's face tightened. "Nobody reads that."

"It had diagrams."

The engineer turned the model. "The west damper took the first abnormal load. Then this rib. Then the sensor housing, which is why the crack went around it. Then the edge anchor." Each one lit red in turn, a path away from where his foot had been. She flicked to a second display. A ledger. Input. Output. Heat. Structural strain. "Nothing added. Nothing missing. The totals close. Everything he threw is accounted for. It just arrived at an address nobody had on file."

He read the totals twice. Whatever he had done had not made force.

Park raised two fingers.

The engineer changed the display one more time. Arena Four closed three days minimum. Six combat sections relocated. Two team assessments with nowhere to go. A crew to lift the western third of the floor and check every anchor tied to the frame. The crack in the video looked dramatic. The list looked expensive.

"Was anybody under it?"

"No. The service trench is checked remotely before every match," she said, holding his eye. "That's procedure. It isn't luck. I want you to know which one it was."

He nodded.

"Anything before the strike?"

He could have said no. Hale was standing right there, and Hale had a card.

"Pressure behind the eyes. Then cold."

Hale unfolded his arms. "One of the four. Did the hand go up."

"It got to my hip."

"And."

"And I wanted to see," Kade said. "So I put it down."

Hale let the sentence stand there and did not help it.

Kade looked at the list of relocated sections. "That was the wrong call."

"That's what I needed," the engineer said, and closed the plan.

Park showed her out. Hale stayed.

"No contest. No ladder movement, either of you. You're restricted from challenges and any unsupervised combat until Park clears you and the Director finishes her review. Not a yellow line. A red one."

There it was, the thing he would have fought at Northline because somebody else had drawn it. This one had his thumbprint under it.

"Understood."

Hale's eyebrows went up a degree. "That's it?"

"Would arguing fix the floor?"

"No."

"Then that's it."

Hale took the restriction card out of his pocket, the physical one, printed, and set it on the rolling table beside the sports drink.

"Keep that. You'll want to know what your own name looks like the next time you decide it doesn't apply."

Elena called before Park had finished the discharge notes, and he thought about letting it ring, and the thought lasted until her name came up a second time.

She was in the concrete stairwell of the hospital parking structure, where the nurses went to make calls the break room would hear. Rain behind her on the open side. Her ID badge was flipped backward against her scrubs, which meant she had come straight out of something.

"Camera on your face."

"Hi to you too."

"Kade."

He turned it. She looked at him the way she had looked at fifteen years of people who had planned to keep a symptom.

"Pupils."

"Equal. Park says."

"Headache."

"Two. It was five."

"Hands."

He held one up. The last two fingers moved without him.

She watched them for a while.

"Discharged?"

"Medically. Not otherwise."

He told her. The restriction. Sen's review. He left out the trench, and then he saw her face begin to do the thing it did when he edited around an injury, and he put the trench back in.

"Nobody was under it. They check before every match. That's a rule, not a lucky day."

"Good." No relief in it yet.

"How did you get the video?"

"A patient showed it to Darius on the bus. Darius sent it to me with no words. Darius always has words. It shows the floor dropping under you."

"A few centimeters."

"I don't want smaller units. I want to know why you accepted a fight from somebody six thousand ranks above you when you're on a line."

"His transfers are big and clean," Kade said. "I wanted to be inside one and see if I could feel it coming."

Her face went absolutely still.

"You ran a trial on yourself. No controls. No stop rule you were willing to use."

"There was a stop rule."

"Did you use it?"

"No."

"Then there wasn't one." She pressed the heel of her hand against her forehead for a second, a gesture he had seen her make at the kitchen table over bills. "Were you trying to find out if the thing from the wall would happen again?"

"Yes."

Elena breathed out.

"Thank you for saying it."

He had prepared for the other thing. This landed somewhere he had no plan for.

"You're allowed to be angry."

"I am angry. I'm angry in a stairwell." Her voice did not rise. "And I am not going to make it cost you more to tell me the truth than it would have cost you to lie. That's the deal. That has been the deal since you were nine."

"I remember the deal."

"Do they know what happened?"

"They know all the force was Pike's. It went into the building instead of me. They don't know why. They don't know about the shaking."

"Then you don't know enough to sign up your nervous system for the next one."

"I know."

"What happens now?"

"Park watches. Sen reviews. Hale has me out of everything with a barrier around it until both of them sign."

"That's them. That's your body. What do you do?"

He looked down the corridor toward the treatment rooms.

"Tell them everything I noticed. Stop at the warning next time, not after."

"Good."

"You sound surprised."

"I sound like a woman who just watched a building lose to her son." Somebody opened the stairwell door above her. She raised a finger at it without turning. "Call me after Sen. A call. Not the thing where you send me three words."

"Authoritarian."

"Alive."

That landed too close to the fear for either of them to joke past it.

"I love you too."

"I love you. You're allowed to stop before it breaks. Nobody will think less of you. I've checked."

The screen went dark. He stood by the elevator with a hand that would not entirely agree to hold still and then he went home.

*Twenty-eight. Mom says you told her the whole thing before she asked and she's furious. She said furious the way she does when it's already half over. I'm not furious. I need the floor explained to me by someone who isn't a patient on a bus.*

---

The footage had escaped, as promised, and Eli was waiting at the elevator with the numbers.

"Official copy's gone. Somebody filmed a screen. Several somebodies. It's been off the island for three hours. Also they're calling it something."

"Don't."

"The Mercer Fault."

Kade stopped in the corridor. "No."

"I didn't name it. I'm reporting."

"It sounds like a medical condition."

"It sounds like a landmark," Eli said, and could not quite keep the pleasure out of it. "Landmarks last."

On the way to the Commons two students stepped out of his path, one without noticing that she had, the other noticing very much. A first-year with the Meridian stripe on his sleeve held the Commons door for him, stood a full step farther back than the door required, and said, to the floor, "Can you do that to a person?"

"No," Kade said, and then, because the first-year was thirteen and it was his House, "I don't know. I don't think so. I don't know."

The first-year's face changed. It was the honest answer and he hated it the instant it existed.

Sera was there, at his elbow, from nowhere. "Go eat," she told the first-year, and the first-year went. "You were about to give a frightened thirteen-year-old a lecture on experimental uncertainty."

He considered. "Fair."

His usual seat was open. That mattered more than the two who had moved. There was already a plate on it.

Mara was in the Meridian kitchen with the kettle going and her back to the door, and she did not turn around when he came in, which was how he knew she had heard the elevator.

"Still shaking?"

"Some."

"Your hand got to your hip."

He stopped. "Who told you that."

"Nobody told me. I was at the glass. I watched it go up and I watched it stop." The kettle clicked off. She poured, and still did not turn. "You were supposed to stop."

"I didn't choose the transfer."

"That's not the same as not choosing the conditions, and you know the difference, because I've heard you explain it to Navarro."

She was angry, not because he had ignored her but because she had stood at the glass and known what the stopped hand meant before the floor did, and she did not enjoy being right that way.

"I had a second," he said. "I spent it on something else. I should have spent it on the hand."

She turned around then, with two cups.

"That may be the most responsible sentence you've ever said in this building."

"Don't tell anyone."

"I'm going to tell everyone." She put one cup in front of him and it was not tea. It was the thing Sera made him drink after Rusk, which meant they had talked. "Drink that."

Eli found them at the long table ten minutes later and sat down with the question already out of his mouth. "What did it feel like?"

Kade looked at his cup. He considered lying, for exactly as long as it took to check who was at the table, and then stopped considering it.

"Like somebody had drawn the whole arena as one shape. And then drawn Pike, and me, and the floor, and the wall inside it with a lighter pen. And the lighter lines were the only part that was optional."

Nobody said anything.

"Optional how?" Mara said.

"Like a diagram. You draw the line where your system stops because you have to draw it somewhere. It's not a fact. It's a choice somebody made so the equation would close." He turned the cup. "For about a second nobody was standing there making the choice. So I did."

Eli had gone completely still, which for Eli was a symptom. "Felt. Not through the Conduit."

"No."

"Then through what?"

"I don't know."

"That," Eli said carefully, "is an alarming answer, and I'm going to write it down and put a box around the box."

Sera came in with a paper bag and put it in front of him. "That was a protein bar at medical. I know your face. It's not food."

For ten minutes nobody asked him about physics. They argued about the name instead, and he lost four to one, and losing an argument about a landmark named after him did more for his hands than two hours of Park had.

Then Taren came in, took the table in at a glance, and held up both palms. "No floor jokes. I want that noted."

"You have six," Sera said.

"I have nine. I'm exercising restraint."

He sat. Kade watched him do it. Taren's eyes went to Kade's hand first, then to the medical band on the wrist, then to Eli's tablet, where the replay sat paused on the spectator angle, and then Taren said, easily, "They locked the sensor trace a minute after Sen looked at it. Pike's coach is furious. He wanted to show the Kepler feed."

"How do you know when they locked it?"

Taren blinked. "The coach said so. On the Kepler feed."

"Which you watch."

"Which everybody watches. It's the best feed. They have graphics."

Eli, wounded, said, "I have graphics."

It worked, mostly. Kade put it in the file with the roommate and the Sol thing and the second-year who had been there. The file was getting crowded, and he did not yet know what kind of file it was.

His band chimed with a priority tone he had heard twice before. DIRECTOR SEN. ARENA FOUR. 20:00. COME ALONE.

Sera read it upside down. "The arena. That's a mood."

He stood. Mara said, "Kade."

He looked back.

"Whatever she tells you tonight. Count what she doesn't."

"That's almost kind."

"It's arithmetic."

Arena Four at night was a different room. The stands were dark. The broadcast lights were off and the work lights were on, low and yellow, four of them on stands around the western third of the floor, so that the fracture threw a shadow. Tape ran around the whole third. Somebody had chalked numbers along the crack at intervals, the way a doctor marks the edge of a bruise to see whether it grows.

Sen stood at the west edge with her hands in her coat pockets and a tablet dark under her arm.

"Walk it," she said. "Inside the tape. It's stable."

He walked the crack from the ring edge to the place where his foot had been, and stood there, and looked east toward where Pike had stood, and the floor was still several centimeters lower under his shoes than under hers.

"Tell me what you perceived. Not what you did. What you perceived, in order."

He told her. This time he kept nothing back. Pike's weight going into the floor and the floor's answer going back up. The ribs and dampers and footings he had known from a plan nobody read. The instant the separate things stopped being separate. The cold line. The hand at his hip. The word *there*, which had not felt like an instruction so much as a selection, a finger set down on a diagram choosing which line got to be real.

When he finished, she said nothing for long enough that the work lights ticked as they cooled. He let the silence sit. It was harder to learn than notation.

"You need a vocabulary," Sen said finally. "Before you invent the wrong one and it sticks."

"That sounds like the start of something."

"It's the start of a smaller thing than you're hoping."

She looked at the crack rather than at him.

"Boundary architecture."

Two ordinary words. She said them the way a person sets down something with a crack already in it.

"What is it?"

"Not a Standard." She crouched, which he had never seen her do, and put two fingers flat on the composite on either side of the fracture. "Tomorrow we discuss what we know. Tonight you understand one thing, and you understand it well enough to say it to your mother. You produced nothing. Force that already existed resolved somewhere our models did not expect it to. That is the entire event. Everything else is where it resolved, and why, and that is a longer conversation than tonight."

Something cold went through him that had nothing to do with the anomaly. Weeks ago, on a covered walk, she had taken four steps and told him yes, the thing had a name, and not from her, and not that day.

"You already had that name," he said.

She did not move.

"You said it the way you'd say something you were remembering. Not something you were making up for me."

The work lights ticked.

"Has somebody done this before?"

"Tomorrow."

"That's a yes."

"That's a Director who wants Park's clearance in her hand before she takes a tired seventeen-year-old any further into a subject with a floor missing from it." She stood. "Which is not the same as no."

"You'd say that if it were no."

"Yes. I would."

He walked back along the crack to the tape, and wanted to demand the rest, and knew by now what wanting felt like right before it cost something. He turned around instead.

"Is it dangerous?"

Her face did change then, one degree, and it was not the change he expected.

"Everything worth having is dangerous in proportion to how badly you misunderstand what it costs. That isn't a saying. That is the whole of what I know."

"That sounded like it had been said before."

"It has. It's older than you."

Another answer with the truth beside it. He put it on the pile, and the pile was not a pile anymore. It had edges. He ducked under the tape and left her standing over the crack with her hands back in her pockets.

The old corridor out of the arena was empty. His phone buzzed against his hip.

*Twenty-nine. A guy in the bay said you're famous now. I said you were famous at Northline for the fire door. He said this is bigger. Mom says call her before you answer me. I say she's right and also I want the floor.*

Kade leaned on the stone and laughed once, and it hurt, and he typed with the hand that had almost stopped.

*Called her. She got the whole thing. You get the floor tomorrow. Tonight I'm getting told what it's called.*

The reply came before he had pocketed the phone.

*PROUD OF YOU. ALSO DON'T.*

He looked back down the corridor at the yellow light spilling out of Arena Four. Boundary architecture. A name that had existed before him, in someone's mouth, long enough to be said carefully.

In his jacket pocket was a printed card with four triggers on it and his own thumbprint under them. He had read it that morning as a permission. He would read it from now on as a receipt.

# Chapter 9 — Meridian Night

*Twenty-three. Mom says the bruise stayed inside the line. She says you told her that yourself, before she asked. She said it twice. I think she's worried you've been replaced.*

At seven fifty-five Taren looked up through the Commons glass at the bell, opened his mouth for the count, and said a different number.

"Eight thousand one hundred fifty-nine."

Half the room had started the groan on reflex, and it broke apart when the number turned out not to have months in it. Heads came around. The panel by the door had cycled to the ladder, and Kade's line sat in the middle of the screen with a green arrow beside it that said eight hundred and twelve places.

"Also fifteen months, five days," Taren said, and sat down.

Kade kept his eyes on his toast. He had known the number since six, when Eli had read it off the tablet and said nothing at all, which for Eli was a speech. A week of ranked fights since the review. More won than lost, none prettily, and both losses to people who had taught him something on purpose. The ribs had stopped talking except on the tower stair.

Sera came down the Commons with a grease pencil and wrote on the wall board under the menu, in capitals that leaned, MERIDIAN NIGHT. Under it, jobs and names. HEATERS AND LIGHTS: BELL. FOOD: KIM. SOUND: ANYONE BUT NAVARRO, which got a laugh from the landing and a wounded noise from Eli. TABLE: PATEL. STAIRS: EVERYBODY WITH TWO HANDS.

Kade read the board twice. His name was not on it. Reading a board twice was the sort of thing he would have noticed in somebody else.

"What's Meridian Night," he asked Nessa Kim, who had sat down across from him with fruit she was not going to share.

"The House went up." She said it the way you would say it had rained. "Second in the engineering relay. Three of ours placed on the week's ladder. When the House goes up, we go on the roof."

"Three of ours."

She looked at him over the fruit. "You're on the week's ladder, Mercer. Where did you think eight hundred places went?"

He had not thought of them as going anywhere. They had been his. It had not occurred to him that a House table had quietly added his week to its own.

"I'm not on the board."

"The board's jobs." Nessa considered him the way she considered a mat before she stepped onto it. "You know what the board is. Then you know what it isn't," she said, and took her fruit to the window.

---

At Northline, not being asked had been the entire reward. You made yourself expensive to ask, and that was called being left alone. Here the same silence had a different shape. A name that was not on Sera's board was going to walk up the tower stair at dark and be handed a plate by somebody who had carried it.

So at four he went up without telling anyone. The roof door was propped with a brick, and the wind came through the gap off the water with salt in it.

Anton Bell was on a ladder against the mechanical housing with a coil of light ribbon over one shoulder and a screwdriver in his teeth. The ribbon ran from the antenna mast down across the roof to the greenhouse frame, sagging to head height where nobody had tensioned it.

"Ground," Anton said around the screwdriver, not looking down.

Kade took the loose end and paid it out. The plate Anton was anchoring to was set into the housing wall at shoulder height, brass gone green: FOUNDING STANDARD EIGHTS, the letters worn soft, with a ring of small portrait medallions around the words, the faces too old and too dark to read. Anton had a carabiner hooked through something at the bottom of the ring.

"That's House history you're hanging lights off."

"That's a screw." Anton took the driver out of his teeth. "It's held the lights six years. Before that it held a banner."

Kade counted the medallions, because he counted things. Eleven. Then he counted the recesses they sat in, and got twelve. The twelfth was at the bottom of the ring, empty, the same size as the others, with one original mounting screw still seated at its center and Anton's carabiner hanging off it.

"There were twelve of something."

"Donor plaque, probably. Board's older than the House charter. The Eights built the place, somebody paid for it, somebody got a disc. Pull."

Kade pulled. The ribbon came taut from mast to frame, and a string of tiny white lights came on along the whole length of it. Four in the afternoon, and they still did something to the roof.

He filed the twelfth recess where he filed things. Asterion was full of objects nobody could explain without becoming annoyed, and a spare screw was not the strangest, and he had come up here to be useful, not curious.

He held the ladder while Anton came down, which was a job, and not the one he had come up here wanting.

Sera found him doing it. She had a folding table under each arm, and she looked at the ladder, and at his hands on it, and at his feet.

"Toes."

He looked down. He was on his toes. Holding a ladder.

"Where does the board say you are?"

"It doesn't."

"So you're up here doing Anton's job badly because nobody wrote your name down." She set the tables against the parapet. "I know that walk. I've done it. You work like there's a bill coming and you'd rather pay before they hand it to you. Nobody sent you a bill, Mercer. If you want a job, Patel needs a second pair of hands on the table. If you don't, come up at dark and eat. Both are allowed. Paying isn't a third option."

He stood there being read.

"The ribs," she said.

"Cleared."

"Then carry the light end."

"Which end's the light end?"

"Find out," Sera said, and went to intercept Eli, who had come through the door with a food crate and a speaker cable he was pretending belonged to the crate.

---

The table lived on the landing one floor down, on its side against the wall. It was a long scarred slab with iron legs that did not fold, and Dev Patel stood beside it with his arms crossed.

"You're the ladder."

"I'm the new one."

"Same thing." Dev was a fourth-year with a broad back and the patience of somebody who had carried this table before. "You take the stair end. The turn at the top is the whole job. When I say tilt, you tilt toward me, not toward the wall. The wall has opinions."

The table was heavier than it looked, and it looked heavy. On the first flight it was an object. On the second it became a conversation about where his feet were. At each turn Dev said tilt, and Kade tilted, and the ribs said something in their own vocabulary, and he let them.

At the second turn Dev set his end on a stair and breathed. "Every Meridian who gets through a first month puts a name on the underside. Rank too, if you want one. You can put a line through the rank, and you can only do it on a Meridian Night, and you never rub one out. Strike it, write the new one beside it. That's all of it."

"Why only tonight."

"So you sit with the old number a while and find out if you care." He picked up his end. "I've been sitting with mine eleven months."

They came through the roof door with the table between them, and the raised lip he had watched other people fight all week turned out to be a problem for tables too. His rear foot found it, and for one half second he was on a mat with Mara's shoe beside his and the word *choose* in the air, and he put the foot down on the far side of the lip instead of on top of it.

"Tilt," Dev said.

They set it down upside down, legs up, and Dev knelt without ceremony, took a marker from his jacket, and found his own name near the center brace.

DEV PATEL — 6,440.

He drew one line through the number with a care that had nothing to do with the marker and wrote 1,998 beside it. He sat back on his heels and looked at it.

"Crossed two thousand in May," he said. "Wanted to know if I'd still care in September. I care. That's worth knowing." He held the marker out. "You're a month?"

"Nearly."

"Meridian rounds up."

Kade got down on the roof beside him. Hundreds of names went out from the brace in every direction, in every hand, some dated years back. Ranks with lines through them and new ranks beside, and beside some a worse one, honestly written, and then better again. Near one leg, in the leaning capitals from the Commons board, SERA VALE, no rank, and under it LEFT TWICE. CAME BACK TWICE. He looked at it longer than he had meant to.

He found a clear space and wrote. KADE MERCER — 8,159.

On the board it was a fact about him that other people read. Here it was a thing he had put on the underside of a table with his own hand, among people who had done the same, and he wanted to put something beside it, so the number would not be the only thing there.

"Rank's the least interesting thing anyone writes under here," Dev said.

"What's the most."

"What they were bad at." They turned it over onto its iron feet, and the names went face down against the roof.

---

By dark the roof was somewhere else. Six heaters in a ring threw orange up the parapet. The ribbon line burned white from the twelfth screw to the greenhouse. Couches carried up the tower stair by people with two hands faced the water as if the water were something you watched. The long table stood in the middle under food that two people could not have carried.

He had a rule for rooms where somebody wanted to hit him. He had no rule for this. Around him the House argued about whether the engineering relay had been robbed of first. Nobody asked him what he could do. He waited for it the way you wait for a step that is not there.

The first person to say the word rank was doing it as a game.

"First Impression," a third-year named Mina was saying, from a couch arm. "You look at a stranger. Posture, hands, what they did with their jacket. You guess their rank."

"Everybody here knows everybody."

"Then guess a guest." She pointed with her cup at a tall boy in an academy jacket cut better than issue, standing with two people who were laughing in the right places. "Him. Go."

Guesses went around. Two hundred. Three-fifty. Eighty, from Eli.

"One eighty-three," Mina said. "Cal Reid. Somebody's guest. He asked Nessa where the Meridian roof was. From the Meridian roof."

Kade watched Cal Reid notice the couch noticing him, detach from his friends, and arrive.

"I heard my name."

"You were being guessed," Mina said. "You came out high."

"I came out correct." Cal looked at the couch, and the game, and Kade, and Kade watched him arrive at an idea. "Let's do him."

"He's not a stranger."

"He's a stranger to me." Cal's smile had nothing in it. "Mercer. The Zero. I watched the end of Shah, the last six seconds, the part that didn't add up." He turned a little as he said it, to the couch, to the heaters, to anyone in range, which was the point. "So here's a better game. Not where he is. Where he'll be at midyear. I'll start. I say nobody knows, because Sen has him on a yellow line, and when she's finished looking at whatever that was, it's gone, and he's back where the board found him." He lifted his cup an inch toward Kade. "No offense. It's a party. I'm asking for predictions."

The couch had gone quiet in the way of people who were not going to be the one to say something.

At Northline the answer would already have been out of his mouth. One sentence with an edge on it, and Cal's next would grow an edge to match, until there was a thing on the roof that Kade could later say somebody else had started. It had never been a decision. It was only where his feet went.

His feet stayed. Not virtue. He was deciding, and the deciding took one beat longer than it used to, and the beat was where the evening got decided without him.

"Four thousand," said a voice from the far side of the couch.

Everyone turned. Julian Cross sat on the other arm with a plate on his knee, had apparently been there some time, and had said it the way you say a guess in a game.

"Four thousand at midyear. Ordinary climb. No yellow line in it, no Shah. He's up eight hundred a week on footwork, the footwork's getting better, and the band gets harder from here." He ate something off the plate. "That's my prediction. You asked."

Cal's face did a thing. "I wasn't asking you."

"You asked the couch. I'm on the couch." Julian looked at Cal for the first time, and not for long. "You've been a hundred and eighty-three since last year, Reid. You should be asking who's coming. Not telling them they won't."

It was not loud. Nobody laughed. Cal stood with his cup for as long as it takes to look for a sentence and not find one, said "Cross" in the voice of a man leaving on his own schedule, and left on it.

"I'm still a guest," Kade said. It came out level, which surprised him.

"You signed the table. I watched you." Mina got up. "Guests don't carry it."

---

Rank one was sitting on a couch arm eating dumplings off a paper plate, and Kade, because it was what his attention did now, looked at his feet.

They were flat. Weight in the middle, heels down, nothing committed, at a party, holding a plate. He had spent a week learning to see people settle onto a front foot before they moved, and Julian Cross was not settled onto anything while sitting down.

"You could have let him," Kade said.

"I could have." Julian moved the plate so Kade could have the other arm, and Kade took it. "Reid does that to people he expects to pass him. He's got good eyes. He isn't wrong about who's coming, he's early, so he goes and says it where nobody writes it down." He looked at Kade properly for the first time. "I'd rather find out what you are on a floor, with a record. Not hear him guess it on a roof."

"Why would you care what I am."

"Because a hundred and eighty-three has stopped scaring him, and I'd like something to." Julian said it the way a man states a preference in food. "People who've finished improving are restful to be around and bad for me. Nobody's coming for it this year. I'd like to keep not being sure of that."

Kade laughed before he had decided to, which he had not done in front of a stranger in some time. "That's the most selfish decent thing anybody's done for me."

"Good. Selfish is reliable." Julian held out the plate. "They're excellent."

Kade took one. It was.

"How do you know how long Reid's been where he is?"

"I know where everyone above two hundred has been for a year. It's the job."

A second-year came around the heaters with the look of somebody carrying a message she would rather have put down. "Cross. House office has your father holding. Second time tonight."

Something went through Julian, all at once and everywhere. Not fear. A tightening, the whole frame of him, and then control laid over it so fast that anybody who had not spent his life reading adults' faces for the temperature of a room would have missed it. Kade missed nothing and looked at his dumpling.

"Tell him I'm at a House event," Julian said. Then, before she had turned: "No. Tell him I'll call at eleven. Eleven exactly. Thank you."

She went. Julian looked at the plate a moment as if it had changed.

"Family," he said.

"Mine reads my medical chart before I've called her."

"Mine reads the ladder." He said it lightly, and it did not land lightly, and Kade revised something. Then Julian stood and lifted the plate a few centimeters, which was apparently how he said goodbye.

"Four thousand, Mercer. Don't make me look stupid."

"Which way?"

"I don't care which way." He went.

Across the roof, Taren had climbed onto the greenhouse step with both arms up and was saying the word relay in a voice built to carry.

---

"Settle the Table," Taren said, when enough people had turned. "No fields. Two tables, two teams, four corners each. West heater to the greenhouse terrace, around the mast, not through it. Set it down on the terrace with the cup still full. Spill, you lift and set again. First table down with a full cup wins."

"What cup," Sera said.

Taren held one up, full to the brim, and placed it on the center of the long table with great ceremony.

"That's the rank table."

"It's on the roof. Everything on the roof is equipment. Rule."

"That's not a rule."

"It is now."

Teams formed the way Meridian formed anything, by proximity and accusation, and Kade found himself at the rear corner of the long table with Mara on the front corner diagonal from him, Nessa on the front on his side, and Eli across the back, looking at the cup as if it had wronged him personally. The second table was a folding one from the food line, with Anton and Dev at the front and Cal Reid and Mina at the back.

Kade read the course. The mast turn was the problem. Going around a concrete base, the inside corner stopped while the outside corner ran, and whoever held the outside rear would run farthest with the heaviest swing, and that was him. Then the terrace, three steps up, where the front pair climbed first and the table tilted back toward the cup sliding at whoever was there.

"Switch me to the front," he said to Mara. "I've got the long swing."

"You've got the long swing because you're the one who can take it." She had her hands on her corner and her eyes on the mast. "Front's mine. At the step I go up first and call it. Nessa, you're on the lip side. Navarro, you watch the cup and say which way it's going before it goes."

"That's my whole role? Watching water?"

"It's the only thing on this table that can lose." She still did not look back. "Mercer. Don't plan the set-down. You'll want to. Decide when you're there."

Taren raised both arms. "All appeals go to me, and I'm biased. Go."

The table came up and the cup rocked and held, and the first ten meters were nothing but weight and roof. Anton's team had the lighter table and led to the mast. Then the mast.

Mara took the inside front corner and did the thing he had watched her do in Bay One for a week: she stopped. All the way. Heel down, weight nowhere, and the table swung around her like a door on a hinge, and Kade on the outside rear was suddenly running with the whole weight of it in his hands, and had a choice about his feet with no time to make it. So he did what she had taught him and made it before he planted. Right foot outside. Then left. The swing finished, the table pointed at the greenhouse, and the cup had run to one edge and stopped.

"Toward Kade," Eli said. "Was. Not now."

The steps. Mara and Nessa went up together, one, two, three, and the front of the table went up and the back stayed down, and a slope opened under the cup and it started toward him like a thing that had been waiting all night.

"Back. Fast," Eli said.

"Down," Nessa called from the lip. "Set it. Set."

He could have set it. Both front corners were on the terrace, the table was level enough to put down, Nessa had called it, the cup was still moving, and the other table was coming up beside them with Anton already saying down. Every part of him that had once accepted a fight at night after fighting at noon wanted to slam it and win.

He held. Rear heel down, weight not spent, the decision open. The cup came to the edge of the slope he was making and stopped when he lifted his end a hand's breadth, and he let the cup choose his angle, then stepped up onto the terrace and set the table down on all four iron feet with the cup in the middle and the water inside it.

Anton's team had set half a second before. Beside their cup there was a dark ring on the wood.

"Spill," Mara said.

"Quarter inch," Taren said, bending to look. "Quarter inch is a splash. Splash isn't a spill. Rule. Official result."

"That's not a rule," Sera said.

"It is now."

Kade stood on the terrace with his hands still open from the table. He could feel the argument lined up. The ring on the wood. Nessa's call. The half second. Each sentence ready, the way sentences had been ready for Cal an hour ago. He looked at the full cup in the middle of the rank table, sitting where he had set it down, and let the sentences go where they went when nobody used them.

"Fine," he said.

Nessa said, "I called down."

"The cup was going."

She looked at the cup, and at him, and nodded once, which from Nessa was a paragraph.

Mara came past his corner on her way to the water jug. "You held at the step."

"We lost."

"You were going to lose the cup and win the race and I'd have had to watch it." She filled her own cup and drank. "Good hold. Bay One, usual hour."

Then half the roof was arguing about what constituted a spill, and for twenty minutes nobody said his number or Zero or Shah or the yellow line, and he did not notice the absence until somebody said the word exam and he realized he had been not noticing for a while. That was new. He did not know what to do with it, so he ate.

*Twenty-four. Mom says you didn't call at twenty hundred and she didn't mind. She says there was a party and you were at it. I asked how she knew you were at it and not standing near it. She said she could hear a roof.*

---

He found Mara later in the greenhouse, when the music had gone quieter on its own and the roof had come apart into groups.

He had been looking for somewhere the wind was not. The greenhouse door stood open a hand's breadth with warm air coming out, and she was inside with her back to him, reading the climate panel beside the pipe as if it were an orientation document.

"You've stood in that door long enough to lose the heat," she said without turning. "In or out."

He came in. The glass was black on all sides with the roof moving beyond it in orange and white, and inside it smelled of wet soil and warm metal.

"You wrote a rank under the table," she said, turning.

"You watched."

"I watched Patel. You were attached to the other end." She leaned back against the bench. "I didn't. My first month. I wrote my name and nothing." Her voice did not change, and he was learning that her voice kept the things that mattered exactly where it kept everything else. "Everybody else gets to put a line through theirs. Seven has nowhere to go that anyone celebrates. If I move it's down, or it's one place up, and either way somebody has an opinion about whose number it was to begin with."

"Whose would it be."

"My father had my entry rank before I'd seen it. He called to tell me seven was acceptable." She said acceptable very precisely. "He likes systems. He thinks a system is honest when it agrees with him."

"And you."

"I like knowing the number's mine." She looked at him. "Why did you write yours."

He almost said the easy thing. He could feel it, bright and ready, and exactly how she would let him have it and what that would cost. He thought about his mother on the bench outside the ambulance bay, counting the times he told the thing.

"Because at Northline I made sure every number was mine," he said. "By making them bad on purpose. Didn't turn things in. Left class. If I'd already quit before anybody could tell me to, then whatever came after was something I'd decided." He looked at the black glass. "Eight thousand one hundred fifty-nine is the first number I've had in years that I didn't arrange. I wanted it somewhere I'd put it myself."

It was too exposed the moment it existed. He reached for the joke.

"You climbed down so nobody could push you off," Mara said.

He stopped reaching. "Yes."

"That's a great deal of work to be safe from a thing that hadn't happened yet."

He laughed, not because it was funny but because it was accurate, the way a step is accurate when it arrives where the weight was going. "It was a great deal of work."

The corner of her mouth moved. She looked at the pipe instead of at him. "I'm here so the seven is mine. You're here so the eight thousand is. We're doing the same thing from different ends."

"That isn't what you said in Bay One."

"In Bay One you plant before you've chosen. Tonight you chose." She picked up her cup. "Don't get fond of it. It was one step."

Through the glass, out on the roof, the ribbon line sagged all at once and came down across the food, and the lights stayed lit, so that the long table was suddenly wearing a string of white stars, and Anton was already going up the ladder, and holding it, with a face of complete seriousness, was the first-ranked student at Asterion Academy.

Kade laughed. Mara laughed, at the same instant, and they both heard the timing of it. He noticed. She noticed him noticing, and her face reset in the time it took the ladder to stop swaying.

"Usual hour, Mercer."

"Usual hour."

She went out into the cold and did not look back, and he no longer heard that as dislike.

---

Taren was in the greenhouse door thirty seconds after she left, which should not have been possible from where he had been.

"She left faster than she usually leaves things."

"It's a greenhouse. It's warm. People leave."

"She left you." Taren leaned on the frame, pleased with himself. "You did one thing tonight instead of three. Usually you do three so nobody can see which one's honest. Everybody saw this one." He tipped his cup at Kade. "Hale ran your review at five forty in the dark, didn't he. That's Hale all over. He thinks a number's more honest if nobody's awake for it."

The review had been Hale, Park, and Kade, on a ramp, before dawn, and it had not been on any board.

"Who told you that?"

"Hale's loud. Park isn't, but Hale is." Taren's wrist lit. He glanced down, turned the band face-in against his forearm before Kade's eyes reached it, and looked up again with his face exactly where it had been. "Laundry."

"At midnight."

"Meridian's machines run when they run." Smooth. Too smooth to mean anything yet, and Kade let it go the way you let go of a thing you mean to come back for, and put it in the file beside the note about nine thousand one hundred twelve wanting his audience, and beside *I sit where people talk*.

"Go help Bell with the lights," Taren said. "Cross is holding the ladder and it's upsetting people."

---

The House lights went down at midnight because a timer believed everyone was asleep, and the roof lowered its voice. The ribbon went back up on the twelfth screw. Couches went down the tower stair. The table stayed, by a rule Dev had just made.

He meant to go down. The instinct was there, the one that had stood at the top of the stairs all night with a hand on the rail: the roof was full of people, and every one of them was something that could be taken away, and the time to leave was before that became a problem. Knowing the instinct did not make it go anywhere. It gave him a choice, and he could hold the choice the way he had held the table at the step. Heel down. Weight not spent.

He went and got under the table.

The roof was cold through his jacket and the names were a ceiling. He read them the way Dev had said to, not the ranks, the other part. CHALLENGED SIX HUNDRED. WOKE UP IN MEDICAL. WRONG SEMINAR. STAYED ANYWAY. FAILED CONDUIT THEORY TWICE, above a rank struck twice and now sitting in the three hundreds. LEFT TWICE. CAME BACK TWICE.

His own line was above his face, fresh enough to smell of marker. Eight thousand one hundred fifty-nine, and nothing beside it, and it looked lonelier than it had in the afternoon.

Shoes arrived beside his head. Eli's, by the state of them. "Are you all right?"

"Reading."

"Under a table."

"Where the book is." He held up his hand without looking, and after a moment Dev's marker was put into it, because Eli had apparently been carrying it, because of course he had.

He thought about what he had been bad at. A fall Hale had had to teach him at seventeen. Mira Chen, in sixty-four seconds, because everybody with a tablet had said no and he had heard them. Every one was true, and he wrote none of them.

Under the number he wrote, in his own bad capitals, HELD AT THE STEP.

It was not a failure and it was not a rank. Somebody crawling under this table in ten years would not know what it meant. It was a thing he had done on a roof with his hands full, not arranged in advance, and he wanted it next to the number so the number would not be alone.

"What does it say," Eli asked, from above.

"Nothing useful."

"Can I put it in the model?"

"No."

Eli's shoes stayed. Somebody put the music on low. Taren's voice said something about the bell, and Sera's said stop counting, and Taren's said when it rings, and Kade lay under a ceiling of other people's worst months and found he had not checked the stair in an hour. He did not check it now. He had mathematics at eight, and the stair would be there in the morning.

He stayed until the roof was nearly empty, and then a little after that.

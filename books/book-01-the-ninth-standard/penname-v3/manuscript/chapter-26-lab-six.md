# Chapter 26 — Lab Six

The transit board said nine minutes, and Eli said he only needed six.

"For what?" said Sera.

"To get my project off a network that strangers have apparently been reading since autumn." He already had his lab card out, a scuffed white rectangle on an orange lanyard. "Lab Five is forty meters that way. You can all stand there and watch me do it."

Sera looked down the engineering hall. It ran the length of the east wing, wide and polished, with laboratory glass down both sides, and most of the glass was dark. Midyear projects had closed out two days before. Tonight the wing belonged to a cleaning drone, three or four lit rooms, and whoever had a deadline the calendar did not care about.

"Holt stays in the doorway," she said. "Where I can see his hands and the hall cameras can see the rest of him."

"That's where I was going to stand," said Taren.

"Then we agree about something."

Nobody laughed. In October somebody would have.

Lab Five smelled of flux and hot dust. Eli's bench was in the back corner, behind a pair of sawhorses with a timber beam laid across them. The beam was as thick as Kade's thigh. Somebody had stacked four iron plates from the conditioning hall on the middle of it.

"Tool roll," Eli said. He was already in his chair, working the black tablet with the cracked corner out of its dock. "The orange one. It's under the horses, right at the back, and my backup wafer's in it. Go round the box, not over it."

The box sat on the floor beneath the beam. It was gray, about the size of a lunch tin, with a carry loop on one end and a stubby orange tab standing up out of the lid like a thumb. Kade got down and went in under the beam on one elbow. The roll had been shoved a long way back. He had to put his head and shoulders under the plates to reach it, and he hooked two fingers through the strap and dragged it toward him.

"Holding," said a small flat voice beside his ear. "Eleven minutes."

He looked at the box. Then he looked up.

There was a saw cut in the beam, directly over the back of his neck. It went all the way through. He could see the ceiling lights through it in a bright line no thicker than a playing card, and the four plates were sitting squarely on top of the line.

He came out backward with the roll. He did not hurry, because hurrying seemed like a bad thing to do near it.

"Eli. Your beam is in two pieces."

"It's been in two pieces since Tuesday." Eli put out a hand for the roll without turning round. "You heard it. Eleven minutes."

"Whose head was under what?" said Sera.

Kade handed the roll over. His pulse arrived afterward, late and rather loud. He checked ceilings. He checked the exits of rooms he had sat in a hundred times, and he looked twice at any chair somebody else had pulled out for him. He had just crawled under eighty kilos of iron because Eli had told him which side of a box to go round, and he had not looked up first. There were not many people whose word could stand in for looking. He had not known until now that Eli was one of them.

"It's one file," Sera said. "You could have pulled it in the morning."

"It's the fellowship file. My panel is on Friday. If somebody has been reading that design since autumn, they are not getting this week's revisions as well. And I am not leaving the only good copy where they can reach it, three days before I stand up in front of five faculty members and defend it." Eli fished a wafer the size of a coin out of the roll and seated it in the tablet. Then he took the tablet off the lab network and opened a plain gray window Kade had seen once before, over his shoulder, at two in the morning. It held a short list of channels. Most of them had sensible names. The last one said only NULL-7.

"What's that one?"

"Null seven. It's a maintenance channel. I registered it last term so the box could report to my desk from across the wing, and then I gave the box its own radio and never went back." He started the copy, and a bar began to crawl across the gray. "It is completely legitimate. There's a ticket for it, from March. Nobody ever closed the ticket, which is the only reason the channel still exists. Nothing logs it and nothing listens to it. It's a dead-letter box. An hour ago that was me being untidy. Tonight it's the one wire in this building I know the far end of."

"Your security plan is that you forgot something," said Sera.

"My security plan is that everybody did."

Mara had crouched by the sawhorses with her hands on her knees. She was not looking at the cut. She was looking at the orange tab on the lid. "There's a speaker and a display on this. What is the tab for?"

"The tab's the only part I'd swear to." Eli swung the chair round. He had been pale and quick-fingered since the archive, and Kade watched the color come back into him as he talked. "There's no software in it. It's a spring and a latch, and the field itself holds the latch. While the field is up, the tab is up. If the field drops, the tab drops, and it doesn't consult anybody first. A display will show you whatever it's been told to. I wanted one thing on there that couldn't be told."

"You built a field stabilizer that doesn't trust its own screen."

"I built one that takes three readings and believes the worst. It's a pessimist. It gets that from my mother."

"What does it cost?"

"Less than a used car."

"Which used car?"

"A bad one. A really regrettable one. That's the whole design brief, Mara. Asterion has rooms full of stabilizers that are better than this. They each cost what a rural clinic spends in a year, and they need a certified operator standing beside them. This is for one nurse on a night shift with a ceiling coming down over a ward. She carries it down the stairs by herself and she puts it on the floor, and it holds what it can and tells her the truth about how long."

Sera had come round the bench while he talked. She looked at the box for a moment, the way she looked at opponents. Then she kicked it.

It was not a hard kick. It was the side of her boot, the sort of knock a person gives a thing on the floor when they are carrying one end of a stretcher and watching the other end. The box skidded a hand's width and rocked.

"Shock," it said. "Three minutes. Holding."

Eli made a noise like a kettle. "That is a prototype."

"It's on the floor of a collapse. Somebody is going to fall over it in the first minute. I wanted to see what it did." She looked up at the cut, and at the tab, which was still standing. "Why three?"

"Because it can't know what the knock did to it. It could be fine. It could have a cracked cell and be about to lie to you with total confidence. So it throws away whatever time it thought it had, and promises only what it can deliver on its reserve, and it won't offer you the rest again until a person has picked it up and looked at it." He was out of the chair and on his knees with a steel jack stand from under the bench. "Kade. Other side. Hold the post straight while I wind it."

Kade held the post. Eli wound the screw until the saddle kissed the underside of the beam beside the cut, and only then did he lift the box, turn it over, thumb two catches, and set it down again.

"Holding. Ten minutes."

"And at zero?" said Sera.

"It doesn't switch off. It lets the load down the way you'd lower a piano, and it says that it's doing it, and the tab goes over."

"Good. It's counting for the wrong person, though."

Eli stopped with his hand on the stand. "Go on."

"Whoever's under that beam can't do anything with a number. They're pinned or they're unconscious. The one who can use it is me, and I've got a helmet on and a radio in one ear and somebody screaming in the other. I'm not going to do arithmetic. There's exactly one thing I need from your box." She held up a finger. "Is there time to go in once more, and come out again? Yes or no. If it's no, I need it told to me early enough that I can hear it and still hate it on the right side of the door."

"A go and a no-go." Eli had found a stylus somewhere and was writing on the back of his own hand. "It would need to know how long a trip takes."

"So you tell it when you set it down. One trip is ninety seconds, or four minutes, or whatever this building is. It does the sum so I don't have to."

"And it has to be wrong in the right direction."

"It always has to be wrong in the right direction. Send me that logic before Friday. I'll mark every place it gets somebody killed."

"I'm going to take that as enthusiasm."

"Take it as a deadline."

He beamed at her. It was the first time all evening that anybody in the group had looked happy, and it was about a gray box and a list of ways to be wrong. He turned in his chair toward the door, still grinning, the way he had turned a hundred times since October. "Taren, tell her that people don't usually volunteer to—"

He stopped.

Taren was exactly where Sera had put him, with his hands in view on the door frame. He had not laughed at the kick, or told Eli which faculty member on the panel could be flattered, or said anything at all. "She's right about the door," he said, carefully, and left it there.

Eli turned back to the bench. After a moment he rubbed at the ink on his hand, found that it was not coming off, and copied it into his notes instead.

The copy finished. Eli glanced at the gray window to check it, and did not look away again.

---

"That's wrong," he said.

Sera was beside him at once. "How wrong?"

"It isn't broken wrong. It's watched wrong." He dragged a second window up beside the first. "Look. Null seven is quiet. It's supposed to be, because nobody knows it exists. But this is the live maintenance traffic for the wing. I've had phantom pings out of Lab Six on my list for weeks. I thought it was an orphan, some old service program asking whether the room still existed. Half this island is new systems pretending the old ones are dead."

"Weeks," said Kade.

"It was item eleven of eighteen, Kade, and it was the dull one." His fingers were moving faster than his mouth, which was rare. "But I've never had the two side by side before. Every status report Lab Six sends goes out twice. One copy goes to campus monitoring, where it belongs. The other goes to an address that isn't on this campus."

"It's a mirror," Taren said from the door. His hands had gone behind his back. "I don't know that one. I know the kind. If you can get inside a facility, you don't need to control it. You only need to be told what state it's in."

"What for?" said Mara.

Taren did not answer quickly enough, and Eli answered instead, without meaning to.

"Oh, no."

A line on the live window had changed color. "Lower coolant return valve reports closed," Eli read. "Reports. Closed." He pulled up a second trace, a thin line climbing at a steady angle. "And that's the pressure behind it, which says the valve is a liar."

He was out of the chair. They followed him into the hall and twenty meters along it to the longest window in the wing.

Kade knew Lab Six mostly from Eli's complaints. It housed the thermal-transfer hardware and the coolant plant, and an experimental Conduit test bed so old that replacing it would have cost enough to make the administration discover a love of heritage. Eli called it the museum that could explode.

The museum had two levels. Behind the glass lay the teaching floor, with worktables and tool cages, and a control booth built into the far corner like a ticket office. The middle of the floor was open. Yellow stairs went down on either side of the rig into the lower chamber, where fat shielded pipes ran between the coolant tanks. On the west side was a pressure door wide enough for equipment carts. On the east, behind a steel wall, ran the service spine. Its hall entrance was a narrow gray door a few steps from where Kade stood.

Three people were still working. Two students in gray lab coats were lowering a transfer ring from the ceiling on a winch. Below them, under the rig, a technician knelt with his back to everyone and ear protectors clamped over his head. One of the students saw Eli and raised a hand.

"They machined my housing," Eli said, and hit the wall intercom. "Lab Six, this is Navarro in the hall. Your lower return valve is open and your pressure is climbing. Clear the floor."

Her voice came back thin. "Board says closed."

"Your board is wrong. Look at me." He held the tablet flat against the glass with the rising line on it. "West door. Go now."

They went at once, which Kade noticed. People who had machined a housing for Eli apparently believed him when he raised his voice. The taller student reached the west door and hit the release. Nothing happened. She found the manual wheel beside the frame and threw her whole weight on it, and then her partner's weight as well, and the wheel did not give a finger's width.

Her partner was leaning over the stair rail and beating on it with a wrench. Below, the technician finally looked up. He pulled the ear protectors down around his neck, saw two students shouting and a row of faces at the glass, and ran for the east stair. He was four steps from it when the light in the lower chamber turned from white to blue.

A shutter dropped across the foot of the stair. He hit it with both hands.

"What was that?" said Kade.

"Automatic stabilization field." Eli's voice had gone flat. "The system thinks the coolant is out of tolerance, so it's holding the coolant steady. The stairs become part of the seal."

"Does the field protect him?"

"It protects the coolant."

Sera had already slapped the alarm plate by the door twice. It was dead. "Local alarm's cut."

"It runs on the lab's network," said Taren. He had come up the hall and stopped two paces short of them. "The building alarm doesn't. It's at the central junction and it's a pull handle. It's mechanical, and it doesn't ask who you are."

Sera looked at him for one second. "Mara goes with you and watches you pull it. Handle and hazard code, nothing else."

"Nothing else," said Taren, and the two of them ran west. Kade had never seen Taren Holt run flat out.

Eli was back on the intercom. "Leave the door. Get up to the booth. It has its own seal."

The student pointed down through the floor.

"I know he is," Eli said. His mouth worked. "You can't help him from up there. Go."

They went. Kade saw what it had cost Eli to say it, and that he had said it anyway.

Eli looked at the tablet. The figure on it changed twice while Kade watched, and then the tablet stopped offering one.

"The chamber's going to vent," Eli said. "That part's decided. What isn't decided is where. There's an upper vent, up through the teaching floor. If the lower chamber goes up through it, it takes the booth and the glass and this hall with it. If the upper vent's shut, the release stays below and the building holds." He tried the command. The tablet refused it, and refused it again. "The controller's rejecting me. Whoever's listening has their hands on the remote side. But there's a local service control on the east wall, off the spine. It's a panel and a wheel. It doesn't take remote commands, because it was built before anybody thought of them."

He had the white card in his hand. He was already looking at the gray door.

"Let the response crews do it," said Kade.

"They aren't here. Two minutes, maybe less."

"Then I'm coming with you."

"The spine is one person wide and you can't read the board. You'd stand behind me and be brave at my neck." Eli almost smiled. He was frightened, and the fear was all over his face, with a decision sitting on top of it. "Kade. Ten minutes ago you put your neck under my beam and you didn't look up first. Do that again."

Kade had hold of the edge of the gray door. He did not remember taking it.

He could think of several sentences, and every one of them began with *don't*. Eli was not asking permission. He had stopped to tell Kade because Kade was his friend, and he had about four seconds to spend on it.

Sera spoke before Kade did. "I can't order you in and I won't order you out. What do you need from this side?"

Eli looked grateful enough to cry. "Keep the hall empty. When the crews come, tell them the panels lie. Every board in there will say whatever somebody wants it to. Spine C is the run that doesn't share a wall with the chamber. Tell them C."

"Spine C," said Sera. "Go."

Kade let go of the door.

Eli put the card to the plate and the door opened on a strip of raw concrete and cable trays. He turned sideways to fit the tablet through. Then he was gone, and the door swung to behind him. The building alarm began to sound from one end of the east wing to the other.

---

There was a speaker grille beside the gray door. Kade had taken it for part of the wall until Eli's voice came out of it.

"Spine's clear. It's also filthy. Somebody tell Facilities." He was breathing hard. "Forty meters. I can see the panel."

Kade pressed the key under the grille. "We hear you."

"Good. Talk to me, or I'll talk to myself. I'm sending the booth some directions while I walk. Their screen's still mine."

Through the glass the lower chamber was blue and very still. The technician had backed against the shutter with his arm over his face.

The blue went white.

There was no fire. The long window turned the color of milk from one end to the other, all at once, every square of it crazed and none of it falling. The sound came through the floor and up Kade's shins. Down the hall a row of steel shutters dropped out of the ceiling, one after another. The gray door jumped in its frame and four bolts went home inside it. A strip above the handle lit red. ISOLATED.

Kade's ears were ringing. He had his hands flat on the door.

The cold line drew itself behind his eyes before he had decided anything.

He had never reached on purpose with so much to reach into. He felt the door and its frame, and the steel wall the frame was set in. Past the wall there was pressure, a great patient weight of it, leaning outward in every direction. He could feel where it went. It ran from the chamber into the spine wall, and from the wall into this frame, and from the frame into the shutter line that ran the length of the hall. There were routes. He could feel one opening, the way one opened on a mat when an opponent's weight came forward. He could change where that push came to rest, and the bolts would have nothing left to hold against.

But it would have to rest somewhere. He looked for the place, and the two he found were a window that had already broken without falling and the floor of a hall. Doors were opening all along that hall. He could hear them.

"Door's sealed behind me," said the grille. "Which is rude, but correct. Kade, if you're standing there thinking about it, that door is what's between the chamber and the hall. Leave it shut."

Kade took a breath that hurt.

"Mercer." Sera had not come to take his wrist. She was ten meters away, turning a coughing teaching assistant round by the shoulders, and she had not even looked at him. "I need hands."

Every part of him wanted the power to mean that he never again had to stand in a corridor while somebody else took the risk. He looked at that wish properly. It was not about Eli. It was about not being able to bear this, and it would have spent a hallway of strangers to make the feeling stop.

He took his hands off the door.

Letting go was not free. A bright wire of pain stayed behind his right eye. The ceiling lights had each grown a second edge. When he looked down his fingers were trembling, and he had only reached. He had not moved anything.

"I'm here," he said to the grille. "I'm not touching it."

"Good. You're very gifted at not touching things. I've always said so." Something clanged. "Panel's live. The upper vent's manual. It's a wheel. Of course it's a wheel, everything in this museum is a wheel. It's stiff. It's turning. Kade, tell Sera, spine C, they have to come by—"

The floor kicked. The grille gave one flat tone, like a held note on a cheap keyboard, and then nothing came out of it at all.

Kade held the key down. "Eli."

Behind the milk-white glass, gray was rising.

"Eli."

He said it four times. Then he went to be Sera's hands, because he had promised to.

---

The hall did its best to become a second disaster before anyone came.

The alarm had emptied a night fabrication shift and two study rooms into it at once. The shutters had closed the north end, so everyone who came out turned the same way and found themselves walking toward a broken window with smoke behind it. A thin gray haze was coming down from the ceiling vents as well. It smelled sweet and chemical, and people were starting to cough.

Sera did not shout at the crowd. She walked into the front of it with her arms out wide and stopped it.

"East wing is closed. You are going to the central stair. What room are you from? How many were in it?" She had a marker from somewhere, and as they answered she wrote on the inside of her own forearm. It was a room number and a count, then the next one. "Fab Two, six. Are all six of you here? Point at them. Good. Go."

Mara and Taren came back at a run. Mara's eyes went to the glass, and to the gray door with its red strip, and to Kade. He shook his head. She did not ask him anything. She went to the emergency plan on the wall and started reading it.

"Not the north stair," Taren said, to Sera and nobody else. "On a building alarm the central stair pressurizes and keeps the smoke out. North doesn't. It turns into a chimney."

"Vey?"

Mara ran a finger down the posted plan. "He's right. It's printed here, in small type."

"Central only," said Sera, louder. "You heard that."

The haze was getting thicker. Kade found where it was coming in, a ceiling return above the Lab Four door, and under it a recess in the wall with a red wheel marked SMOKE DAMPER, MANUAL. He got both hands on it. It was stiff with years of paint. He pulled, and his shaking fingers skated off the rim and he barked his knuckles on the recess.

He set his hands again. A second pair closed on the far side of the wheel.

Taren had not touched it yet. His hands hovered. "Sera. Can I take the other side of this?"

She glanced over for half a second. "Go."

They hauled together. The paint cracked and the wheel came round a hand's width, then another. Neither of them spoke. Kade could feel Taren's weight through the iron, going in the same direction as his own, and there was nothing he could do with that tonight.

"Quarter turn more," Taren said. "There's a detent. You'll feel it drop."

It dropped. Above them the haze stopped coming.

"Response map?" said Kade.

"Response map."

Taren took his hands off the wheel and held them up and open, where Sera could see them, and went back to the wall.

Kade swept the two study rooms because Sera pointed at them and said his name. He looked under every table. In the second a cup was rolling in a slow circle on the floor, and nobody was there. On the way out he passed the grille and pressed the key again, and got nothing, and kept walking.

Then the hall was full of people in sealed armor, and Sera was in among them with her forearm turned out like a slate.

She gave it to them in the order they needed it. There were three people in Lab Six, two in the teaching-floor booth and one in the lower chamber behind a field shutter. A fourth had gone in by the service spine four minutes ago to close the upper vent by hand. Nineteen had come out of the rest of the wing, all counted at the stair. "And don't believe anything that lab reports about itself. The boards were showing a closed valve while the pressure climbed. Somebody outside has the remote side. Go in by spine C. It doesn't share a wall with the chamber."

The officer with the map looked at her arm, and then at her. "Whose count is this?"

"Mine. Vale, Meridian, first year."

"You're a student."

"Tonight I am."

He sent his team in by spine C.

Somebody put a casualty slate into Kade's hands. "You know the one who went in? Fill in what you can."

The form wanted a name, and he gave it. It wanted next of kin. Kade knew that Eli's mother labeled leftovers with threats. He knew she thought compartmentalizing was either a life skill or a future therapy bill, depending on the invoice. He knew the three worst songs her son loved and the face he made when code compiled the first time. He did not know her name. He stood looking at the empty box until Mara reached round him and entered Eli's student number, and the record filled itself in.

Under equipment he wrote: *black tablet, cracked upper left corner. Orange tool roll. White lab card, orange lanyard.* The slate had no box for *talks when he's frightened*. He would have filled that one in first.

Mara stayed beside him. She did not touch him, and she did not leave.

When the hall had steadied she took the antistatic sleeve out of her inside pocket. Sera brought an evidence bag from the responders' crate. The sleeve went into the bag, and Mara signed across the seal and Sera signed under her. On the tag Mara wrote that it was for Director Sen's hand and no other.

"Five of us were going to give her that," Mara said. It was the only thing she said about it.

Across the hall Taren sat down on the floor with his back to the wall, by himself. Kade had never seen him choose that when there were people to sit with.

---

The students from the booth came out first. One walked between two responders with a mask on his face. The other came on a rigid litter under a clear hood, and her hands were still hooked as though they held a wheel.

The walking one dragged his mask down when he saw their faces. "Navarro?"

"Still inside," a medic said.

"He got us the booth seal. The intercom died, and then the booth screen started typing at us. It said where to sit and when to get below the window line." He coughed until he bent over. "It said not to wait for the lower chamber. It said it twice. The second time it said *please*."

The medic put the mask back on him and steered Kade out of the way.

So Eli had been walking forty meters of filthy concrete toward a wheel, and had stopped to put two people somewhere they could live. He had not rushed at it blindly. He had been working the whole way.

Knowing it made hoping worse.

The technician came out much later. His gloves had frozen to the shape of his hands, and the medics were cutting them off as they wheeled him. Kade got alongside the litter.

"Navarro made east control," the man said through his hood. "His tag came up on the chamber board. Then the upper vent went to isolated. Manual."

"Did you see him?"

"I saw the board."

"And after that?"

"After that there were no cameras." A medic's hand came down on Kade's shoulder, not roughly. "That's everything he's got. Let him go."

The first team did not go back in. The joints of their armor were scored white where the field had tried to hold metal and coolant in the same state. A second team went down spine C, and the map on the commander's stand began to change color behind them. East control went red and the spine went yellow. The lower chamber stayed black. Kade listened to the talk around the stand. He heard field persistence and thermal inversion and delamination and telemetry they could not trust. He understood each phrase, and he could not make any of them say *alive*.

Then the commander ordered the second team out.

Sera went straight to her. "They're thirty meters from that control."

"They're thirty meters from it through a chamber my instruments can't read." The commander did not argue any further. She held out the suit slate. "Four people, Vale. Those are their seal numbers. Tell me which one you'd send on."

Sera looked. Kade watched her go down the column, once and then again more slowly, and he watched her want one of the figures to be different.

"None of them," she said.

"Say why."

"Because then you'd be looking for five." Her jaw was tight enough to hurt. "First priority is not to make more casualties."

"Then bring them home. They know your voice. You briefed them."

Sera took the comm. Her hand closed on it, and she made it open again. "Team Two, this is the hall. Confirm your way out."

"Spine C. Ninety seconds."

She shut her eyes once. "Your way out is clear."

So that was response work too. Part of it was knowing where to stop sending people. Kade had done the same sum at a gray door an hour before. He hated it just as much when someone else did it.

The last man out had a helmet scorched down one side. Kade knew what he would say before he took it off.

"Lower lab's gone. The chamber's flooded with field-stabilized coolant. Thermal's useless in it."

"Navarro?" said Mara.

He checked his list. "Not located."

It was two words, and neither of them was *dead*.

"And the upper vent?" said Sera.

"Shut. It was shut by hand, at the wheel, before the chamber failed." The helmet turned toward the milk-white window. "If it had been open, I don't think we'd be standing on this floor. Whoever did it bought the whole level."

"It was Eli," Kade said.

"We don't know who it was."

"I was listening to him do it."

The man did not argue. "Then he did it right." He turned to the map. "We can't put a search into that chamber until the field decays."

"Is that hours?"

"It could be."

"Days?"

He took a moment before he answered. Kade understood what the delay meant before the man spoke. "It could be."

Kade looked at the black square. Somewhere inside it a status line read UPPER VENT ISOLATED, MANUAL. That was the last thing in the building that Eli had certainly done, and it sat right at the edge of everything nobody could see.

On her way past, the commander stopped in front of Sera. "Response assessment office opens at eight. When this is over, walk in and tell them the east wing incident commander sent you. They'll know what it means."

Sera looked at her as if she had spoken another language. "This isn't the time."

"It never is. Go anyway."

---

Meridian filled up without anyone calling a meeting.

People who had barely known Eli came down to the common room and sat. Someone put the incident feed on the wall display, and someone else turned off the comments when strangers began arguing under it about sabotage. Priya ran the big kettle over and over for tea nobody drank. Anton Bell went floor by floor with a list, knocking on doors, and came back each time with the list longer and the names of people who should not be left alone underlined. Nessa sat at the end of the long table with her hood up and her eyes shut, which was how she listened to a room that was too loud for her. When the feed changed she was the one who said so. Nobody was in charge. The work found people who could do it.

Kade stood by the display. Eli's bed was eight floors over his head in Room 814, and he could feel where it was like a point on a map.

A family liaison from Student Services came with an administrator, and they asked for Eli's roommate. They took him into the strategy room. Sera, Mara and Taren were being asked about timelines at the other end of the table. Julian Cross walked in behind them without being asked and leaned on the wall, and nobody had the nerve to send Rank One away.

The liaison was a gray-haired woman with a paper notebook. "I'm going to call his mother in the next half hour," she said. "She'll want to know what he was like in the last hour. The log can't tell me, but you can. I only want things you saw."

Kade opened his mouth, and hope came up first. *He might be fine. There's no body.* He heard how it would sound at the other end of a phone. It would sound like a promise, and he had no way to keep it. The dark sentence came up behind it, and that one was a guess as well.

He took a while, and she let him.

"He wasn't panicking," he said. "He was scared, and he was working. He was funny about it, because he talks when he's frightened. He got two people into a sealed room from inside a service tunnel, and he said please. Then he shut the vent by hand. The responders say that's why the building is still there. The last thing I heard him do was complain about a wheel."

She wrote all of it down. "And what shall I tell her he is?"

"Not located. It's what they said, and it's the only true thing." His voice had started to go, and he did not try to stop it. "Her name isn't anywhere I could find it tonight. When you call her, use his. Don't say *the student*."

"I will use his name," the liaison said. "Thank you. That was harder than the form."

At the other end of the table Sera was frowning at the search board the administrator had brought. Most of the wing was green.

"What does green mean?"

"Cleared."

"Cleared by whom?"

He checked. "West teaching floor, by sensor sweep."

"The sensors in that lab said a valve was shut while the pressure climbed." She said it without heat. "Make two colors. One for where a person has walked, and one for where a machine says it's empty. His mother is going to read that board. So is everyone in this House."

The administrator looked at the board for a long moment, and then he changed it. The green shrank to a thin line from the booth to spine C, with a hatched amber area around it and black beyond. It was less comforting, and it was more honest.

"When do you go back in?" Kade asked him.

"When the commander believes a person can go into that chamber without adding a name to the notice."

Kade wanted to hate him, and the man looked too tired for it to take.

The notice went up an hour later without a name, and forty minutes after that it vanished, which was worse. Mara put a cup of water in Kade's hand and sat down on the floor beside him with her shoulder against his. He drank the water because it was easier than arguing with her.

Then the casualty page changed, and Nessa said, "It's up," and the room went still.

Five injured. Two critical. One missing, presumed dead.

ELIAS NAVARRO.

Kade read the name until it stopped looking like language.

The notice explained itself in clean technical sentences. The collapse had breached the thermal chamber and flooded the lower level with a coolant built to stay stable under field stress. The property that had kept the release from being larger also made recovery impossible until the system cooled. Below that was one more line. INCIDENT STATUS: CONTAINED. EXTERNAL ACCESS UNDER INVESTIGATION.

Somewhere behind him a person began to cry. An upper-year Kade barely knew spoke over it, loudly enough for the whole room.

"External access. Ask Holt about external access. Half the engineering wing saw you four walking him through it like a prisoner."

Every head turned. Taren was standing by the far wall, where he had stood all night. He did not defend himself. He looked at Kade, and then he looked at the floor, and he waited for whatever was coming.

Kade felt how easy it would be. He would not even have to speak. He could turn back to the display and let forty grieving people have somebody to be angry at. There would be a face to go with the hole in the evening. He knew the feeling, because he had felt it at the gray door. He wanted a way not to have to stand and wait.

"He pulled the building alarm," Kade said. His voice came out rough. "Mara watched him do it and read the log after. He told Sera which stair would turn into a chimney, and he was right, and nineteen people went down the other one. That's what he did tonight. What he did before tonight, the Director gets, with evidence, from all of us." He had to stop for breath. "I'm not saying he's my friend. I'm saying he didn't do this, and I'm not going to let us decide he did because it would feel better."

The upper-year looked away first. The room did not warm toward Taren, but it eased a little, and people stopped staring at him.

Taren came halfway across and stopped outside their circle. "There's a link under the notice. The evidence packets. I can't touch a terminal. But I can tell you what the routing format on that mirror will look like, and possibly which office issued it."

Kade's thumb was already over the link. He had not noticed it get there.

"Then write it down," Mara said. "On paper. It goes in the bag for Sen with everything else."

"The incident team might not know what they're reading."

"Then Sen will," said Kade. He moved his thumb away and closed the display. It took more effort than the wheel had. "I can't find him in a packet list. I'd just be running again, only indoors."

Taren nodded. Anton tore a sheet off the back of his list of names and held it out without a word, and Taren sat down at the end of the long table to write.

---

Kade could not go up, because up was Room 814. He went into the House kitchen instead, and found Rank One doing the washing up.

Julian had his sleeves rolled to the elbow. Forty cups stood on the counter. Most of them had never held anything but hot water, and he was washing them anyway, one at a time, and standing them upside down on a cloth in a straight line.

"Dry," he said, and threw Kade a towel.

Kade dried. His hands had nearly stopped shaking. The wire behind his eye had dulled to a headache he could work around.

"If I hadn't gone into that archive," he said to the cup in his hands.

"No."

"They knew what I'd found. Taren said so. They knew where we were tonight, because the doors told them. And a lab blew up while the five of us were standing in front of it. They picked their minute, Julian."

"Probably."

"Eli was there because of me."

"Eli was in that *hallway* because of you." Julian rinsed a cup and set it down exactly in line. "He was in that tunnel because of Eli. You told the room what he did. Did it sound to you like something you made him do?"

Kade said nothing.

"My father has a rule," Julian said. "Every failure belongs to the strongest person in the room. He says it at dinner, the way other people say grace. So I'll apply it, shall I? I'm Rank One. I was the strongest person on this island tonight, and I was in the library with a practice paper. By my father's rule Lab Six is mine. Does that sound right to you?"

"It sounds insane."

"It took me years to hear it that way, because it's also very flattering. If everything is your fault, then everything is yours." He turned the tap off. "And you can never hand another person a decision, because you've already taken all of them. You can answer for going into the archive. That one's yours. The wheel was his."

Kade thought of the bright line of the saw cut over his neck, and the small flat voice counting beside his ear. *Do that again.* He had let go of the door. It was the only thing he had done all night that he was sure of, and it had not saved anybody.

He knew Julian was right. He could feel himself knowing it. But the sum under it had not moved. If he had never gone looking for a name on a wall, Eli would be upstairs complaining about protein powder. Both things were true, and neither one would cancel the other.

"Eli would give them each a column," he said. "In a spreadsheet. He'd color-code it."

"He would," said Julian, and handed him another cup.

His phone went while he was drying it. It was his mother, and this time he answered.

"I'm all right," he said. "I'm not hurt. Eli's—" The word would not come. He tried twice.

"I saw the notice," said Elena. "You don't have to say it. You don't have to say anything. I'm on my break and I have eleven minutes. I'm going to stay on the line, and you can be quiet at me."

So he stood in the kitchen with the towel over his shoulder and the phone against his ear. He listened to a hospital corridor on the mainland, and she listened to him breathing. Near the end of it he said, "I love you," without making a joke of it first, and she said it back as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world.

Afterward he climbed as far as the eighth-floor landing, and that was all he could manage. He sat down on the top stair with the fire door shut at his back.

He opened his messages. The last one from Eli had come at lunch. *They moved my panel to Friday. If I die of nerves before then you may have my protein powder but NOT my soldering iron.* Kade had sent back a picture of a shovel.

He typed, *Spine C. They came in by spine C, like you said.* He looked at it for a long time, and did not send it, and did not delete it either.

Below him, somebody in the House rang the old bronze bell. It was not the victory peal. It was one low stroke, and it came up the stairwell through the concrete and went on up past him toward the roof.

Somewhere in the engineering network a channel called null seven was still open, because nobody had ever closed a ticket from March. Somewhere under the east wing a wheel had been turned by hand. And somewhere outside Asterion someone had listened to that lab for weeks, waiting, and had picked a minute when the five of them would be standing in front of the glass.

He had gone looking for one name in a sealed record, and he had found it. Tonight the casualty page had a second name on it. He sat on the stair with the door to his own room behind him and could not find any way of adding it up that did not begin with him.

He opened the model before sleep. Eli had already relabeled the axis in his small capitals, WHO'S BUYING. Kade added a row beneath Rusk's, no opponent in it, for the fight he had not taken this morning, and scored it honestly. ME.

---

He found version nine at one in the morning, because Eli was still awake with it and the glow was on the ceiling.

Twenty-three columns. Opponent tendencies. His own reaction time by hour. Failed approaches. Distance control. Fatigue. One called CROWD TAX. One called MERCER GETS CLEVER HERE, which Eli described as painfully predictive.

Then Eli did something he had not done before. He turned the tablet face down on the towel with the drone parts, and left his hand on it.

"There's one more. I want you to decide before you see it, not after. I can delete it without showing you."

Kade looked at the hand. "Show me."

TRANSFER OPPORTUNITIES. It sat apart from the others, with a heavier rule above it.

"The model predicts where force goes. Every fight, it guesses, then it checks itself. Mostly the guess is fine. In four of your fights it's wrong, by a little, in the same direction." Eli said it steadily. "I didn't go looking. It's a leftover. The model found it, and I didn't delete what the model found, and you should know it's there and that I know it's there."

Priya's fight first, the forty-one seconds Kade had told him to keep. Then three later bouts. Tiny moments. A stumble that traveled farther than a stumble should. A block that put an opponent sideways instead of back. A collision where Kade had felt less than the acceleration said he ought to. None strong enough to flag. None a room would have stopped.

All of them shared one thing, and he knew it before Eli said it. In each of those moments he had not been thinking about force at all. He had been thinking about what counted as connected to what.

"Do you want it gone?"

The question was real. Sen had forbidden him to try to reproduce the event. She had not forbidden anyone to notice anything. He had a week and a half of evidence about what he did when a thing he wanted came with a warning attached.

"Keep the observations."

Eli nodded.

"Don't build anything on them. No drill. No test. No standing me somewhere to see what happens."

"I won't."

"Promise."

Eli sighed, and looked at the column, and Kade watched him want it, the way he had wanted the six frames, and watched him put it down. "I promise."

Kade held the look one second longer and nodded. Trust, apparently, was partly asking somebody to stop before they had done anything wrong, and partly believing them when they said they had.

---

The review came two days later, where the gray line had put it, at five forty in the dark, because Hale had decided that was where it belonged.

He had the slate under his arm at the foot of the service road and did not explain. "Same loop. Go."

Kade went. Service road, the towers black overhead, the bridge with the wind coming through the rail, the breakwater throwing spray that found his collar, the ramp. He ran it with the ribs wrapped, and the ribs told him about every stride on the ramp. Park was at the top, by the bell court, and she had his wrist before he had stopped moving.

Hale walked up at his own pace and turned the slate around. Twelve thirty-three. Under it, from two weeks ago, twelve fifty-one. Under that, the median, eleven twenty, exactly where it had been.

"That's eighteen seconds," Kade said, when he could.

"That's eighteen seconds." Hale put the slate away. "Park."

"Fourteen days of steady. No event. No unsupervised field activity. No prohibited experiments." She looked at him over the tablet. "Cleared to continue. Same conditions. The field line stays where Sen put it."

He waited for the rest of the sentence, the part with until in it. Hale watched him wait, and understood.

"Median's still eleven twenty. It'll be there tomorrow."

"So will I."

"That's the review," Hale said, and went down the ramp toward whatever he did at five fifty in the morning.

That evening the board ran its weekly rerating, weighing every opponent by what they had done since. Larson had won twice more. Mills had climbed. Mason was three hundred higher again. Even the loss to Rusk had one clean point in it and no penalties. The number came up in the corner of Eli's model in the color the model used for facts.

8,971.

Ten days of ranked access. Fifteen hundred places and a little more. Nowhere near relevant. Nowhere near good. Room above him that did not need a miracle to reach, only stairs, and he already knew where the stairs were.

*Twenty-two. Mom read me the new one. She said it like a temperature coming down. I'm not going to explain that. You know how she sounded.*

---

Mara passed behind his chair at breakfast the way she had on the morning of the gray lines, and looked at his right hand where it lay by the tray.

He left it there.

"Still an answer," she said, and did not slow down, and it was a different answer, and they both knew which.

Eli watched her go and opened his mouth, and Kade pointed at him without looking, and he closed it.

At seven fifty-five Taren looked up through the glass and called it. "Fourteen months, twenty-eight days." Half the room groaned. Sera's napkin arrived where it always arrived.

On the way out Kade stopped at the panel by the Commons door. It had cycled to the ladder, and the drawn glass case was still there from two weeks ago, card and all. PLEASE DO NOT TOUCH THE EXHIBIT.

His line was not inside it. Another name was. A first-year he had never met, who had lost something this week to somebody, and had woken up this morning as the last square on the island with a museum drawn around it.

Two weeks ago he had stood here and agreed with the joke. It was funny, and it was true, and the ladder had it coming. From inside, it had not been a joke about the ladder. It had been a room with one window, and every hour of stairs since had been spent getting out of it, and none of those hours had known the case would still be here, waiting for the next person.

He put his thumb on the panel and dragged the case off, and the card went with it. What was left was a number the same height as the ten thousand others, with a band over it that a person could post into. Whoever the first-year was, they could find the stairs or not. They did not have to find them under glass.

He did not look for his own line. He knew what it said, and it was a long way up the page and still nowhere. He opened the band on his wrist instead, the one that had moved every time he had. Two weeks ago every name in it had been a stranger who could beat him. Now he could put a face to half of them, and a habit to some of the faces. Larson had said nine thousand as if it were a place she would reach first. He had gone past the number in the dark, on a ramp, with his ribs wrapped. He did not know yet whether she had.

He went up the stairs to mathematics with a count in his head that was not the median and not the bell. Somewhere above him in the band, somebody was eating breakfast who did not know his name yet. He meant to fix that.

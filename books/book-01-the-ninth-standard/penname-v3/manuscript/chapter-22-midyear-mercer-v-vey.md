# Chapter 22 — Midyear: Mercer v. Vey

The form had two fields, and Kade had been looking at the first one for eleven minutes.

Hale had only required it before the first bout. Nobody had made him file one for Ortez, or Min, or Bisset. He had filed them anyway, partly because Julian had turned it into a race, and partly because somewhere around noon he had noticed that being wrong felt different when he had written down in advance what wrong would look like. Four forms, four timestamps. The fifth sat open on his tablet in a service stairwell behind the main arena, where the concrete was cold through his warmups and nobody from Meridian could watch his face while he thought.

OPPONENT: VEY, MARA. RANK SEVEN.

PREDICTION.

He typed SHE WINS, and looked at it. It was very likely true, and it was not what the field was for. The field wanted to know how she fought, and he found that four months of standing across from her had left him with a great deal of experience and very few sentences.

So he went back through the experience. Mara had never once stopped him in the middle of a mistake. She let him make it. She let him notice it, and fix it, and then she told him what was wrong with the fix. Every correction she had ever given him had arrived one movement after the error, at the moment he was busiest being pleased with himself for catching it.

He deleted the two words and typed again. VEY DOES NOT ATTACK THE MISTAKE. SHE ATTACKS THE CORRECTION.

The second field took longer, as it always did. CONDITION THAT PROVES THIS WRONG. He had to imagine Mara doing something, and then imagine himself believing it.

SHE COMMITS AGAINST MY FIRST MOVEMENT, BEFORE I HAVE CHANGED ANYTHING.

He sent it. The timestamp said twenty-four minutes to six. His band buzzed almost at once, and it was Julian.

FIVE FILED. YOU?

FIVE, Kade sent. WHAT DID YOU PUT FOR YOUR QUARTERFINAL?

NOT TELLING YOU. WHOEVER COMES OUT OF YOUR BOUT IS MINE AT EIGHT. I AM HELPING NEITHER OF YOU.

VERY SPORTING.

VERY SELFISH. LEARN TO TELL THEM APART.

Kade put the tablet in the duffel, on top of the towel. His left forearm, the one that had worn Bisset's counter all through the fourth round, had gone the color of a plum, and it complained when he shouldered the bag. He switched shoulders and went to find his House.

---

House Meridian was having a constitutional crisis.

The House chant had a hole in it. It was four lines long, it was older than anyone in the building, and the third line ended on the name of whoever was fighting. It had worked for decades, as far as anybody could tell him, because the first-year bracket had never before put two Meridian names on either side of the same line this deep.

"We alternate," said a second-year, who was holding the chant sheet as if it were a legal instrument. "Mercer, then Vey, then Mercer."

"Vey has the higher rank," said someone else. "She goes first."

"Mercer's the one who needs it."

"Thank you," Kade said, from the door.

Nobody looked embarrassed. Priya, who had gone out in the third round that afternoon by a single point and still had the blue thread on her jacket, raised a cracker at him in greeting. "I'm for Mara. I wanted that on the record before you got attached to me."

"You put me on the floor the first week I knew you."

"And I'd like to see it done properly."

Eli was sitting on an equipment crate with his tablet dark in his lap, which was so unusual that Kade checked whether he was ill. "He's my roommate," Eli said, to the room. "But she's correct. Generally. About things. I don't know where to put that."

Anton came through sideways with a crate of ice packs on one shoulder. "Put it on a bench. We're seated in six minutes."

It was Sera who ended it. She took the chant sheet out of the second-year's hands, read it once, and crossed out one word with the pen from behind her ear. "You cheer the point. Whoever scores it. Nobody's name goes in the third line tonight. The line says Meridian, which is accurate either way."

"That's not the tradition," the second-year said.

"Tradition isn't required to survive cross-examination," said Sera, who had evidently been standing near the thread bowl that morning. "I've been told."

The second-year considered her, and considered how much of the House was already nodding, and gave way. Kade watched them go out toward the stands in a loose blue column. He found that he minded less than he had expected. A room full of people had just declined to choose him, and it had not felt like being left. They had declined to choose against her as well.

His band buzzed. Darius. ROOM FOUR IS STAYING LATE. ALVAREZ BROUGHT CHAIRS.

Then his mother. I HAVE A BREAK AT SIX. THIS IS A COINCIDENCE.

He did not answer either one. He turned the band face down on his wrist and went to warm up.

---

The prep corridor had six squares taped on the floor. He took the last one, as he had every day since October, and started the sequence, and had done it twice before he understood that someone was standing at the edge of the tape waiting for him to finish.

Mara had her gloves in one hand. She was looking at the square, and then at him, with the expression of a person who has walked into her own kitchen and found it occupied.

They had warmed up on this square together four mornings a week for three months. She fed him entries. He fed them back. Neither of them had thought about it on the way down the stairs.

"There are five others," Kade said.

"This one has the good tape."

"It's tape."

"The corner on that one lifts." She did not move toward any of the five others. Neither did he. After a moment she stepped into the far half of the square and began her own sequence, facing the wall, and he went back to his, facing the glass, and for several minutes they worked back to back in four square meters of floor without touching. Two Kepler students went past. One of them said something to the other, and they both looked back.

"Are you going easy on me?" he said, to the glass.

"No."

"Good."

"Were you worried?"

"I'd have been insulted."

Her feet stopped. He heard her turn round, so he turned as well. She was looking at him now, without any of the dryness she generally kept between herself and other people.

"I need to tell you something before we go out, so that it isn't a surprise." She chose the words carefully. "If it starts. The cold, the floor turning into one thing. I've seen your face when it happens. I'll see it tonight if it comes."

"I'm not going to use it."

"I believe you. That isn't what I'm telling you." She pulled one glove on and sealed it. "I'm not going to stop. If you're standing there deciding, I'm going to come through you and score. I won't wait for you to finish, and I won't back off to make it easier. I thought you should know that beforehand, and not find it out on the floor."

He thought about it. A year ago he would have heard a threat in it. He heard the opposite now. She was telling him that nobody out there was going to catch him, and that the stopping would be his own work or nobody's.

"Understood," he said. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. It's going to be unpleasant."

"I assumed."

She nodded, and sealed the other glove, and wound the loose end of blue thread back round its seal. Then she went out by the south tunnel, and he went out by the north.

An official met him at the tunnel mouth with a slate. First to four. A clean touch on a scoring band, the opponent over the strip, or a controlled takedown. No House corner, since they shared one. Between points, each fighter to his own reset sector, and no word from the stands. He pressed his thumb to the slate under all of it. There was one more line at the bottom, in type that looked newer than the rest. DELIBERATE COUPLING TO ARENA INFRASTRUCTURE: IMMEDIATE STOP AND TECHNICAL REVIEW.

"Has that always been there?"

"It has always been a rule," the official said. "This season it became a line."

The doors opened, and the noise reached him before the light did.

He laid the floor out in his head while he still had attention to spare. The ring was the ring he had fought on all day, with the lit strip round its edge and the two center marks. Beyond the strip lay one stride of dead floor. Beyond that stood the barrier, humming, held up by four anchor posts as thick as his body, one at each point of the compass. His reset sector was north and hers was south. Meridian had been seated low behind the east post, a block of blue with a gap down the middle where the argument had been.

There were ten thousand people in the building, and an announcer explaining at length who he was. Kade listened to none of it. Across the ring Mara stood on her mark with her weight centered and her hands loose, and he could read nothing at all in how she stood.

---

He did not rush her. He was proud of that for roughly four seconds.

She came to him. It was only a walk, steady and unhurried, with nothing in her hands yet. It took the space between them away at a constant rate and asked what he planned to do about it. His rear foot wanted to go straight back. He caught it, as he had been catching it since breakfast, and turned the step forward and onto the angle. It was the correction she and Julian had spent a season drilling into him, the one that had finally held under pressure against Bisset.

Mara was standing where the angle ended.

Her forearm came down across his lead guard, square on the bruise, hard enough to send a white line up to his elbow. While his arm was busy with that, two of her fingers touched the band at his shoulder.

One to nothing. The east block made its noise, all of it, for the point. He heard Priya over everyone.

He walked north. His form had been exactly right. She had not gone after his old mistake, the straight retreat. She had waited one movement for the correction, and she had not needed to guess where the correction would land, because she had installed it herself. He had predicted her perfectly, and it had done him no good on the floor.

All right. If she lived in his corrections, he would give her something she had never corrected.

On the second horn he came out with his right foot forward. He had never fought her that way. He had never fought anyone that way, outside of one bad afternoon with Julian. It was clumsy and he knew it was clumsy, but for the first time all evening Mara gave ground. One step, then another. The stands came up. He pressed, because it was working, and the pressing brought him in close, and there was her sleeve.

He took it. Grips were what he had won with today, and his hands knew it. They turned round the grip on the west side of the ring with the strip a pace off, each hauling for the line, and he was winning the haul. He could feel it. Her free hand was posted on his left forearm, on the plum, an ordinary brace, and even with that he was winning.

Then the resistance went out of the sleeve entirely.

She had not pulled loose. She had simply stopped opposing him and stepped the way he was dragging her. He had been hauling against a weight, and now it was coming along willingly. He went backward across the white on his own effort, both heels, with a good grip on the sleeve and nothing behind him.

Two to nothing. It was his own trick. He had done exactly that to Ortez at twenty to two that afternoon, and she had been at the rail for it.

She had not predicted the stance. She had not needed to. She had given ground so that the strange thing would seem to be working, because she knew what he did with a thing that worked. He leaned on it. He always leaned on it, inside a minute. She had predicted what he would do after he succeeded, and built the floor there.

He walked north the long way, to buy the seconds. In the stands Eli had both hands over his mouth. Sera sat forward with her elbows on her knees and her face giving away nothing at all, since giving anything away would have counted as coaching.

Mara was not fighting him. She was fighting the person he would be three decisions from now, and she had met that person and taken notes. Every mistake he made, he corrected, and she was waiting inside the correction. Every answer that worked, he repeated, and she was waiting for the repetition too. Julian had spent the autumn teaching him to plan, so that other people could rely on what he did next. Anything other people could rely on, she could rely on.

He could not fix it by being random. Random would beat itself inside two exchanges while she stood and watched. There was only one thing she had never seen him do. In four months he had never once made an error and left it alone.

Third horn. She showed him a lane inside her right guard, narrow, about the width of a shoulder. It was bait, and good bait. He went in anyway, and halfway down it he felt the door begin to close. Every nerve he had trained since October told him to bail out onto the angle and fix it.

He left it broken. He kept going straight down a lane that was shutting on him, took her forearm check full on the bruise with his teeth set, and put his right hand on the band at her ribs. A quarter second later her counter arrived, fast and beautifully placed, in the empty air to his left. His shoulder would have been there if he had corrected.

Two to one.

The east block went up for the point exactly as it had gone up for hers, and this time he heard Eli over everybody, which should not have been acoustically possible.

Mara looked at her own hand, and at the place it had gone to. Then she looked at him.

"That was a bad entry."

"It scored."

"It was still bad. You didn't fix it."

"No."

"You always fix it."

"I've been told."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth and was put away again. She went south. He went north with his forearm throbbing and his pulse loud in his ears, and he made himself say the rest of it. It would not work twice. He had used a favorite answer once already tonight, and lost the second point to it.

She did not give him the chance to try. On the fourth horn she simply took away his time.

It was not reckless. It was close, even pressure, one problem after another, and each one arrived a little before he had finished with the last. He blocked an entry. He turned a second with plain mechanics, and ate a controlled contact on the forearm doing it, and slipped a third. None of it was wrong, but all of it took effort, and his lungs had begun to burn, and he was drifting left. He knew he drifted left when he was tired. She had told him so herself, on a bench, two hours ago. Knowing it did not stop his feet. They went left and left again, round the ring, and she let them, and cut off only the way back.

His heel found the edge of the white. The barrier hummed against the back of his neck. One stride behind him stood the east post, and behind the post, in rows, sat everyone he ate breakfast with.

And the arena offered itself.

It came as it always came, politely, from the edge of his attention. He felt the floor under his stance and hers, the anchor post rooted down through the floor, and the barrier hung between the posts like cloth on a line. It was all one connected thing, with paths running through it, and the cold was starting its thin line up from the base of his skull toward the place behind his eyes. Her next step would put him over. The post was right there. It was available, and it was a different answer.

The crowd knew. He did not understand how ten thousand people could know, but the noise changed. There was no shouting this time. High in the general stands someone started stamping, slow, and then a section had it, and then a tier, and the beat came down through the building into the soles of his shoes. They were asking him for it. Behind him the Meridian block was completely silent.

He could move the problem. He knew that much. He did not know where every part of it would land. The paths ran through the post, and the post stood in front of his House. They ran through the floor, and Mara was standing on the floor. She was not outside the system. She was one of the things it was made of.

She was already coming, too. She had told him she would be.

He opened his right hand, the one with the thread on it, and told himself what belonged to the match. His body. Hers. The ring, the strip, the bands. Nothing underneath. It did not go at once. The post stayed joined to the floor a moment longer, as though a thing he was able to do had become a thing he owed. Then the cold drew back down the way it had come, and the arena went back to being objects. There was a post, and a floor, and a girl with her weight already committed.

She came straight through the space his attention had left and put her palm flat on the band at his chest.

Three to one.

The stamping fell apart into ordinary noise, some of it disappointed. He stood with his heels on the white, breathing. Mara had not stepped back yet. Her hand was still on the band. She looked at his face in the way Dr. Park sometimes did, checking for something specific.

"You said you wouldn't stop," he said.

"You believed me?"

"It's why it worked."

She took her hand away. Relief crossed her face, plainly, and then something else under it that he had no column for, and then she had turned and was walking south.

In the medical booth above the north tunnel, Park stood at the glass with her slate. There was someone behind her, a step back from the light, who turned away as he looked up. He thought it was Sen. He could not have sworn to it. Nobody signaled, and he took that for permission.

One more point and she had it. He stood on his mark and threw out everything clever he had. Clever was where she lived. Her feet were faster and her field was cleaner and her planning was a full season ahead of his. What he had was reach, which was dull, and which was the only thing on the floor she could not train away from him. He would put a straight lead hand on her shoulder band from outside her range, the same plain touch that had worked on Ortez, and if she came inside it he would take the forearm and turn.

The fifth horn went. He stepped, and threw the lead. It was the left hand.

It arrived a quarter of a beat late.

It was not much. The arm had been hit once in every exchange since the first horn, always legally, always as a small part of something else, and it had stiffened a little each time, and now it came up about as slowly as it had been taught to. Mara was inside it before it was straight. She took the wrist as it went by, turned under it, and fed all of his forward weight across her hip.

He understood the whole match while he was in the air, which was not a useful time to understand it. The contacts had come one per exchange, on the same arm. She had not been winning five points. She had been spending four of them to build the fifth.

He landed flat, under control, and the floor field took the last fraction off it. The horn came down on top of him. WINNER, said the board, in letters a story high. VEY. FOUR TO ONE.

He lay there and laughed. It came up out of his ribs and hurt on the way.

Mara's face appeared above him, upside down against the lights. "What?"

"The arm. You've been working on the arm since the first point."

"Since before the first point."

"When?"

"When you wore Bisset's counter on it. I was at the rail."

"The bracket hadn't posted."

"I can read a bracket before it posts, Mercer. There were two people it could have been." She put a hand down. "I made a plan for the other one as well. It was shorter."

He took the hand and came up. The thread on his right hand had pulled loose in the fall and hung off the strap in a blue loop. She glanced down and saw it. She took his hand in both of hers, turned it over, and tucked the ends flat under the strap where they would not snag, quickly and without any fuss, as she would have squared a stack of paper.

It took about two seconds. The arena cameras had it for all of them, forty feet high, on every screen in the building.

The noise ten thousand people made was not one that a takedown earns. Mara looked up at the display, and at his hand, and let go of it as though the strap had been switched on.

"Equipment," she said.

"Obviously."

Kade looked for Meridian, which was a mistake. The House was on its feet for the point, as ruled. Eli was making a face that Kade filed for later revenge. Sera had both eyebrows up. Taren was applauding with enormous goodwill. Priya had her arms folded and looked satisfied. Two sections along, gloved and waiting on his own call, Julian gave him a single nod.

His band went off before he reached the tunnel. Darius. ROOM FOUR WOULD LIKE TO KNOW WHAT THAT WAS.

EQUIPMENT CHECK, Kade sent.

ALVAREZ SAYS SURE.

---

Park was waiting at the gate with the penlight, and he got in ahead of her.

"I started one. Fourth exchange, with my back to the east post. I'd say two seconds. The cold line came up to about my ears and went back down. No headache. No lag." He held the right hand out flat before she asked. It was steady.

She checked his pupils anyway, and compared what she saw against the slate. "That agrees with what I have."

"You could see it?"

"Your pupils changed, and your balance, and then they changed back." She wrote for longer than a check mark took. "You have been treating that as the absence of an event, Mr. Mercer. It was an event. You began a thing and declined to complete it, under load, with people asking you to. That is a skill. It can be trained, and I intend to test it."

All his life, not doing a thing had felt like nothing. It was what he had done in hallways. It had not occurred to him that anyone could score it.

"It goes in my file," he said.

"Everything goes in your file." She waved him down the ramp. "Ice that arm. She was very thorough."

Julian was twelve steps farther on, working his wrists loose.

"She beat you with the fifth point from the first," he said.

"I noticed. Eventually."

"You plan an exchange now. Six weeks ago you couldn't, so don't sulk." Julian pulled a glove tight. "Vey plans a match."

"And you?"

Julian smiled, all teeth and goodwill. "I plan a bracket." He went off to win his quarterfinal, which took him a little over a minute. It occurred to Kade that there seemed to be more useful people every week, and that they were wearing him out.

---

They sent him to the recovery hall for the forearm. A medic strapped a cold pack round it, told him twenty minutes, and left him in a bay beside a divider. Nessa Kim sat behind the divider in dark glasses, with her back to the wall screen.

"You lost," she said.

"Four to one."

"I heard the building." She tilted her head. "I heard it twice. The second one wasn't a point."

"It was equipment."

"Mm."

He stayed the twenty minutes, and then he stayed longer, because the semifinal went up on the wall screen at eight and Nessa could not look at it. The medics kept the sound low. After the first horn she said, without turning round, "Tell me."

So he told her. He had never called a match aloud before, and it turned out to be work. He could not say *she did a thing*. He had to say which thing, and why.

"Julian has the center. He's left her both sides. She's not taking either. She's walking him. It's the same walk she opened on me." He leaned forward. "No. It isn't. She has no corrections to wait for. She never taught him anything. She's measuring."

Julian scored first, a clean shoulder touch off a closing step. Then Mara found something, and Kade heard his own voice speed up.

"She's stopped going after where he stands. She's going after when he changes. Every time he switches direction there's a sliver where the old plan has left and the new one hasn't got his whole weight yet, and she's working in the slivers. That's one. That's two. He's gone half a step deeper on his mark. He does that when somebody's hurt him. That's three. Three to one, Vey."

Eli had arrived at some point with a tablet and a tray, and sat on the end of the bench eating Kade's crackers. Nessa said, to the wall, "He's said *she* fourteen times and *he* three."

"I've been counting since House Trial," Eli said. "It gets worse."

"I'm describing a match."

"You're describing half of one," said Nessa.

Then Kade stopped talking, because Julian had given up a step on the west side that he had no reason to give.

He did it again on the next pass. It was the same step, in the same place, given up the same way. He did it a third time, and Mara took the ground each time and scored nothing with it, and Kade's stomach went cold, because he had stood in that exact spot. It had been in the seventh private session. Julian had handed him the center twice, and the second time Kade had built his whole next exchange on having it.

"He's letting her plan," Kade said.

"What?"

"She plans matches. So he's giving her something to plan on. Same concession, three times, so she'll trust it." He was on his feet, with the cold pack hanging off his arm. "And now he takes it away."

Julian took it away. The west side was open as Mara committed, and then it was shut, and she had built on it, and he touched her back as she turned. Three to two. She adjusted at once, and the adjustment was a good one, and Julian had planned for it. Three all. With eight seconds on the clock she tried to make a third choice out of nothing. Julian turned her off her base and put her down on her left shoulder, controlled, and the horn went.

CROSS, FOUR. VEY, THREE.

On the screen she lay still for one breath, and got up, and shook his hand. She did not make a scene of it, and she did not smile either.

She came into the recovery hall nine minutes later with a medic at her elbow and ice on the shoulder. She saw Eli's tablet before she saw anyone's face, and held out her good hand. Eli gave the tablet up without a word. She sat down on the bench beside Kade and watched her last forty seconds twice, and then a third time.

"You're allowed to be annoyed first," Kade said.

"I'm doing both. It's efficient." She froze it on Julian's third concession. "You've seen this."

"He did it to me in October. I built on it and he pulled it out. I nearly said something to you this afternoon."

"Why didn't you?"

"He wouldn't help me against you. It seemed fair that it ran both ways." He paused. "Would you have listened?"

She gave that a moment's honest thought. "No."

She took out her own tablet, opened something, and after a visible hesitation turned it round to him. It was Hale's form, with a timestamp from that afternoon.

OPPONENT: MERCER, KADE. PREDICTION: CORRECTS EVERY ERROR WITHIN ONE MOVEMENT. CONDITION THAT PROVES THIS WRONG: HE LEAVES ONE UNCORRECTED.

"You filed one on me."

"I filed one on everybody. I had you at four to nothing. The third point was the only one I hadn't planned." She said it flatly, as a finding. "You left one."

He got his own out of the duffel and handed it across. She read both fields. "That's accurate."

"It didn't help."

"You knew what I'd do. You couldn't afford to stop giving it to me." She scrolled to her next form and looked at it for some time before she showed him. OPPONENT: CROSS, JULIAN. PREDICTION: CONCEDES NOTHING TWICE. CONDITION THAT PROVES THIS WRONG: HE CONCEDES THE SAME GROUND TWICE.

"He did it three times," she said. "I wrote down what being wrong would look like. Then I watched it happen and filed it as an opportunity."

Kade knew roughly what it cost her to show that to anyone. He said nothing about it, because he thought she would want it left alone.

"He had to build that," he said. "I watched him build it. It took him three exchanges and most of the match, and he needed your own choices to do it. He didn't have anything else that was working."

"That's suspiciously generous, from someone I threw."

"Once. The rest were touches and a strip."

"I'm counting the warmup corridor in November."

"That's not in the bracket."

"It's in my records."

He pulled her tablet across and ran the footage back to the first concession. "I can show you how he sets it. Tomorrow, zero-field, on the good tape. It's all in his rear foot."

"And I'll show you how to stop telling a whole arena which arm hurts." She took the tablet back. Their shoulders were touching, the iced one and his, and had been for some time. "You guard an injury with your entire face."

Eli leaned toward the divider. "Are you getting this?"

"I can't see," Nessa said, "and I can still see it."

Neither of the people on the bench appeared to hear. Kade was looking at Julian's rear foot. He had expected that losing to her would feel like being moved down a place. It felt more like being let into the room where the real work was done. He was surprised to find he would rather be in there at four to one than outside it at any score.

---

Julian won the final a little before ten. Meridian watched it from the dining hall over a very late dinner. Rank one stayed rank one, and the man who hung the medal on him had to reach up to do it. Julian looked more tired than triumphant.

Then the broadcast cut to a remote feed from an office with a great deal of glass in it, and introduced Lucian Cross.

Kade had never seen him before. He looked at the father for about a second and at the son for the rest, because the display had split, and on the left half of it Julian had gone half a step deeper on his mark.

"He did what he was there to do," said Lucian Cross.

The interviewer waited, politely, for something warmer. It did not come, and she asked what set his son apart.

"Ownership. The strongest person in a room owns what happens in it. Every failure in that room is his, whoever commits it. Julian has understood that since he was nine. It is why he doesn't lose."

It was delivered with complete calm, as a compliment. Kade had heard pieces of it all term, in Julian's voice, and had half admired them. Whole, and in the original, it left nothing in the building for anybody else. It left Julian the winning and took everything else from everyone. Mara's fifty seconds went, and the finalist's split lip, and the eight technicians who had kept a barrier stable for fourteen hours.

Someone handed Julian a microphone. Along the Meridian table, the forks stopped.

"I'd like to thank my opponent for the final, which I did not enjoy," Julian said. There was laughter. "And Vey, who had me beaten for fifty-two seconds tonight. I counted them at the time. I got out of it because she is good enough to be worth planning against. The barrier crew has been on since six this morning. None of what happened on that floor was mine alone."

He had not contradicted his father by a single word. He had stood in front of the whole island and politely handed the result back round the room, a share at a time. On the right half of the display, Lucian Cross's face did not move at all.

The hall applauded the sportsmanship. Mara, across the table with her arm in a sling of ice, watched the screen without applauding, and Kade thought she had heard what he had. It was an argument, and only one of the two people in it was free to admit there was one. Rank one had costs of its own, it turned out.

The recalculation posted while the plates were being cleared. Kade's tablet shook against his tray.

MERCER, KADE. MIDYEAR OPEN: QUARTERFINALIST. RECORD: FOUR WINS, ONE LOSS. ELITE OPPONENT PERFORMANCE: HIGH. SAFETY COMPLIANCE: FULL. NEW RANK: TWO HUNDRED AND ELEVEN.

The word PROVISIONAL was nowhere on it.

"I lost," he said. "By three points. And went up sixteen hundred and ninety-three places."

"From nineteen hundred and four. That number was six weeks stale," Eli said, with his mouth full. "The rules told you this morning. Opponent rank, bracket depth, clean wins, penalty rate. You beat a five hundred and three, and then you took a point off a seven without drawing a single penalty. That's a normal response to losing."

"Your previous rank lagged your demonstrated performance," Mara said, not looking up from her rice.

"That was almost a compliment."

"It was arithmetic."

His mother called while he was carrying his tray back. He took it in the corridor, with his good shoulder against the wall.

"I saw your hand open," Elena said. She had not said hello. He could hear a monitor chiming somewhere behind her, and someone paging a porter. "Fourth point. You were standing against that post with your whole face gone strange, and your hand opened, and you let her hit you."

"You said you were charting."

"I chart very fast. Kade." She stopped. When she went on her voice was careful. "I don't understand what it is that you put down. I know what it looks like when somebody puts down something heavy on purpose. I've watched people fail to do it for twenty years. That's the part of tonight I'll keep. The number's very nice as well."

"Two hundred and eleven."

"Very nice. Ice the arm. I love you. I have to go, someone's pulled out a line."

He stood in the corridor for a moment after the band went dark. When he had been ten thousand four hundred and eighty-second, the ladder had been the whole sky. Tonight it seemed to be about the right size. It was one system, and there were people in his life whose opinion of him it had not produced.

The eighth-floor laundry had six machines, of which four worked, and at eleven at night in Midyear Week all four were full of tournament kit. Kade sat on the folding counter with his feet on a dead washer and his tablet on his knee. He had come down to wash two training sets. He had stayed because of Hale.

He had wanted to know, idly, whether the five forms counted for anything. So he had requested his own recalculation detail, which any student could do, and which almost none did. The public notice had given six factors. The detail view had sixty-four.

PRE-BOUT PREDICTION: SPECIFICITY. PRE-BOUT PREDICTION: FALSIFIER QUALITY. So they did count. He kept scrolling. Entry commitment latency. Recovery path efficiency. Behavior following successful technique. He winced at that one. Gaze direction. Heart rate. Response to crowd escalation. Reset interval utilization, which seemed to mean that the system had graded him for walking north the long way and thinking.

The quarterfinal had thrown off a little under fifty thousand timestamped events, and one of them carried a faculty flag. BOUNDARY EVENT INITIATED. NOT COMPLETED. VOLUNTARY. SAFE. Beside it was a note with Park's initials. SUBJECT DISCRIMINATED MATCH BOUNDARY FROM STRUCTURE UNDER COMPETITIVE PRESSURE.

The crowd had called that moment a disappointment, and his mother had called it putting something down. Park called it a skill. The ranking system, he saw, had given it a coefficient, and he could not find the value anywhere.

"They publish weights by cohort," Eli said, reading upside down from the floor, where he was sorting socks into a system. "Never by person. Otherwise people would optimize for the visible function."

"People already do. It's called the ladder."

"They optimize for the number. They can't optimize for what goes into the number, because they don't know what that is." Eli turned the tablet round to face himself. "It's a lot of what, I'll admit."

Sera came in with a basket on her hip and read over both of them while her machine filled. Kade pointed at the gaze fields. "It knows how long I looked at a post before I decided not to break it."

"Park knows that."

"Park's a person."

"Medical needs contact and neurological data to stop a bad bout. I'd want them to have it." She read further, and her face changed. "They don't need to keep this one to stop a bout. Or this." She put her basket down.

They sorted it on the folding counter, the three of them, among the warm towels. Fields that scored the match went in one column, and fields that kept someone safe went in a second. A third was for fields they could explain by coaching. The fourth was for fields that had more than one plausible use, or none that any of them could name. The fourth column grew faster than the rest. Near the bottom of it was a line timestamped to the second the cold had reached the east post. RESPONSE UNDER INFRASTRUCTURE INSTABILITY.

"That isn't a tournament category," Sera said. "That's one of mine. That's an emergency-response category."

"What's it doing in a ranking?"

Nobody had an answer. One of the dryers finished and buzzed.

"I can't find what lets them hold it," Kade said. "There's a consent line in enrollment, but it points at six other documents."

"Fourth one. The part on research use." Taren had come in behind them with a laundry bag over his shoulder and his easy smile, and he had not yet looked at the screen. He dropped the bag by the last free machine. "The student summary calls it anonymized training studies. That's the friendly version."

Kade opened the fourth document. The section was exactly where Taren had said it would be. It listed four permitted purposes. Operational readiness. Institutional safety. Curriculum validation. He read the last one aloud. "Aggregate capability forecasting, for accredited institutional partners."

"Forecasting for whom?" Sera said. "Who are the partners?"

"It doesn't say," said Eli, who had already searched. "It isn't defined. Not here, and not in the other five."

Kade looked up. "Taren?"

Taren was measuring detergent with great care. For a moment he was very still. Kade had begun to notice that stillness in him near locked doors and restricted shelves. Then it was gone. "It isn't written anywhere you can open."

Kade noticed that he had been asked who and had answered where. He noticed, too, that the answer had not been *I don't know*. He put both things on the pile with the taped ankle, and the interesting air, and the right coffee orders, and it occurred to him that a pile was a way of storing evidence in the hope of never having to look at it.

Eli copied the policy number into his own notes. "A system can need a thing for one good reason and keep it for another," he said quietly.

Kade thought of his mother's records at the hospital. He thought of Sen, who had known which questions to ask him before he understood that they were questions. He thought of an accident report with Aaron Mercer's name on it, and a transfer order dated twenty-three days too early to fit the story.

They drifted out one at a time, as the machines finished. Sera went first, with her basket squared. Eli followed with his socks in their system. Taren went last, whistling. Kade stayed on the counter with the fourth column, and that was where Mara found him. She had a bag of kit in her good hand and the other arm out of its ice at last.

She read the columns without being asked, from the top, as closely as she read physics. It took her some minutes. One dryer was still turning.

"Measurement can have more than one purpose," she said finally.

"Ten thousand of us. Every bout recorded, every mistake put in a category, every kind of person put under load until somebody knows exactly what they do." He turned the stylus over in his fingers. "Sen told me once that rank makes performance legible. I thought she meant to us."

"It sounds paranoid."

"It did in September. I've read my father's file since." He pulled a blank page across the list and wrote in capitals, from habit. PREDICTION: THE RANKING KEEPS MORE THAN IT SHOWS, AND SOMEBODY OUTSIDE THIS ISLAND READS IT. Underneath he wrote the second field. CONDITION THAT PROVES THIS WRONG: SOMEONE SHOWS ME WHO THE PARTNERS ARE.

Mara took the stylus out of his hand. Under his line she added one of her own, in small exact letters. OR THE FOURTH COLUMN HAS AN EXPLANATION ANY OF US CAN CHECK.

"Two conditions," she said. "You should have more than one way to be wrong."

"Nobody's offered either."

"No."

The last dryer wound down and stopped, and the room went quiet. On his wrist the band still showed the evening's number, two hundred and eleven, in clean white figures. It was one line of sixty-four, and it was the only one they let him see. Kade looked at it, and then at the page with two handwritings on it, and he did not put the timestamp on yet. He wanted to know first who else would be reading the form.

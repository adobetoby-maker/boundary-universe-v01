# Chapter 21 — Midyear: Open Rounds

The second-years came over the residence bridges before sunrise with their arms full of cloth, and by the time Kade took the duffel down off the shelf the island had been tied up like a parcel.

He watched them from the window with the bag in his hands. They worked in pairs, one holding, one knotting, narrow strips on every third railing post, blue for Meridian and white for Kepler and red for Horizon. The wind off the water got into all of it at once. From the eighth floor the Central Bridge looked like an argument conducted entirely in flags.

"You took the bag down," Eli said, from under his blanket.

"Spare kit. Competitors bring a change of clothes. It's in the rules."

"I know it's in the rules. I'm noting that the bag came down and nobody died." Eli surfaced, hair flat on one side, and reached for his tablet before his glasses. "It's a shelf. It's not a door. I was listening."

Kade put two training sets, a towel and a pair of socks in the duffel, and zipped it as far as the slider would go.

Eli had the bracket up before he had both feet on the floor. Two thousand and forty-eight first-years had gone into qualifying pools on Monday, and two days of pool bouts had cut them to a hundred and twenty-eight, and Kade had come through his pool with forearms that still remembered the bouts he had not won. Now the hundred and twenty-eight hung over Eli's desk in a lattice of names, and Eli looked at it the way some people looked at the sea.

"It's beautiful," he said.

"It's a spreadsheet."

"It's destiny with column headings."

Kade read the rules instead. The Open was not the ladder. Rank decided seeding and nothing else. Every bout fed a weighting: opponent strength, point margin, clean technique, penalties, how deep in the bracket the bout was fought. The weighting produced a live projection beside each competitor's name. LIVE PROJECTION. PROVISIONAL. SUBJECT TO FULL RECALCULATION AT CLOSE OF EVENT. A person could lose in the fourth round and finish a thousand places higher than he started. A person could win ugly and barely move.

He was seeded at nineteen hundred and four, the number the House Trial had given him. It had been his for six weeks and he still read it like someone else's mail.

---

Midyear Week cut classes down to a single morning seminar, and the faculty had responded to the loss by making the seminars compulsory. At seven Kade sat in Tactical Systems with thirty-one other people who were all pretending not to check their brackets under the table.

Instructor Hale stood in front of a frozen frame of pool footage and waited. He did not ask for anything. One at a time, around the room, the wrist displays went dark.

"The Open evaluates adaptation under repeated observation," Hale said. "By this afternoon anyone you face will have watched every bout you've fought this week. Your favorite answer expires each time you use it. Some of you have only the one. We will find out who."

The frame was Julian Cross, caught halfway into an entry with his weight gone forward and his rear heel off the floor. It was strange to see him wrong.

"Cross. What's the matter with this?"

Julian studied himself from the second row without visible discomfort. "I entered on a retreat I'd assumed. He'd stopped retreating two exchanges earlier. I was fighting the man I expected."

"Mercer. What happens next?"

Three months ago Kade would have guessed, and put enough confidence on the guess that nobody could tell. He looked at the frame instead. Julian moving forward. The other fighter planted at an angle, barrier close on Julian's right, lead foot turned out a few degrees too far.

"The obvious thing is Cross gets turned into the barrier."

"Obvious things are frequently things."

"Not here. His lead foot's outside the angle he'd need. He can't finish the turn from there. Cross gives up the first contact, takes the forearm instead, and rotates through before the field closes. It's a draw."

Hale let the footage run. Julian gave up the contact, took the forearm, rotated, and turned a bad entry into an exchange nobody scored. Several heads came round. Kade kept his eyes on the screen, because looking back would have made it a performance.

"What did Mercer do correctly?"

"Ignored the likely result," said someone from Sol.

"More specific."

"He separated the position from the reputation of the people in it," Mara said, from the end of Kade's row. "It's Cross, so everyone assumes Cross has a way out. It's a first-round pool opponent, so everyone assumes he doesn't get to do that to Cross. Mercer looked at the feet."

Hale nodded, once. Kade wrote it down. Mara glanced at his notes.

"That was a compliment. From Hale."

"I'm recording the date. For legal purposes."

Hale turned from the screen. "Your assignment. Before your first bout, each of you will submit one prediction about your opponent and one condition that would prove the prediction wrong. Timestamps will be checked." The room objected as a single organism, and he waited it out. "You are not graded on whether you're right. You are graded on whether you said, in advance, what being wrong would look like. Most of you have never once done that. It shows on film."

Kade opened his first opponent's profile. ARMITAGE, DEV. HOUSE KEPLER. RANK SIXTEEN HUNDRED AND ELEVEN. REDIRECTION, CONFIRMED. He had watched four of Armitage's pool bouts twice each. The first field was easy. UNDER PRESSURE, RETURNS TO CENTER BY THE SHORTEST ROUTE AVAILABLE.

The second field sat empty longer. It was easy to say what he expected. It was hard to name, for a form with a timestamp, the thing that would take the expectation away from him.

TAKES A LONGER RESET AFTER A MISSED OUTSIDE STRIKE WHEN THE CENTER LANE IS OPEN.

He sent it. Across the aisle Julian's tablet chimed a second later.

"Race you to four honest revisions," Julian said.

"That's a very specific thing to compete over."

"I intend to win it."

"Obviously you do."

---

At the bridge junction an upper-year from Meridian stood with a wooden bowl of blue thread, cut in lengths, and held it out to every first-year in House colors who passed.

Kade stopped. "What is it?"

"First-year Open marker."

"What does it do?"

"Marks you as a first-year in the Open."

Kade looked down at the tournament uniform he was wearing, which had FIRST-YEAR OPEN printed on the sleeve.

The upper-year shook the bowl once. "Tradition isn't required to survive cross-examination, Mercer. Take a thread."

He took one. Around him the House was tying them on: wrist, ankle, the end of a braid, a zipper pull. Mara wound hers twice round the seal of her left glove and knotted it with her teeth. Sera tied one to the shoulder of Priya's warmup jacket, because Priya's fingers would not hold still long enough to do it, and Priya let her, which was its own information. Eli tried to fix his to his tablet case.

"Body or uniform," the upper-year said.

"My tablet is functionally both."

"Navarro."

Eli tied it round his wrist.

Kade knotted his to the strap across the back of his right hand, the hand Hale had once promised to stop if it started a conversation. The thread weighed nothing at all. All the way over the bridge, every time the wind pulled at it, he checked that it was still there.

The Commons had become a command post nobody had authorized. Three screens ran live arenas. One ran the House standings. One carried Eli's bracket, which now had filters, and filters on the filters, and a column he had labeled LIKELY EMOTIONAL DEVASTATION until Sera made him change it. He changed it to UPSET PROBABILITY.

"Less honest," he said.

"More normal," Sera said.

Julian fought at eight, because rank one drew the first seeded slot. Meridian watched it over eggs. It lasted fifty-one seconds, and his opponent scored first. The whole room made a noise. On the screen Julian did not. He went back to his mark, came out half a step deeper than he had been standing, and took the next three points without being touched again.

"You're studying him at breakfast," Eli said.

"I'm eating."

"You stopped chewing forty seconds ago."

Mara fought at eight forty and was back before his tea was cold. Four to nothing. Two minutes fourteen.

"That seemed rude," Kade said.

"To whom?"

"She didn't score."

"That was the intention." Mara sat. "Armitage likes the outside entry and goes to his rear hand when he's pressed. He'll want to put you over the strip early, because everyone knows you back up straight."

"You finished twelve minutes ago."

"And then I watched his warmup."

"That's a different kind of concerning."

Taren came in on her other side with a plate and his easy smile and a folded half page. "I have notes as well. I resent being outworked."

Most of the half page was what the public footage showed. One line was not. LEFT ANKLE TAPED YESTERDAY AFTER CONDITIONING. STIFF, NOT HURT.

"How do you know that?"

"People train in public."

"I train in public. I didn't see it."

"I see more people than you do." Taren took a piece of Kade's toast. "It's a gift and a burden."

It was plausible. Most things Taren said were. Kade put it with the other things he was keeping until later. If the ankle was hurt, he did not want a win that came from it. If it was not, aiming at it was a waste of a plan. Either way it came out of the model.

At two minutes past nine his band buzzed. Darius. ROOM FOUR HAS YOU ON THE BIG SCREEN. The picture under it showed the classroom display carrying the Asterion bracket, and Ms. Alvarez beneath it with her arms folded, and on the whiteboard behind her, in her capitals, DO THE PROBLEM SET DURING THE ADS.

Then his mother. I AM WORKING. I AM NOT WATCHING.

Thirty seconds after that. WHY IS YOUR WARMUP SHIRT INSIDE OUT.

He looked down. It was. He turned it round in the stairwell before anyone saw. Eli saw, and drew breath, and let it out again without using it, which Kade understood to be love.

---

Equipment check ate the twelve minutes before his call. A technician weighed his forearm guards, pressure-tested both field seals, and ran a calibration wand round the edges of his shoes. The wand chirped at the right heel.

"Again." The second pass was clean. "Salt on the contact strip."

"From what?"

"You live on an island."

At the next station Dev Armitage stood with both arms straight out while another technician checked the redirection nodes sewn along his sleeves. He was compact and unhurried. He caught Kade watching.

"Looking for illegal equipment?"

"Trying to work out if the pose is compulsory."

"It helps." Kade's technician pointed, and Kade held out his arms. Armitage nodded gravely. "See."

The prep corridor had six squares taped on the floor. Kade took the last and started the sequence Julian had drilled into him over six weeks of being thrown: forward on the angle, recover, lateral, recover, reverse without crossing his feet. His body wanted it fast. Fast made it sloppy. He slowed it until each foot arrived under him and not ahead of him.

Through the glass the arena came in pieces between the structural ribs. A block of blue cloth. Cameras tucked under the scoreboard. Somewhere behind the cameras, a little under two seconds late, a classroom in Northline and a nurses' station at St. Vincent. He felt that land in his shoulders, and reset.

"Your bag," Sera said, from the door.

She was holding the green duffel up by its doubled handle. The slider had given way where it always did, and one sock hung out of the gap.

"That's ventilation."

"Sure." She fed the sock back in and worked the slider shut with her thumbnail, and put the bag under the bench with five other Meridian bags. The last time she had seen it, it had been on his shoulder going toward a gate. Neither of them said so. It sat there with the others, inconvenient and ordinary.

"Your feet stopped," she said, and left.

Forward on the angle. Recover.

They met at center before the barrier sealed. Armitage shook his hand and said, "No floor tricks."

Kade sighed. "I need a shirt."

"Saying what?"

"It was twice."

Armitage laughed, a real one, and then the horn went and he stopped being funny.

The arena was the standard Open floor: a ring with the strip running round its edge in a band of lit white, and two center marks facing each other across the middle. A clean touch on a scoring band scored, shoulder or ribs or back. So did putting the other fighter over the strip.

Armitage opened with the outside entry, exactly as Mara had said he would, and Kade's rear foot had started straight back before he had made any decision about it. He caught it, turned the step onto the angle, and the strike went past close enough to move the cloth on his shoulder. No score. Armitage recovered through center, by the short road.

That was one. One was an event. It was not an answer yet.

Armitage came again, outside, then cut in, and this time the catch came late. Kade went back straight, the way eight years of hallways had taught him to give ground, heel, heel, and the strip lit under both feet. The Kepler rail came up around him like weather. One to nothing.

At the reset Armitage's eyes went to Kade's left shoulder, reading the guard. They did not go anywhere near the ankle on Taren's half page. Kade let go of the thought before it turned into gratitude. Armitage wanted a clean bout too. That was all it was.

The second point he took by refusing the same pressure. He went off the angle and not away, got two fingers on Armitage's sleeve at the contact band, and turned the pair of them round the grip until Armitage, not Kade, ran out of floor. It was not elegant. The board counted it regardless.

Two exchanges went by unscored. In the second, Armitage nearly had him with something Kade had seen twice on film and never felt: a turn with the field in it, a pull at his forearm guard that tried to make his own momentum finish a throw he had not started. He dropped his center, gave up his attack entirely, and stayed on his feet by half a step. Armitage smiled as they broke. There was nothing in it about Zero or floors. It was one fighter pleased that another had understood the question.

Then Kade pressed, and under pressure the rear hand came exactly where Mara had said it would, big and a shade early, and he slipped inside it and touched ribs. Two to one. And then he backed up straight again, at three minutes, tired for the first time, and gave the point back over the strip. Two all. Knowing about the habit had not yet finished turning into feet.

But through all of it he had been counting. Every missed outside strike, Armitage went home through the same narrow lane, left of center, short road. Three times. Four. Kade did not use it. Six weeks ago he would have jumped on it the first time he was sure, and Julian would have read the jump and put him on the mat. He had paid a great deal for that lesson, and he made himself wait the pattern out. Five.

On the last exchange he gave Armitage the outside lane, wide and inviting. Armitage took it, missed, and turned for home, and Kade was already standing in the short road.

Armitage saw it with his weight committed. He did the only thing left. He broke the other way and took the long reset, round the far side, with the center lane standing wide open behind Kade's shoulder.

For half a second the prediction was wrong, in exactly the words Kade had typed that morning. Having typed them, he was not surprised by it, and that made the difference. He let the plan go without an argument. He did not close a center nobody was coming to. He turned with Armitage, cut the chord across the long road, and touched the band at his shoulder as he arrived.

Three to two, and the horn came down on top of it.

He caught himself smiling. He had known, this time, exactly why it worked, and exactly when it had stopped working, and had still been standing in the right place.

Armitage shook his hand. "You sat on that for three exchanges."

"Five."

"That's worse."

"People keep telling me."

Hale was at the exit gate with a slate. "Visual lag."

"No."

"Tremor."

Kade held his right hand out flat, thread and all. It was steady.

"Cold line."

"No. Never came up."

Hale made his marks. "You backed up straight twice and it cost you two points. You know that, so I've saved my breath. Otherwise you won clean."

"That was nearly approval."

"Don't make it unpleasant."

A medical technician checked his pupils anyway, and a second checked the first one's reading. In September Kade would have made a remark about being singled out. He understood now that a person who had broken a floor had earned a second penlight. He did not like it any better for understanding it.

On the board his line rewrote itself, and the number beside LIVE PROJECTION was a good deal shorter than nineteen hundred and four. PROVISIONAL, it said after it. He decided not to memorize a number that had promised to change.

---

Tournament days, it turned out, were mostly corridors. He had ninety minutes. He showered, ate half of something, and reached the rail of Arena Four for the last of Priya's first round.

Priya fought every exchange as though it had said something about her family. The Lagrange fighter across from her had a head of height and enough raw output that the barrier brightened whenever he committed, and she had answered by living inside his reach, where his big motions had nowhere to grow. Two all, and under a minute.

He drove. She planted. Her field came up round her shoulders, and Kade knew what came next because he had watched it all autumn: Priya would meet force with force, because she always did, and she was a good deal smaller than the force.

She let the brace go. She stepped across the line of the push and let him spend the whole of it on air, and hooked an arm inside his guard as he went by and touched the band at his ribs. The barrier chimed. Blue came off the rail in a wave. Priya shook the Lagrange fighter's hand and cleared the floor before she looked at any of it.

Then she found Kade. "You missed the good part."

"I saw you not do something stupid."

"Exactly. Historic." She reached for the water Sera was holding out, and her left hand shook, and Sera had the bottle before it dropped.

"Medical."

"I'm fine."

"Your hand disagrees."

Priya took the bottle in her right. "My hand is dramatic." Sera was already steering her down the ramp by the elbow, and Kade went with them, because his next arena lay that way, and because he had said *I'm fine* in that tone often enough to know what it was worth.

In the recovery hall barrier impacts came through the walls as soft thuds. Medics talked low under a display that listed rest minimums, neurological holds, and withdrawals. KIM, N. was on the third list. MANDATORY WITHDRAWAL. PERCEPTION OVERLOAD.

Nessa sat two bays down in dark glasses with her head against the wall.

"Too much?" Kade said.

"Too many fields at once. Six practice floors running underneath us. Two live barriers sharing a wall. And somebody in the stands with a projection band, pulsing it."

"That's allowed?"

"Having terrible taste is still legal."

Priya leaned round the divider. "Was it the orange one?"

Nessa flinched. "Don't describe colors at me."

"Sorry." Priya withdrew.

Nessa pressed two fingers to the bridge of the glasses. "People think Perception means you see more. It means you can't stop seeing more when what you need is less. Usually I can pick the field that matters. In there I couldn't. I knew it was building. I stayed, because it was two all." She tipped her head at the display without looking at it. "I lost the next exchange because I could see the barrier anchors better than the person hitting me."

"Worth it?"

"Ask me tomorrow. No."

He thought of a floor that stopped being a floor. Her foot, the lattice, the wall, all one thing, and the cold drawing itself from the base of his skull to a place behind his eyes. "Sounds familiar."

The glasses hid her eyes. They did not hide where her attention went. "You know before it happens."

He flexed his right hand. The thread shifted on the strap. "Sometimes."

"And you stop."

"Sometimes."

Nessa nodded toward her own name on the withdrawal list. "Then get better at sometimes."

He did not explain and she did not ask. A medic gave Priya twenty minutes' rest, minimum, and gave Nessa permission to sit in the dark until the world had fewer edges. Sera moved the divider a hand's width to cut the corridor light off Nessa's face. She did it without being asked and without looking to see who noticed.

The application band on Sera's wrist showed a single amber mark. PENDING.

"Still nothing?" Kade said.

"Still pending."

"When did you last check?"

"Six minutes ago."

"Four," Nessa said, behind the divider.

Sera glared at a person who could not see her do it.

---

At eleven Eli lost.

It was not combat. The engineering-control bracket ran in parallel with the Open in a converted calibration lab under Arena Two, eight workstations behind clear partitions and three judges who looked professionally immune to excitement. Each pair got the same task: hold a simulated emergency grid stable through three load spikes, one component failure, and a forced restart. Twelve minutes to inspect the hardware first.

Eli and his partner, a second-year from the ninth floor with a soldering scar on her thumb, spent eleven of the twelve arguing.

"The secondary sink is under capacity."

"It only has to catch the restart transient."

"Which is over capacity."

"For three tenths of a second."

"That is still a length of time, Navarro."

"If we move capacity off primary, the first spike takes us out and the restart never matters." He pointed at a graph. She looked at it, and at the clock.

"I dislike you."

"Useful. Lock channel three."

They locked it with eight seconds to spare. Kade watched from the narrow spectator row with Sera beside him. Across the partition the Kepler pair had built a controller like a clenched fist. It held the first spike almost flat, and their score leapt. Meridian's rig bent under the same spike, wider than Kepler's, and came back without a wobble. Second spike, the same. Kepler held. Meridian bent and recovered.

Then the failure light went red. Kepler's primary controller dropped out of the model, and their line fell off a cliff, bounced back past the safe band, and steadied two seconds later. Meridian's grid handed its load to the undersized sink. Amber flooded the station. Eli's hands went across the panel shaving demand off three minor branches, one, two, three, while the sink drank the transient, and the amber cleared without ever going red.

Kepler was still ahead. "Peak control weighting," Sera said, before he could ask. "Navarro has mentioned it."

On the restart the little sink ran over its listed capacity for three tenths of a second, exactly as promised, and held. Eli hit the cutoff as the primary came back, and the recovery curve settled like a sheet being smoothed. Kepler restarted faster, overshot once, dropped a branch to save the rest, and came back with a peak number the rubric adored.

KEPLER: NINETY-ONE POINT FOUR. MERIDIAN: EIGHTY-EIGHT POINT SEVEN.

A judge approached Eli's station with the face of a person walking into weather. "Your recovery architecture was excellent."

"Then why is their number bigger?"

"They dropped a hospital branch."

"A noncritical branch."

"It was labeled surgical ventilation."

"Within the simulation, the label doesn't alter the weighting."

Eli opened his mouth. His partner put a hand on his shoulder, said, "Thank you, Judge," and walked him out before his respect for procedure took permanent damage.

He was still furious when they got him to a table. Sera put food in front of him.

"The rubric's wrong," he said. "It pays for peak control and not for recovery."

His partner dropped into the chair opposite. "The rubric measures what it says it measures."

"Then it says the wrong thing."

"Which is not the same as us winning."

Eli stabbed the food. "We built the one that comes back."

"Then build it somewhere coming back is what counts," Sera said. "You've already got an application in for exactly that."

Eli stopped with the fork in the air. The anger did not leave. Kade watched it change jobs.

"The fellowship model uses peak retention," Eli said slowly. "Because the academy benchmark uses peak retention. Because I copied the benchmark. I wrote a unit for a nurse in a corridor and then scored it like an arena." The tablet was already in his hands and the food was already forgotten. "I need a recovery metric. Time to safe. Time back from safe. Nobody's scoring that. Why is nobody scoring that?"

His partner looked at Sera. "You shouldn't encourage him at meals."

"I gave him food. What he does near it is his business."

"An institution measured you precisely and missed the point," Kade said. "Welcome to my entire childhood."

Eli stared at him, and then laughed, against his will, and kept typing.

Kade left them to it. It was a real loss, with a number on it, and nothing about it needed him. He found he liked knowing the House had disappointments he was not in.

---

Round two was rank twelve hundred and four, Luis Ortez, who was short and built like a doorstop and plainly regarded Kade's reach as a personal remark.

He shook hands at center and said, pleasantly, "You lean when you think."

"I what?"

"Left shoulder. Tiny bit. Right before you move."

"That's a rude thing to hand someone before a fight."

Ortez smiled, and the barrier sealed.

Kade spent the first exchange thinking about his shoulder. That was the trap, and he was halfway to the strip with Ortez under his guard and driving before he understood that it was one. One to nothing. The shoulder had dipped, too. Less than an inch. Ortez had come in on the signal and not on the opening. He had given Kade a true fact and let Kade's own attention do the rest.

At the reset Kade rolled the shoulder, which only made him more aware of it. Ortez waited with his hands loose.

*You solve the floor you're on,* Julian had said, more than once, usually from above. *Never the next one.*

So Kade changed the question. He could not stop Ortez getting in, not with that much drive that low to the floor. He showed Ortez a lane that looked useful and narrowed at the last step. Ortez took it and arrived where Kade wanted him, and Kade met him with both feet set, and Ortez had enough control going forward that neither of them could score. They broke.

"Still leaning."

"Your concern means a great deal."

On the next mark he let the shoulder dip on purpose. Ortez moved on it, and Kade was already going outside the entry, and touched the band at his back as he went past. One all.

It had worked so well that he did it again, straight away, and that was the oldest mistake he owned in a new shirt. Ortez stopped short, watched Kade rotate toward an attack that was never coming, and tagged his ribs almost gently. Two to one. Kade walked back to center with the heat climbing his neck. One good adjustment had become a favorite answer inside a minute. Hale's seminar was proving irritatingly efficient.

He got the point back with the dullest thing he had: the reach Ortez resented, a straight lead hand to the shoulder band while Ortez was still coming in. Two all. He was starting to be suspicious of clever.

The last exchange came with both of them breathing hard, thirty seconds on the clock, and most of the crowd watching a different floor. Kade showed the same lane with a different end to it. Ortez came. Kade pivoted outside and did not retreat, and Ortez turned with him, and for a moment they were shoulder to shoulder on the edge of the ring, each hauling for a clean line and neither with the leverage to take it. Kade's lead foot was a hand's width from the strip.

And the arena offered itself. It came in at the edge of his awareness the way it always did, politely: the floor under both their stances, the barrier anchor two steps off, the whole of it one connected thing with a line drawn through it that did not have to be where it was.

He left it alone. He let go of Ortez's sleeve. He gave up the better grip, the one thing he had been winning, and took the sudden slack to finish the pivot on balance, and Ortez, still hauling against a hand that was no longer there, went over the strip first.

Three to two, and the horn on top of it.

Two sections over, Julian stood at a rail with his own gloves on, waiting for a call. He had watched the sleeve go. He nodded, once. Kade kept his eyes on the strip walking off, and carried the nod out with him anyway.

"You fixed the shoulder," Ortez said in the tunnel.

"No. I stopped thinking about you telling me."

"That's also useful." Ortez seemed to mean it. "You let go of my sleeve. Nobody lets go."

"I've had a lot of practice leaving."

The board moved his line up again, past a great many names he did not know, and said PROVISIONAL after it.

---

Lunch was on the floor of a Meridian prep room, because every table in the building was covered in brackets or equipment or somebody asleep. Kade sat against the wall between Eli and the door, with Mara, Sera, Priya and Taren in a rough ring round a pile of containers Anton had carried in and then gone back out for more.

For ten minutes nobody mentioned his next bout, and it was the best part of the day so far.

Priya had been assigned a problem set due at eight on the morning after the final. "He called it a defense against tournament-related learning loss."

"Did you tell him resentment is also learning?" Kade said.

"I submitted a formal proof." She held up her tablet. She had retitled the set ON THE CONSERVATION OF SPITE.

Mara read it over her shoulder. "Your third derivation is wrong."

"We were having a social moment."

"It can be wrong socially."

Sera's band had gone from amber to a thin green bar an hour ago, and she had told no one, and everyone knew.

"It's one afternoon," she said.

"You've read the confirmation six times," Mara said.

"Administrative caution." Sera locked the display. "It isn't acceptance. It's an observation slot. I watch one coastal-response drill, and then they decide whether I'm allowed to apply for the actual rotation next term."

"What do you get to watch?" Eli said.

"Command structure. Field triage. Evacuation routing, if they let me near it."

"They'll let you," Priya said. "You make evacuation routing sound like a threat."

Sera looked at the container in her lap. Kade knew what she was looking at. A grease pan, a curtain, two adults with keys on their belts, and a back door she had counted on her first day. She had given him that on a covered walkway with a stopwatch running, and it was not his to hand round the room.

"I got some kids out of a building once," she said, to the rice. "It worked. I had no count, no assigned route, and no idea whether the other rooms were clear. I made the right decision badly. I'd like to find out how it's done properly, by people who do it on purpose."

Mara tapped the locked band with one finger. "They'd be stupid not to take you."

"That was almost sentimental."

"You misheard."

The door banged open, and two Meridian first-years came in with a third hung between them, one shoe dragging. Sera's lunch was on the floor before anyone had spoken.

"What happened?"

"Knee. Went on a boundary stop."

"Medical seen him?"

"Cleared him off the floor, sent us to recovery, didn't say how."

She shifted a stack of guards off the only bench. She did not touch the knee. She asked the boy his name and got it, told the taller carrier to take the ankle and the shorter one to take the weight, and sent Priya ahead to clear the hall. Thirty seconds after the door had opened it closed again behind four people moving at one speed, with less pain among them and no doubt at all about who was doing what.

Eli looked at the band Sera had left beside her rice. "Observation," he said.

Mara took one of Sera's crackers. "Administrative caution."

Taren's wrist lit. He glanced at it and let it go dark. It lit again. He turned his arm over on his knee. The third time, he stood.

"Need some air."

He went out by the inner door, the one that led down toward the service levels and the arena offices and, as far as Kade had ever been able to tell, no air of any kind.

"Interesting air," Kade said.

Mara looked at the door, and then at him. Neither of them got up. It went on the pile with the ankle, and the half second at dinner the night he had not left. The pile was getting tall enough to lean.

Taren was back in nine minutes with coffee for everyone. Nobody had asked him for coffee. Every order was right.

---

Round three was rank eight hundred and eighty-three. Jae Min had cleaner field control than anyone Kade had ever beaten, and he was patient.

Patient opponents were still Kade's least favorite kind. Min did not commit to anything until Kade gave him a reason. Two exchanges went by with nothing in them, a touch offered and declined.

On the third exchange Kade got tired of declining. He went in behind a shoulder check, and Min turned a quarter and sent the check back down through Kade's own stance, and Kade sat down hard on the composite. One to nothing.

As he came up off the floor, the floor changed.

Floor. Anchors. Wall. The lattice under the composite he had once known from a plan nobody read. A connected path, laid out and waiting, and the cold beginning its line from the base of his skull toward the place behind his eyes.

He could reach for it. He knew that now. He also knew the rest of the sentence. The tremor. The eyes arriving late behind the stylus while Park wrote down how late. Force resolving somewhere that neither fighter had chosen.

He let it go. It took a moment to set down, and in that moment Min came in and touched his shoulder band. Two to nothing.

The stands groaned. And somebody, high on the left, shouted, "Use it!"

He heard it quite clearly, and the anger surprised him. It was not that they wanted him to win. They wanted the strange thing. A good part of that crowd had climbed the stairs hoping he would risk himself in an interesting way.

Fine. He would be a disappointment. He had years of training in it.

He reset. Min's control was still better. Min's timing was still cleaner. Wanting harder changed neither fact. So Kade watched the only thing left to watch, which was how Min came down out of his own technique, and after every high-output redirection Min's left heel touched first, a fraction before the rest of him was home. Mara had spent a whole evening teaching him to see exactly that in a person's recovery, and had been insulting about how long it took.

He went at the timing and not at the leg. He pressed to draw the redirection, rode it and did not fight it, and was on Min's chest band in the quarter second the heel was down and the weight was not. Two to one. The fourth exchange was a draw. In the fifth he got impatient again, because impatience was not a thing he had cured, only a thing he now recognized on the way down, and Min put him over the strip. Three to one.

He could not afford another. He stood on his mark and stopped trying to win the exchange in front of him.

He built the next three. A false pressure on the outside, to be read and answered. A retreat a beat later than it should have been, so that Min had to put more field into each redirection to catch him, and a bigger redirection meant a longer landing. He lost nothing doing it, and took a point on the long landing. Three to two. He did it again with the retreat later still, and Min, who was patient but not made of stone, committed a little more, and landed a little longer. Three all.

Last exchange. Kade's rear foot came late on purpose. His center stayed where it was. Min redirected, big, and came down out of it heel first and reset to exactly the spot Kade had spent three exchanges teaching him to reset to, and Kade was already standing in the lane with his hand out.

Four to three.

Some of the applause was disappointed, and he could hear which part. Most of it was not. That helped more than he would have expected.

Dr. Park was at the gate herself this time. She lifted the penlight. "Symptoms."

"I thought about it."

Her attention sharpened behind the light. "And?"

"Put it down. It cost me a point while I did."

"Why did you put it down?"

He looked back through the barrier at the floor, which was only a floor. "I was losing a tournament. I wasn't saving anyone."

Park nodded and wrote. She offered no praise, and he had been around her long enough to know what it meant when Park did not need to say anything.

---

By the fourth round people were coming on purpose. Student media had parked a camera drone at the prep gate, and it turned to follow him like a flower. His projection had climbed under five hundred. He had always preferred being underestimated to being watched, and Asterion had taken that option off the table.

Rank five hundred and three was Hana Bisset, and she was better than he was. He knew it inside the first minute. Her feet were cleaner. Her field came on faster and went off sooner. She did not repeat herself. For three exchanges he learned almost nothing except how many ways a forearm could be made to hurt within the rules, and he was down two to nothing.

Then he saw one small thing. After a successful inside counter, Bisset exited to her left.

He did not trust it. That was Julian's as well. One was an event. Two might be coincidence. Three could be the start of a plan, if you were willing to be wrong about it. She did it again. Then she did not. Then she did.

He folded it into the next exchanges without leaning on it. He scored on reach. She scored on speed and made it three to one. He took one back by going off the angle, finally, under real pressure, the thing he had given two points away that morning for not doing. Three to two. She very nearly put him out, and he stayed in by the width of the strip and scrambled a touch on her back band as she overran him. Three all, and he was drifting left when tired and knew it and could not stop it, and his lungs burned, and the clock went under thirty.

Bisset came inside. Kade gave her the counter she wanted, all of it, and wore it on the forearm that already hurt. She took it, and went out to her left.

He was already there.

Four to three, with six seconds on the clock. He was down on one knee and had no clear memory of getting there. Bisset stood over him with her hands on her hips, heaving for breath, and then she laughed.

"You waited five minutes for that."

"Four and a half."

"That's worse." She put a hand down and he took it. "You got me once."

"It only had to count once."

"It won't next time."

"I was hoping you'd say that."

At the exit gate Armitage was leaning on the rail beside Ortez, with two Kepler students Kade did not know, and Min a row behind them with his arms folded. They had all stayed.

Armitage raised a hand. "You still owe me a shirt."

"It's getting less accurate."

Ortez pointed at Kade's left shoulder. Kade refused to look at it. Ortez laughed out loud. Min only nodded. It was not quite approval. It was what you gave a person whose next bout you intended to watch.

The ladder turned every person on the island into a number. Yet the people he had beaten had not vanished when the score closed. They were still in the building, arguing technique, holding ice to things, reading the next round.

The board rewrote his line. MERCER, KADE. LIVE PROJECTION: TWO HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-SIX. PROVISIONAL.

Eli looked at the number as though it had proposed to him. "The model's stopped treating you as a statistical accident."

"That sounds healthy."

"It's progress."

Sera handed him a towel. "You look terrible."

"Also progress."

Mara arrived with a water bottle and a replay tablet. She had won her own fourth round forty minutes ago, four to one, and had her hair retied already. "You drift left when you're tired."

"I know."

"You're still doing it."

"Those are two different problems."

She handed him the bottle. Their fingers touched on the plastic. Neither of them reacted, which was technically true. Eli turned away with enormous dignity, and Sera smacked the back of his head without looking.

Kade drank. Mara ran the replay back. "You nearly reached for it. Against Min."

"You saw that?"

"Your focus changed. You looked at the floor as if it were a person."

"That's unnerving."

"You didn't use it."

"No."

"Why not?"

He looked down the ramp toward the recovery hall, where Nessa was sitting in the dark. "Because one tournament isn't worth finding out which wall goes next. And because somebody told me to get better at sometimes."

Mara's face eased by about one degree. "Good."

"That was praise."

"Don't make it unpleasant."

His band buzzed. Elena. FOUR WINS. I AM STILL NOT WATCHING.

He typed. YOUR INFORMATION IS SUSPICIOUSLY CURRENT.

NURSES HAVE SOURCES.

Then Darius. ALVAREZ SAYS YOUR FEET LOOK LESS BAD.

He showed Mara. She read it. "Accurate."

He laughed, and for ten minutes, on a bench with a towel round his neck and a forearm going purple, the tournament was very nearly fun.

Then the bracket board refreshed, and Eli made a sound that was not quite a word.

QUARTERFINAL. VEY, MARA, RANK SEVEN. VERSUS MERCER, KADE, LIVE PROJECTION TWO HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-SIX.

Nobody said anything.

"Well," said Sera.

Taren appeared at Kade's shoulder with two cups of coffee and no visible reason to have known which of forty benches they were on. He read the board. "Oh, this is excellent."

"For whom?"

"Everyone but you."

Mara had seen it before any of them. She lowered the replay tablet and turned on the bench, and looked at him.

For four months he had trained beside her, been corrected by her, argued physics with her until a dining hall closed round them. He had trusted her calls on a House Trial floor with a red bar on his own name. She had stopped at a door because he asked her to. The bracket had made all of that two names with a line between them.

Mara Vey smiled. It was not polite, and it was not an accident. It was the smile of a person who had finally been handed a problem she had wanted for months.

He felt his own face answer before he could do anything about it.

Eli looked from one of them to the other. "This is profoundly uncomfortable."

"For you?" Sera said.

"Yes."

"Good."

Kade picked up his towel. "Try not to embarrass yourself, Vey."

One eyebrow. "Mercer."

"Yes?"

"You are projected at two hundred and eighty-six because the system hasn't finished correcting itself."

"That sounded like a threat."

"It was informational."

She had watched all four of his bouts, and had them on the tablet in her lap. Hale had promised it at seven that morning: a favorite answer expired every time it was used. He had used four.

This was going to be terrible. He could not wait.

# Chapter 10 — Vector Class

*Twenty-five. Mom says you told her the thing again and it was about a table. I said a table doesn't count. She said this one did. She also says you've got math at eight and you sound more scared of that than of the people who hit you.*

Vector Analysis was Amadi again, at eight, in a room with no rig in it and nothing on the wall until she put three coupled equations there before half the seats were full.

"Resolve the transfer manifold."

Then she turned and waited, as if the answer were already in the room and only needed collecting.

Styluses moved, forty at once, a dry scratching with no gap in it. Mara Vey, two rows down, had started before Amadi finished turning.

Eli leaned in. "Do you know what a manifold is?"

"Yes."

"Do you know what this one is?"

"No."

"Good. Then neither of us is alone."

Kade looked at the wall. Every symbol on it was one he had seen before somewhere. Put together they were a locked door with the key painted on it. What he could see instead was the thing underneath. Three bodies in a constrained field. One transfer path they were permitted. Two loops that corrected. Loss climbing as the whole arrangement leaned toward the edge where it stopped being an arrangement. He could watch it happen. The first mass shifted, the second answered, and the third inherited a force nobody's notation wanted, the one the equations called secondary while it quietly became the whole problem.

He could have built it out of chairs. He could not write one line of it.

At Northline being ahead of a lesson had been a way to be bored with dignity. This was the other thing. He was behind, in a room where behind was visible from every seat, and some part of him he did not entirely approve of was interested.

"Mercer."

Of course.

"Where does the instability start?"

He answered the diagram and not the equations. "Upper left. Where the second body couples back."

Styluses stopped.

"Why?"

"Because the model lets the second body stay by itself after the first exchange. It doesn't stay by itself. It's carrying what the first one gave it, and the third has to answer that, not the original."

Amadi's face did nothing. "Show me."

The stylus was warm from her hand. He stood at the wall with the room at his back and knew the thing the way you know where a dropped cup will land, and had nowhere to put it. Every symbol he reached for had a second meaning he had never been told. A room like this one remembered a stylus held too long. The seconds went by. He held it.

From the third row, low and unhurried, Mara said, "Lambda three."

He did not look back. He wrote the symbol.

Next term. He put the index where it seemed to belong. At the edge of his vision two of her fingers made a small turning motion, the way you straighten a picture from across a room, and he swapped the index and wrote the next. The thing on the wall began to resemble the thing in his head, and then it was the thing in his head, and he stepped back.

Amadi looked from the wall to him. "Correct."

He walked back to his seat through a silence that knew exactly what it had watched.

"You solved a problem you can't write," Eli said, without moving his mouth much.

"I prefer conceptual elegance."

"You prefer not doing the reading."

Also true.

The next forty minutes were worse in a way he had not planned for. Not humiliation. Inventory. He understood the vectors and mishandled the notation in ways that made the boy beside him flinch. Two of the tensor conventions the room treated as air, he had never seen. His algebra was fast and his proofs were a mood. He skipped steps because the steps were obvious, and in a room where people could actually check, a skipped step was not a shortcut. It was a claim, and nobody could tell whether the claim was insight or error, so they marked it error.

By the end he had four pages of corrections. Mara had one, and he could see it from where he sat, and found that offensive on principle.

The last exercise was a six-operator stabilization grid around a platform that was trying to fall into a simulated sea. Pairs. Amadi read them off a list without looking up, and Kade's name went with Owen Park, House Sol, two thousand eight hundred four, who had the vocabulary Kade lacked and no patience for anything that could not be said in it.

Owen wrote three lines before Kade had finished reading the diagram.

"You're going to overload the southwest anchor," Kade said.

"The field solves symmetrically."

"The bodies don't."

"The exercise assumes equal operator response."

"Then the exercise is lying."

Owen looked up for the first time. "You cannot change the assumptions."

"I'm not. I'm noticing the operators are standing in different places." Kade reached across and dragged one response node three meters east. The platform held two seconds longer and failed somewhere new. He moved another. The failure time doubled.

Something reluctant happened to Owen's expression. "Why?"

"Because your lines think the geometry stops mattering once the field's up. It doesn't stop. It gets harder to see."

Owen studied the model, then Kade. "Write that."

"And there's where the partnership gets difficult."

For ten minutes Owen translated and Kade kept finding assumptions that produced physically stupid outcomes, and they finished third of twenty pairs. Owen stared at the standing as if it had lied to him personally.

"You're infuriating."

"I've had that feedback."

"Do you actually know the notation?"

"No."

"That makes it worse," Owen said, and packed up.

For once, being behind did not feel like being less. It felt like a language problem. A language problem was the kind that came with a book.

Amadi stopped him at the door with one word.

"Transcript."

He stopped.

"It says you completed introductory calculus independently."

"Technically."

"Define technically."

"I passed the assessment."

"That was not the question."

He waited. She waited better.

"I learned enough to pass the assessment," he said.

"And not enough to build the grammar around what you can see." Something arrived on his band with weight in it. "Supplemental modules. Vector notation. Proof structure. Tensor convention C. The Foundations Clinic meets at seventeen hundred under the mathematics wing. You will attend."

He read the length of the list. "Is this a punishment?"

"This is the invoice. You skipped the part of every course that lets other people know whether you are right." She was already turning to the next student. "Here that part is not optional. Nobody in this building will take your word for where a force went."

Mara passed him on the stair, not slowing.

"You wrote the index backward."

"Stylistic choice."

"You guessed. It was a good guess." She kept going. "A guess isn't a method, Mercer. Usual hour."

She did not offer anything more, and he noticed that he had been waiting for her to, and did not care for having waited.

---

Elena called on her break at four, from the corridor outside the ambulance bay where the light was the color of old tape.

He showed her the list.

She read it twice. "You have homework."

"I've always had homework."

"You have homework you intend to do."

"That sounds like an accusation."

"It sounds like a chart I've never read before." The smile took a moment to arrive. "Your father hated notation too."

It came out of her the way the ordinary things came out, and then both of them heard it. She looked down at her hands as if she had put something in them.

"He did?"

Her face tightened one degree. "Engineering school. He used to say if the machine worked, the symbols were showing off."

"That's objectively correct."

"No."

"You're on the side of the symbols?"

"I'm on the side of bridges staying up." Somebody said her name from the bay. She raised one finger without turning. "Go to your clinic. Tell me the thing tomorrow."

The screen went dark. He stood on the bridge with wind in his collar and put the sentence on the pile with the others. A man who hated notation. A man who had been to engineering school. There was a photograph in the hallway at home and an industrial accident in every version of the story, and nobody had ever mentioned school.

*Twenty-six. Mom says you sent her a list before she asked. She says that has never once happened. I said maybe they gave you a different kid. She said she checked.*

---

He had expected remedial work to be kept somewhere decent. A basement. A door with a number instead of a name. Asterion had put the Foundations Clinic behind a glass wall facing the busiest academic bridge on the island, with the lights up, and lettered in brass over the door: FOUNDATIONS ARE LOAD-BEARING.

He stood outside long enough for three people to walk past him. The fourth stopped.

Owen Park had a stack of practice tablets under one arm.

"Are you coming in?"

"Reading the sign."

"It has three words."

"Dense sign."

Owen opened the door and held it, and Kade went through, because refusing in front of Owen would have turned an embarrassment into a decision with a witness.

Fourteen students. Two in first-year gray. The rest in House colors from every year, a fourth-year from Sol relearning fractional exponents at the front table beside a first-year who had apparently missed the whole unit on proofs. Nobody arranged by rank. That alone made it the strangest room he had entered on the island. Everywhere else on Asterion the number decided where you stood.

The person running it was a compact third-year who read Kade's list off a tablet and said, "Batra. I run the room. Notation, proof structure, tensor C."

"When you say them together it sounds worse."

"It is worse. You skipped the parts that tell other people whether you're right." Batra pointed at a table. "Diagnostic set. No field. No hints. Every line shown."

Kade sat across from Owen. "Why are you here?"

"Proof structure."

"You told me to write what I meant."

"I know what a correct proof looks like. I produce ugly ones." Owen squared his tablets into a stack. "Were you hoping the room only had people less prepared than you?"

"No."

He had been.

The first problem was an ordinary load across three supports. He had the answer before he had finished reading, wrote four lines, and stopped.

Batra was beside him. "Initial condition."

"It's in the diagram."

"The diagram is not your proof."

"It's a very involved diagram."

Batra tapped the empty first line and waited until it was not empty.

On the third problem he changed coordinate frames halfway down because the second frame made the answer fall out, and Batra drew a box around the switch without a word.

"Declare it."

"Anyone reading can see what I did."

"Anyone reading shouldn't have to reconstruct your mood."

The fourth was different. A rescue platform hung between two field anchors while a third operator moved an injured person across it. The model solution treated the passenger as a changing load and the three operators as fixed sources. Kade drew the first two arrows and stopped with the stylus in the air.

"Third operator moves differently once the passenger reaches center."

Batra leaned in. "Why?"

"Because by then the left anchor's compensating, and the carrier feels it."

"Write it."

"You people use that as a weapon."

Batra waited. Kade wrote an interaction term, wrong symbol, crossed it out before anyone else could, tried again.

Owen leaned over. "You're putting the carrier inside the control loop."

"The carrier is inside it."

"The problem defines three operators and one load."

"The injured person grabs the carrier at midpoint." Kade pointed at the small line under the diagram, set in a smaller font, the kind of line you skip. "Now the load can change the carrier's balance. The load has hands."

Batra read the line. "Most people treat that as decoration."

"Then most people drop the patient."

The Sol fourth-year had turned around at the front. "Response practical?"

"Bad diagram."

She came over anyway, exponents abandoned. "I ran that practical last year. He's right about the grip. The dummy locks an arm at midpoint and everybody loses the left anchor the first time. Everybody."

Owen studied the interaction term. "Your sign is wrong."

Kade checked. It was. He fixed it, and said nothing about it, and noticed himself saying nothing.

The fourth-year gave them the real carrier position out of her own memory. Owen rebuilt the proof in a cleaner frame. Kade added the correction loop. Batra made all three of them label every assumption, and the result ran twice as long as the model solution and kept the platform level, and Batra sent a copy upstairs to the Response faculty with a note on it.

Around them thirteen people kept working, in the light, behind glass, on things they were supposed to have mastered before anybody let them on the island. There was nowhere in the room for the shame to be private, and after an hour of that it had gone smaller, the way a noise does when everyone can hear it and nobody leaves.

Batra handed him the marked diagnostic on the way out. "Tomorrow."

Outside, Owen turned toward Sol.

"Your proofs are ugly," Kade called after him.

Owen did not turn. "Your coordinate frames are crimes."

It was not friendship. It was beginning to be a language two people spoke.

---

Bay One at the usual hour, lights at half, and Mara's tablet propped on the wall bench where she could see it between passes.

He had never seen her bring work to the mat. She ran the pattern with her eyes going to the screen at every reset. Four steps, turn, a glance, four steps. He fell in beside her and did his own and let the ribs make their remarks.

"Shoulders."

He put them down.

On the sixth pass he looked at the tablet because it was there. A schematic. Two operators in a stabilization frame, a training harness, the kind the acceleration floor used. Two control loops drawn beside it in the faculty's clean hand, each with its own delay, each correcting toward its own target, joined by one arrow that said the frame was shared.

He kept moving and looked at it again on the next pass, and again, and on the ninth he stopped.

"That's wrong."

Her feet did not stop. "It's the faculty set."

"Still wrong."

Now she stopped. "Why."

"It has them correcting independently."

"They do."

"Not after the first correction." He went to the bench and did not touch the tablet. "Delay's in there. Fine. Timing's modeled. Ownership isn't. You compensate for me, my next move starts from a state you made. Then yours starts from mine. Six cycles in, calling that two independent controllers is convenient, and it's a lie."

She came over with her arms crossed, and he watched her read it for the thing he had said, and find it, and not like finding it.

"What would you change?"

"Stop treating them as two operators."

"For part of the calculation."

"Whenever they're actually coupled."

"That creates a shared state."

"Yes."

"Which reduces individual control."

"Maybe."

"It could amplify error."

"Also maybe."

"Dangerously."

"You say impossible too fast."

"You say maybe too comfortably."

Neither of them said anything. Then he said, "The table."

"What about it."

"The mast turn. You stopped. I ran. For three seconds nobody owned that table. It went round you because you'd stopped, and I put my feet where I put them because of where it was going, and where it was going was because of you." He nodded at the screen. "Draw that with two loops. You can't. You'd have me correcting toward my corner and you toward yours and the cup on the roof."

She looked at the schematic for a long time. Then she did a thing he had not seen her do. She saved the screen with a note on it, three words in her narrow hand, and closed the tablet.

"You wrote it down."

"I record anomalies."

"Almost flattering."

"It isn't." She picked her jacket off the mat and did not put it on. "I have to submit that with my name on it. I am not putting my name under a thing I can't defend, and I can't defend Mercer said. Nobody can. Which means you have to be able to write it, and you can't write anything."

"I'm working on that."

"Under glass. With Batra." She weighed something, and it was visibly not light. "Library. Nineteen hundred tomorrow. Bring the diagnostic."

"You're tutoring me."

"I'm protecting an assignment." The jacket went on. "Ask Navarro if you'd rather. He'll build a simulator and something in the library will catch fire."

"Why do you care about the model this much?"

"It's an assignment."

"That's not the answer."

She was at the door. She stood there long enough that he thought she would let it close on the question.

"My parents both work in coupled-field systems," she said, not turning.

He knew the little the public profiles said about the Veys. Famous Resonants. Research. Government contracts. Enough of it that seven at entry looked less like an achievement than a delivery.

"That sounds convenient."

"It isn't." The door closed on it, and he let it, and letting it was new.

---

The library filled six floors inside the base of the Spire and considered books beneath it. Paper occupied one round room behind glass on the fourth floor, which Eli said was Asterion keeping an endangered species where visitors could see it.

Mara had a table by the glass with three texts open flat and a fourth floating above them, and she had been there long enough for the tea to go cold.

The first hour was a fight. She would not let him skip a step. He would not take a symbol without a reason. They argued about coordinate conventions, then sign conventions, then whether a model that hid a physically meaningful assumption inside one clean line had any right to be called elegant.

"Elegance is compression without losing truth."

"Then half of these books are lying beautifully."

"They're simplifying."

"Same family."

She looked at him, and then, unexpectedly, the corner of her mouth went. "Do that again."

"Which part?"

"The last problem. No notation. Say it."

The problem was two operators holding a loaded beam through separate control loops. He leaned back.

"Two people carrying a table through a door. One thinks the table's theirs. The other thinks it belongs to the room. Both wrong. The only thing that matters is where the force is allowed to go when the edge hits the frame."

Something moved in her face and went away again. She turned the tablet to him. "Write it."

He did. Badly. She circled three things and did not say what they were. He did it again, less badly, and again, and the library's lights went warmer on their own, which was how the building told you the evening had started.

At twelve past eight his attention simply left. He read one line four times and it stayed a line. He wrote lambda where he meant mass.

"Break."

"I'm fine."

"You just wrote lambda where you meant mass."

"Creative notation."

"Break."

The vending alcove had fruit, protein, coffee, six teas, and one candy bar priced as an insult. Mara bought tea. He bought the candy bar because resentment had a limit and the limit was there. They sat on the floor under the window with the walkways glowing blue-white below.

"Did you always know you were coming here?" he asked.

"To Asterion."

"Yes."

"No." She drank. "My family assumed."

"That sounds like yes."

"It's different."

"How?"

"A choice can go invisible if everyone around you expects you to make it."

That was too exact to be casual. He turned the candy bar over in his hands. At Northline he had been the kid who pushed on a sentence like that until it gave, because the push was the only leverage he had ever been handed.

"Did you want to?"

She looked at the glass. "Yes." A pause with weather in it. "I think."

He nodded and said nothing. No joke. No question about her father. No push.

She glanced over. "You're not going to say anything?"

"Learning boundaries."

Her eyes narrowed. "That was terrible."

"Still true."

She almost smiled and got up, and they went back.

She cleared the display. "The beam again. No diagram. You make it."

She read the conditions and he drew. Two operators. One loaded beam. Unequal field strength. A sensor on the east support that reported late. A limit on how fast either operator could change output without losing coherence. He drew the beam, then the forces, then two control loops, and she watched with her hands flat on the table and helped with nothing.

He added the sensor delay and started the first transfer equation. His notation survived two whole lines before he used the same symbol for output and correction.

She circled both. "You knew what I meant."

"I knew there were two meanings and guessed which one kept the beam up."

"Correct guess."

"Not a defense."

He changed the symbol. On the fifth line the east operator's correction arrived late. The west operator corrected the correction. He started a third loop for the crossover, stopped, and erased the line between the first two instead.

Mara leaned forward. "Why did you do that?"

"Because neither loop owns the beam anymore."

"They still control their own output."

"Yes. And every choice they make starts from a state the other one helped make."

She looked at the blank space where the dividing line had been. "Separate control. Shared state."

"That."

"That is not notation."

"I was hoping you wouldn't notice."

She took the display back and did not restore the line. She drew a bracket around both loops, and inside it wrote each operator's limit on its own, separately, so that the bracket held two people and not one.

He watched the symbols go on. "You can write that?"

"Provisionally."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning Amadi can tell me why it's wrong in the morning."

Mara Vey had written uncertain mathematics on purpose. He did not say anything clever. He could not think of anything cleverer than what she had just done.

"Now derive the correction," she said, "without changing frames halfway."

The first try failed because the bracket hid which operator hit limit first. She did not fix it. She said, "What did you lose?" and he put the individual limits back. The second failed because his delay term described the sensor and not the person reacting to it. He wrote both. The third held. Not elegant, not fast, and every assumption in it visible, including the one he had wanted to keep inside the picture in his head where nobody could argue with it.

She read it through. "Acceptable."

He heard the word. So did she. He watched it land in her a half second after she had said it, her father's word in her own mouth, and she did not take it back. She closed the book.

House Meridian treated homework as a contact sport, and he found this out at twenty-two hundred when he came down with two pages and a bracket and found every table taken.

First-years filled the long table. Two second-years fought about orbital mechanics under the window. Nessa Kim had the floor with a perception exercise spread around her in colored tiles, and somebody in the kitchen was whispering vocabulary into a recorder while stirring noodles. Sera sat on a couch with an emergency-procedure manual and one boot on the table. Eli had a pocket projector in pieces across three cushions.

"I need a surface."

Eli swept the pieces into a heap. "Academic emergency."

"Mara says ideas need notation."

The near end of the room got quieter. Taren looked over from the long table. "Mercer has been assigned scholarship," he told the first-years, who had not asked.

Kade sat. The title on his page said DEPENDENT CORRECTION IN COUPLED OPERATOR SYSTEMS, in capitals that leaned. Under it were three false starts and one paragraph that got worse every time he read it.

Eli leaned over. "What are you trying to say?"

"That two operators stop being independent when their corrections change what the other one has to correct."

"That sentence was free. Why are the next two pages hostile?"

"Notation."

Eli picked up the projector. "Simulator."

"Mara predicted this."

"Mara respects engineering."

"She predicted a fire."

"Same field."

Two white circles came up over the table, each tied to a blue square. Move a circle, the square followed. "Independent," Eli said. He tied both circles to one square and it jittered between them like a dog with two owners. "Coupled and terrible."

Kade nudged the delay. The jitter slowed, then grew. "Because both corrections land after the other one's already changed the state."

"So give one priority." Eli handed the left circle authority and the square went still beneath it.

"That fixes it by making the second operator decorative."

"Efficiently decorative."

Sera lowered the manual. "What's the task?"

"Keep the target stable," Eli said.

"For what?"

Eli looked at Kade. Kade looked at the simulation. "The model doesn't say."

Sera went back to the manual. "Then it isn't a task."

"You read one handbook and you're a philosopher."

"Page forty-three. A response plan without a success condition is movement with uniforms."

Eli turned the square into a stretcher. "Fine. Keep the patient level through a doorway."

Sera put the manual down and stood. "Now neither carrier's decorative."

She took the right circle. Eli took the left. The stretcher went to the door and hit the frame.

"Your side was high."

"Your side went in first."

Again. Eli slowed at the door, Sera turned, the stretcher cleared, and the patient marker slid toward the foot. Kade watched their hands, not the screen. Each controlled an end. Neither could do it alone. On the roof, with a full cup on a table, he had known that with his arms.

He reached in and moved both limits inside one bracket. Two inputs. One success condition. "Again."

Eli looked at the revised model. "Who controls the shared state?"

"Both of you."

"That is not how control works."

"Maybe that's the problem."

Sera was looking at him, not the projection. "Do we still choose our own movement?"

"Yes."

"Then we're not one operator."

"Not one person." He moved the bracket a finger's width so both limits showed inside it. "One active system."

The phrase stopped something in his head. He sat with it while Eli ran it again. With the shared state on the screen, each of them could see the load the other had just made. Sera turned before the frame. Eli dropped his end to hold level. The stretcher went through touching nothing and the patient marker stayed centered.

Eli stared at it. "That's either obvious or illegal."

"Mara said dangerous."

"Promising."

Sera sat down. "In a real carry, shared information doesn't remove separate responsibility. My end is still my end. If I drop it, that's mine."

Kade wrote it down. She caught him at it. "Delete that."

He kept writing.

The room went back to its arguments. Taren set a packet of noodles beside Kade's elbow without asking whether he wanted them. He finished the first page, then the second. Eli marked the line where the machine state changed without a cause. Sera struck a sentence that said the load stayed passive. Nessa came off her tiles, read the diagram upside down, said that the two operators would not perceive the shared change the same way, and went back.

By midnight four people had corrected the paper Mara had taught him to write. His name was still at the top. It no longer looked like the only name on the page that mattered.

Amadi returned the problem set at eight. Mara's was perfect. His was not, and he had improved enough to be annoyed by exactly where the points had gone. At the foot of the last page, under a long red correction, one line.

*You understand more than your notation suggests. Fix the notation before it limits the understanding.*

He read it three times and folded the page into his bag instead of the bin.

That afternoon she assigned a team exercise using the same two-operator harness model. Mara's group changed the assumptions. The simulation score came up twelve percent over the next best, and Amadi stared at the number.

"Vey. Explain."

Mara stood. For the first time since Kade had met her, she glanced at him before answering an instructor. Not for permission. Acknowledging where it came from. Then she explained the shared-state correction in clean mathematics he could almost follow.

Amadi listened all the way through. "Interesting. Dangerous if generalized."

"Yes."

"Mercer. Did you contribute to this?"

"Mara did the math."

"That was not the question."

He looked at the model. "I said the system changed once they started correcting each other."

Amadi studied him a while. "Write a note on it."

"I did."

Mara looked down at her table to hide what was a smile.

"Then submit it. Two pages. Proper notation."

Eli, beside him, whispered, "You invented homework." Across the room Mara mouthed one word at him. *Progress.* He hated her a little, and the feeling was getting suspiciously comfortable.

---

Two days later Amadi tested whether the improvement existed when Mara was not in the row behind him.

She called it a timed technical recitation. Kade called it an ambush with stationery. Teams of four, a failing stabilization system, twenty minutes to diagnose it, write the governing transfer equations, and propose a correction before the structure crossed its threshold. Mara was on the far side of the room. Eli's team had the mezzanine, out of signaling range, and Kade suspected the seating had not been an accident.

His team was Owen, Nessa, and a feeder-program first-year who had been writing tensor notation since roughly birth and had her stylus moving before the timer did.

The north support began to oscillate.

She wrote. Owen checked constraints. Nessa watched the live field trace with her whole face still. Kade watched the structure.

For thirty seconds everybody did the thing they were best at. Then the model began failing faster.

"Your damping's late," Kade said.

The first-year did not look up. "It's synchronized to the field update."

"The field update's synchronized to the sensor."

"Yes."

"The structure moves before the sensor finishes saying so."

Owen looked at him. "Write what you mean."

The old version of him would have pointed at the simulation and expected the pointing to count. He picked up the stylus. First line, wrong subscript. Owen tapped it. He fixed it and did not defend it. Second line. Third. He wrote the delay term Mara had drilled into him under the warm library lights, the sensor and the person reacting to it, both.

The first-year stopped writing. "Oh."

It was a satisfying sound. They changed the model. Failure time went up. Not enough.

"West operator's compensating twice," Nessa said, eyes on the trace.

He saw it then. Two people solving the same correction on their own. The coupled-state problem, wearing a different diagram.

"Treat west and center as one loop for the next cycle."

"That isn't standard," the first-year said.

"No."

"It could destabilize both."

"Yes."

Owen looked at the clock. "Eight minutes."

The first-year looked at Kade. "Can you write it?"

That was the question. Not can you see it. Not can Mara say it for you. Can you write it, in a form somebody else can check, before the floor goes.

He wrote it. Slower than she would have. Cleaner than two days ago. Owen corrected a sign. He fixed the sign and kept going, and Nessa read the trace and called the west limit as it changed, and they submitted with forty-three seconds on the clock, and the structure held. Not perfectly. It held.

Second. Mara's team first, of course.

He looked at the standing, and second bothered him, and then he saw why, and sat with it. He had expected to compete. Not survive. Compete.

Amadi stopped at their table and read his equations with one finger, and the finger stopped on the corrected sign.

"Park?"

Owen nodded.

"Good."

"You're praising him for correcting me."

"I am praising the system for not depending on your pride."

The first-year laughed out loud.

Amadi put his two-page note on the table beside the submission. Red across the first page like weather. Less on the second.

"Your shared-state model is unfinished."

"I gathered that from the climate."

"You keep the limits separate. Good. You define a common state. Interesting. You do not define success until halfway down page two."

He tapped the line Sera had made him add. "It's there."

"After the mathematics begins."

"The mathematics explains the task."

"The task decides whether the mathematics is any use."

*Movement with uniforms.* He could hear Sera say it with her boot on the table.

"So I move that to the top."

"And?"

He looked at the submission. Owen's corrected sign. Nessa's limit call. The first-year's clean damping term. His own delay term, holding the middle of it. Four names, no single author, and a floor that had not gone.

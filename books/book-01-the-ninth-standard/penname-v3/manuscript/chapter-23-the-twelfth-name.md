# Chapter 23 — The Twelfth Name

Professor Halden held his practical sections in the Hall of Standards, on the principle that nobody should be allowed to study public history sitting down.

It was two days after the Open. Fifty first-years stood about in pairs along the curved wall at the eastern base of the Spire, under eleven bronzes lit from below, and tried to look as though they had not been reading the recalculated ladder on the walk over. Kade's forearm had gone from plum to a yellowish green that Eli called encouraging. Twice already somebody had looked at him, and then at a tablet, and then at him again, matching a face to two hundred and eleven. He had spent four months as the bottom line of that ladder. He had not expected to miss how restful it was.

Everybody knew the eleven faces. They were in airports and on stamps. Chen Wei had rain cast into his hair. Nadia Okonkwo wore the collar ring of an orbital suit. Amara Sen was fifth from the left, twenty-six years old, regarding the sculptor as if he owed her a citation.

"Each pair has a bronze, a plaque, and a records packet," Halden said. He was a narrow man who walked the curve with his hands clasped behind him, like a visitor who had been warned not to touch. "Footnote the plaque. Every clause gets a record, and every record gets its subject's Authority identifier. A clause you cannot footnote comes out. When you have finished, you will tell me two things. What did the plaque lose? And did it lie? You have twenty minutes. Those are different questions, and I will be able to tell if you think they are the same one."

Kade drew Chen Wei, and a Kepler boy with a very new tablet, who stood looking up at the bronze for some time before he opened anything.

"I had him on my wall," the boy said. "The poster, with the rain. He's why I applied."

"I'm not here to take him off your wall."

"People say you argue with everything."

"People exaggerate. I skip meals sometimes."

The plaque was one sentence long. Chen Wei held the failing spillway at Lanzhou for nineteen minutes and saved the valley below.

The packet was four pages of operations log in narrow columns. There were gate technicians and municipal engineers. There was a dispatcher who had turned a hospital convoy onto the high road six minutes before anybody confirmed that the dam was failing. There were six village crews who had opened the auxiliary channels by hand. Chen came in halfway down the fourth page, beside a field-output estimate and a medical override he had ignored.

The first clause footnoted beautifully. Held, spillway, nineteen minutes. The log had it to the second, and the medical record had what it cost. The Kepler boy typed the citations with real satisfaction.

Kade took the second clause, and could not make it stick to anything.

"Saved," he said. "Find me the line where Chen saves the valley."

"He held the dam."

"He held the spillway. The clause says saved. Who does the log give that to?" He ran his finger down the columns. It was a system, and he knew how to question a system. "Dispatch moved the low ground first. The gate crews took pressure off the wall. The villages gave the water somewhere to go. Chen held on until the level dropped below the crack. He bought nineteen minutes. Everyone else on these four pages spent them."

"So the plaque's a lie." The boy said it as if Kade had kicked something.

"No. Nineteen minutes on that wall should have killed him, and every model says so. All of that's true." Kade tapped the bronze sentence. "There are two verbs up there and he's got both. There are four pages of people and not one of them gets to do anything."

The boy went back into the log. He was quiet for a while. "The dispatcher hasn't got a name in here," he said. "Just a desk number."

"Then footnote the desk."

Halden reached them with six minutes left and listened with his head on one side. Lost, everyone who spent the time. Lied, no.

"No? I was promised you would be more trouble than that, Mercer."

"It's short. Short isn't the lie." Kade hunted for the rest of it. "It would be a lie if being short changed what happened."

"Changed who acted, or who paid, or what made the thing possible. Yes." Halden looked along the wall. "A plaque has an edge. So has a visitor's attention, and a classification law, and whatever the survivors will sit still for. Something always fails to get across. I do not mark you on whether the wall is short. I mark you on whether you can say what it cost to make it short, and who was charged."

He moved on. The Kepler boy said, a little stiffly, that they still needed Chen's identifier for the footnotes. Kade opened the old Authority registry, the open one, which any student login could read and no student did, because it was a list of numbers.

The Standard Eight block was a single short page. He copied Chen's line. Then he counted, because he had been counting things all morning and his eye was in.

There were twelve lines. He counted again, slowly, with a finger. Eight-dash-one through eight-dash-twelve, all issued on the same date. Eleven of them had names he had known since he was nine. The twelfth had its number and its date and nothing else.

He had seen that column before. It had been in Amadi's legacy calibration matrix, in his first month, a twelfth heading nobody would name. He had seen it on the founders' board on Meridian's roof, where eleven medallions hung beside one empty mount. And he did not have to turn his head to know what stood past the last bronze. He had counted these bays months ago, on his way to break a floor. There were twelve. The last held a flat panel, painted to match the stone and not quite matching, with a tag that said ACCESS SEALED.

He had stacked all three in the same corner of his head and carefully never looked at the stack.

"That's reserved," the Kepler boy said, reading over his arm. "They set aside a block before they knew how many there'd be. Everybody knows that."

"Who's everybody?"

"It's." The boy stopped. "I don't know, actually. Somebody told me."

Kade asked Halden as the pairs were breaking up. Halden did not even slow down. "Legacy schema. The Authority reserved twelve before anyone knew how many would qualify. Eleven did."

"Professor Amadi told me that in October. In nearly those words."

"Then it has the virtue of consistency."

"Or you both got it from the same place. What's the footnote?"

Halden stopped walking. His mouth opened, and nothing came out of it, and his hands came slowly from behind his back.

"I was told it as a graduate student," he said at last. "In a corridor, by a man I admired. I have been repeating it for nineteen years." He looked down the curve to the painted panel, and Kade had the impression that he was seeing it for the first time in a good while. "That is a deeply irritating question, and I want it noted that I taught it to you this morning."

"Is it reserved?"

"I don't know. I have just discovered that I don't know." He put his hands behind him again, with some care. "A careful historian does not fill a gap with whatever she would most like to find there. She also does not call it empty because a man in a corridor said so. If you footnote that line, Mercer, I should like to read it."

The bell went, and the Hall emptied toward lunch.

---

Kade did not go to lunch. He sat down on the stone bench across from bay twelve with the registry open on his knee.

There was a cross-reference field at the bottom of the page. It took any historical subject code. He had one. It had come off the precursor archive cards, and Eli had tidied it into a file labelled AARON, VERIFIED, because Eli labelled everything.

All his life he had wanted into every locked room he met. This one had a handle, and he sat and looked at it.

His father had been a finished story for seventeen years. There had been an accident, a box of effects, and a man who burned rice and hummed without a tune. For four months the story had been coming unfinished, a piece at a time. His father had been a Zero. He had been a name on a precursor roster, and a transfer order dated twenty-three days too early, and a lie Sen had told to Kade's face. There ought to have been a limit on how much a dead man could go on changing.

He got as far as opening a message to Eli. *Can you run something for me.* He looked at it. He knew what it was. He wanted somebody else's hand on the control, so that whatever came back would be a thing that had happened to him. He deleted it and typed the code himself.

The screen showed a metadata conflict and hung on it. Then an old cache resolved underneath.

MERCER, AARON J. HISTORICAL CLASSIFICATION: STANDARD VIII. STATUS: SEALED.

"Standard Eight," Kade said aloud. None of the eleven bronzes answered him.

Nothing in the Hall changed. The ventilation ticked. Somebody's shoes squeaked away beyond the west door. In the time it took to read one line, his father had become one of the dozen most capable people in the history of the field, and the lights under Chen Wei did not so much as flicker.

He tried to put a twelfth face on that wall and could not do it. He had no older face to use. He had two photographs of a young man who hated being photographed, and his mother said the eyebrows were Kade's. That face belonged in one front room of one small apartment. Three meters of bronze was the wrong size for it, and the wrongness was what hurt.

He wanted to be angry, because anger was clean and he knew how it went. Something else kept getting in its way, and it took him a moment to place it as embarrassment. It was not for himself. Halden had said *eleven did* an hour ago, in perfectly good faith. The Kepler boy had said it. Kade had said it himself at nine, reading it out of a schoolbook. Nobody had needed a million liars. A few people had taken out the evidence early enough, and everyone after them had told the clean version honestly.

He found he was standing up. He was at the east door with his hand on it, and Sen's office was six minutes away across a bridge.

He stopped there. He made himself look at what he was carrying. It was one cached record, and a wish so large that he would have accepted the same name written on a sticky note.

Two nights ago he had written in capitals on a form that there ought to be a condition that proved him wrong, and Mara had taken the stylus out of his hand and added a second. *You should have more than one way to be wrong.*

He went back to the bench and wrote it properly.

PREDICTION: THE TWELFTH STANDARD EIGHT IDENTIFIER BELONGS TO AARON MERCER.

CONDITION THAT PROVES THIS WRONG: THE NUMBER EVER HAD ANOTHER OWNER. OR A LATER MIGRATION RENUMBERED THE RUN. OR ANY INDEPENDENT RECORD DISAGREES.

He sent it to himself for the timestamp. It said eight minutes past noon.

The harder part came after. If he walked into Meridian and said the name, three of the sharpest people he knew would go looking for Aaron Mercer. People who went looking for a thing generally found it. He wanted them to go looking for a blank, and he wanted to see what they brought back.

"You missed lunch."

Taren was coming down the curve with a cup in each hand. "Priya said you were in here communing with the ancestors." He sat, and handed one across. It was the right coffee. It always was. "Which one did you draw?"

"Chen Wei."

"Good one. Very wet."

The tablet lay face down on the bench between them. Taren's eyes went to it once and came away. Then he talked for three minutes about Julian's medal, and a rooming dispute in Kepler that he was apparently mediating, and whether the kitchen could be talked out of the fish. He never asked what Kade had been reading.

Taren asked everybody everything. It was how he worked. Kade sat and drank coffee beside a closed question, and Taren, of all people, did not reach for it.

Taren's band buzzed. He glanced down. For about as long as a blink the easy face was gone, and what was under it looked like a person doing a sum he did not like.

"Scheduling?" Kade said.

"Always." The face was back. Taren stood, and knocked twice on the bench for luck, and went out by the west door, whistling.

Kade was halfway down the cup before he remembered that Priya had lab on the far side of the island until two. Somebody might have told her where he was. Somebody usually had. He put it on the pile with the taped ankle, and the interesting air, and the fourth document in a list of six. The pile no longer fit where he kept it.

---

He sent the same message to three people. NEED THREE PEOPLE WHO WON'T TAKE MY WORD FOR SOMETHING. OUR ROOM, FOUR.

THAT IS EVERYONE YOU KNOW, Mara sent back.

Sera wrote, BRINGING FOOD. NOT FOR YOU.

Eli wrote, I LIVE THERE.

At four he put the registry page on Eli's desk and said nothing at all. Mara got there first. She had spent the morning on the good tape teaching him to stop announcing a sore arm with his whole face, and her shoulder was still stiff from Julian, and she counted with her chin.

"Twelve lines."

"Eleven names," said Eli. He had gone very alert. "That isn't administrative. It can't be. These are permanent. They're assigned once and never reused. You can die, retire, be disgraced, be classified, and the slot stays yours. It's the one thing the Authority ever got right. There isn't a mechanism for an empty one."

"So somebody was taken off," Sera said, from the bed, around a sandwich.

"Hidden," Mara said. "Taking them off would have closed the gap."

"I ran one lookup on that line at noon," Kade said. "I got a name. I'm not going to tell you what it is."

Three faces turned to him. He had expected that. He had not expected how much he would dislike standing in front of it.

"I want this one too much. I'd have believed it off a napkin. If I tell you, you'll go and look for that name, and you're good, so you'll find it. I want you to go and look for whoever's there. Three roads. Public sources. If you all come back to the same place without me pointing at it, then it's a place."

"And if we don't?" Sera said.

"Then I've got a cache error and an afternoon I'd like back."

Mara was looking at him without any dryness at all. "You realize this is withholding. You have a well-documented opinion about people who do that to you."

"Yes. It's why I'm telling you that I'm doing it, and what it's for. It ends at nine tonight whatever you find. And you can say no."

"An owner, a reason, and a clock," Sera said. "That's better than the Director manages. I'm in."

Eli had his hand half up. "I mean, I think we can all guess."

"Don't," Mara said. "A guess all three of us share is one source wearing three coats."

They divided it in under a minute, which told him they had wanted to. Eli took the technical road. "Amadi's legacy matrix had twelve subject columns. There's an open schema. I don't have to ask anybody." Mara took paper. "The Spire public archive holds the Authority's bound technical indexes. Print is harder to migrate." Sera finished her sandwich and took the wall. "There's a bay down there cut for a bronze that isn't in it. Facilities records are public. I read building systems for pleasure now, apparently."

She pointed the crust at each of them in turn. "Public doors only. The first time anything tells you no, you stop. You write down what said it, and you walk away. Eli."

"Why is it always *Eli*?"

"You know why."

Mara stopped at the door. "And you?"

"My mother."

"Then ask her a question that hasn't got the answer inside it."

---

He took the call up to Meridian's roof, to the founders' board, where eleven small medallions had gone green in the salt air beside one bare mount. The wind was rising. Out over the water the cloud had begun to stack up.

Elena answered from the ambulance bay at St. Vincent, in scrubs, with a jacket over them and her hair coming down. "Is it the arm?"

"The arm's green. Eli says that's good."

"Eli's right. So what is it?"

"It's a strange question. When Dad talked about the work. The people he worked with. Did he ever say how many there were?"

She frowned at him. "That is a strange question." Then her face changed, and the answer came before she had gone looking for it. "Twelve."

Kade put his hand flat on the bare mount.

"He said it one time. This was before you were born. He'd been having nightmares, and I asked him what about, and he said there were twelve people in the room when everything changed. Those words. I asked what changed. He got up and burned some rice at two in the morning."

"You're sure of the number."

"I'm a nurse. I remember numbers that people say at night." She pulled the jacket closed. "Kade. Why?"

So he told her. He told her about eleven faces and twelve lines, and the lookup at noon. He turned his band so that the camera took in the cached record, and watched her read it. Her hand came up to her mouth.

"Oh," she said.

She said it as she did after a bad call at work. Nobody could have faked it.

After a moment she said, "You asked me for the number before you showed me that."

"Yes."

"So I couldn't get it from you."

"Yes. I'm sorry."

"Don't be. It's what I'd do with a patient. I didn't say I enjoyed it." She almost smiled. It went. "Standard Eight. He never told me. Nobody did. He said once that they hadn't got a box for what he did. He thought that was funny. Then he stopped thinking it was funny, and then he stopped talking about the work at all."

The Standards had not fit, and somebody had filed Aaron under Eight regardless. An institution had needed a box, and it had used the nearest one.

"Everybody on earth knows the Eleven," Kade said. "He should have been."

"I know." She looked away, at something in the bay. "I've been going over it since you found that file. I was twenty-four. You were asleep in the next room. People with government credentials sat at my table and told me attention would make things worse. I let the story get small because small felt safe. I'm not proud of it. With you in the next room, I would probably do it again."

Kade thought of a boy at Northline, making everything small before it could matter. He had spent the whole day hunting for a family resemblance. He did not enjoy finding it there.

He had not known he was going to say the next thing. "If this comes out, he goes on a wall. There'll be documentaries. Governments will explain him, and people will decide what he meant. I've got two photographs and burned rice, Mom. Everybody gets the bronze before I've got the man."

He had spent years resenting how small his father's story was. It had not occurred to him that a huge one could be a theft as well.

"Then get the man first," Elena said. "You're allowed to want that. It isn't the same as hiding him."

"That's what Sen would say."

"Amara Sen decided for me, for seventeen years, and never asked. You'd be deciding for yourself, for a week, out loud. If you can't tell those apart, ask Sera. I'm told she's loud." A pager went off against her hip. She silenced it without looking. "Go to her, Kade. Not alone, and not guessing."

"That's the plan."

"Call me after."

"I will."

The first rain reached the roof as the band went dark. It ran down the eleven medallions, and down the bare mount as well.

---

At nine the Hall was empty and lit only from below. Rain ticked on the high windows. Sera was already there, cross-legged on the floor in front of bay twelve with a building plan across her knees. Mara came in with a folder of photographed pages under her good arm. Eli came last, at something close to a run.

"I need to say one thing first," he said. "I rang a bell."

"Then you go first," said Sera.

He had found the open schema for the legacy calibration tables. There were twelve subject columns. Eleven mapped to public identifiers. One mapped to eight-dash-twelve and to nothing else. He turned the screen so that they could read the headings above the columns. COUPLED STABILITY. MULTI-SUBJECT TRANSFER. COHERENCE DURATION.

The back of Kade's neck went cold, and for once it was only his skin. *Coupled* was the word from Vector Class, from the night he had told Mara to stop treating two operators on one beam as two. It turned up whenever somebody near him tried to describe things that would not stay separate.

"Multi-subject," he said. "These weren't twelve people being tested. It was twelve people being tested as one thing."

"It gets better. Or worse." Eli brought up a surviving public paper from the precursor years. Its caption listed eleven participant traces. The graph under the caption had twelve colors, and the twelfth was labelled CONTROL ARTIFACT. "That isn't an artifact. Watch it. When the others destabilize, it climbs. When they settle, it comes down. It's answering them. Noise doesn't care what the other lines are doing. That's a person. Somebody changed his label and left him in the data."

"Why leave him in at all?" said Sera.

"Because you can take a man out of a biography. You can't take him out of the arithmetic. Every average in that table is divided by twelve. Pull the column and nothing balances."

"And the bell," Mara said.

"I asked for the study underneath." Eli held up a hand. "A permitted request. It said ACCESS DENIED. Under that it said something I've never seen on a student terminal. QUERY LOGGED. OVERSIGHT NOTIFICATION GENERATED. So I stopped. I wrote down what said it, and I walked away. Sera, I'd like that acknowledged."

"Acknowledged. When?"

"Twenty to eight."

"So for an hour and twenty minutes, somebody has known a student asked."

Nobody said anything to that. Mara opened her folder.

She had spent four hours with the archive librarian and a trolley of bound indexes. The rule was there in print. Identifiers were issued once and were not reissued on death, retirement, revocation, or reclassification. The Standard Eight block had been issued on a single date, all twelve. For six years the twelfth identifier turned up in the listings of restricted technical publications, under a generic subject code. Then the references stopped, all in one quarter. The first public list of Standard Eights came out two years later.

"The drafts of that list are on deposit," she said. "I read every one. It's eleven from the first printing. Nobody edited him out afterward. The Eleven were built as eleven." She laid down a last page. "And the old registry has a checksum, printed in ink in a bound volume. Whoever designed this expected the computers to die before the bureaucracy did."

"Read me the subject code," said Eli.

She read it. Eli checked it against the heading of his twelfth column. It was the same code. Then he ran the printed checksum against the registry cache. It passed through three archive migrations and two signatures in a standard nobody had used for twenty years, and it held.

"Could it be forged?" Sera asked.

"Anything can be. You'd have to rewrite three separate archives, and one of those went to the Authority before this academy existed. Then you'd have to reprint a book. It would be a lot more work than hiding one man."

Sera unfolded her plan against the stone. It was the precursor building plan, out of the public facilities record. The curved wall was older than any portrait on it. It had been drawn with twelve bays, and every bay had four load anchors and one conduit run for the environmental controls.

"This one has its anchors. It's got its conduit as well. They never pulled it. It just stops, behind the panel." She brought up the public maintenance register. Eleven device addresses reported illumination, climate, and proximity, all normal. A twelfth sat at the end of the run. It was labelled ARCHITECTURAL FILL, and its power draw was zero. "Fill doesn't get an address. This stone expects power."

The renovation ledger was the last thing. Eleven portrait assemblies had been transferred to the new mounts. One assembly had been removed, disposition sealed. She read out the date. It fell two years before the first public list, inside the same two years as Mara's silence. Two archives that had never heard of each other disagreed with the public story in the same place.

"Mine proves a portrait came down," Sera said. "It doesn't say whose. I'd like that on the record before anyone gets excited."

Kade put his palm flat on the panel. It was cold. There was no seam and no scar, nothing to say that anyone had ever been there, which would have been the point.

"Can it be woken?" He heard himself say it.

"Probably," said Eli.

"That's the question I walked down here to say no to," Sera said. "One bell is enough for tonight. That feed hasn't been drawn on in a quarter of a century, and I've read how those stories end."

He did want it. He knew the wanting. It was what he had felt with his back to the east post, when a thing was available and ten thousand people were stamping for him to use it. A lit recess would have told him nothing that three people had not already told him better. He took his hand off the stone before she had to say it twice.

"Names," Mara said. "Written. Turned over together. Sera, label yours."

They wrote. Kade put his own tablet on the bench, face down, with the form on it. Mara counted three, and four screens turned over.

MERCER, AARON J., said Eli's. It was the cache line, with the checksum result under it.

AARON MERCER, said Mara's. Underneath, smaller, she had written, SUBJECT CODE MATCHES PRECURSOR CARDS, MY OWN SEARCH.

A. MERCER, said Sera's. GUESS. THE WALL DOESN'T SAY.

Kade's form said what it had said since eight minutes past noon.

For a while there was only the rain. Eli had no joke. He started twice. "I'm sorry," he said finally. "I don't know if that's the right thing. He's one of the twelve most capable people there has ever been. And he's your dad. I spent all afternoon calling him a column."

"It was a good column," Kade said, not very steadily.

Mara went down the form with one finger. "No other owner. No renumbering. No record that disagrees. Those were your three." Her voice changed, only a little. "My father knew your family's name before I ever said it to him. The Director lied to your face. The history I grew up reciting was built around a hole. I wanted to be careful with that. Thank you for making us."

Kade felt something in his chest settle. It was certainty. He had expected that certainty would feel better than it did.

He looked up the curve. Sen stood fifth from the left, twenty-six and owed a citation. She had posed for that. She had stood still for a sculptor, knowing exactly how many bays were in this wall.

"Whoever heard my bell," Eli said.

"I'm not waiting for them to come and find us."

In a concrete stairwell Sen had once given him an index, a name against every hole, and he had promised to stand in front of each one. It had not crossed his mind that one of the holes would be shaped like his father. He opened the index and filed against it. He attached the form, and Eli's graph, and Mara's printed page, and Sera's ledger. In the request field he wrote, I AM NOT ASKING WHETHER. I AM ASKING WHY.

The answer came in under a minute. I AM IN MY OFFICE. BRING WHOEVER HELPED YOU.

"She was waiting," Mara said.

"She heard the bell." Sera was folding her plan. "*Whoever helped* includes me. I checked the grammar."

"Why would you want to come?"

"If an oversight team turns up, I want one person in that room who looks for the exit before the argument."

"My first instinct is technical curiosity," said Eli.

"Yes. That's why it isn't you."

---

They took the pedestrian bridge through the rain. It came in sideways off the water and drummed on the canopy. Sera went a step ahead. Kade had Mara on one side and Eli on the other.

"What do you ask her first?" Mara said.

"Not whether she knew him. I've got three sources for that, and a wall." He watched the lit windows of the administration building come up through the wet. "Who decided the world would count to eleven. And what twelve people did in one room that cost one of them his name."

"And after that?"

"After that I want to know who he was. Before somebody tells me what he represents."

"That seems reasonable."

"Careful."

"I said reasonable. I didn't say she'd make it easy."

Under the portico Sera shook the rain off her sleeves and held the glass door open. Kade stopped on the step and looked at them. There was Sera with her hand on the door. There was Eli with the tablet zipped inside his jacket, and Mara holding her folder of pages flat against her ribs to keep them dry.

That made four, with him. He realized he had counted. It had taken one afternoon to become a habit, checking the number against the names. He did not think it was a habit he was going to lose.

Everyone was present. He went in.

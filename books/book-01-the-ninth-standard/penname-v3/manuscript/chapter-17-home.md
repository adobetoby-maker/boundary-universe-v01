# Chapter 17 — Home

*Forty-seven. Thursday. Alvarez says she's not clearing the day for you, you get whatever Thursday is. Boyd says bring ID. I said he has a face. Boyd said a face is not a document. Tessa says the chips are a document.*

The number fourteen bus still stopped at the tire warehouse and not at the school, because the school had never been enough of a place to earn its own stop, and Kade got off where he had gotten off for four years and stood a moment in the diesel with the bag on his shoulder.

The building had not changed. The blue wall the desert had taken down to the color of an old bandage. The self-storage sign next door with the lower corner gone out of its S. The flag halyard ringing its two notes against the pole. What had changed was that he could see it all at once, the way Asterion had taught him to take a room, exits and load paths and who stood with whom, and the building gave itself up in about a second and a half. One front door. Three concrete steps. A platform lift beside the steps with a yellow tag zip-tied to its rail. Forty-two windows, eleven of them painted shut.

He had never once counted the windows when it was his.

Boyd was behind the desk with his coffee and the roll of electrical tape and a bicycle wheel clamped to the desk edge, and he looked up as the door opened and said, "Mercer," in exactly the voice he had said it in every morning of Kade's life, and looked past him at the door for whoever was next.

Nobody was next. Second block had started. Boyd looked back.

"You're not on the roster."

"No."

"Then you're a visitor." He reached under the desk without looking and came up with a lanyard, blue, VISITOR in white on the tag, and held it out across the wheel. "Wear it where I can see it. District says anybody not on the roster wears one, and the district fixed the arch the week the van came, so the building can tell the difference now."

Kade put it over his head. It hung against his chest, above the band on his wrist that had once refused to open a gate for him, and the two of them together made a sentence he did not want to read yet.

"Tape's for the wheel," Boyd said, seeing him look. "Spokes. Miguel's bike. He's been on it since seven. Room Four, and take the corridor, not the hall. The hall's got Omondi's lift in it and I'm tired of people looking at it."

"What happened to it?"

"Inspector. Tuesday. Tagged it and left." Boyd went back to the spoke. "Go on. She's counting."

Tessa was at the water fountain outside Room Four and did not look surprised, which he had expected, or pleased, which he had not. She held her hand out flat. He put the bag of chips in it. She read the flavor, weighed it, and pocketed it without a word, and the debt closed the way Tessa closed things, with no witnesses and no receipt.

"Interest," she said.

"There was nothing about interest."

"There is now." She looked at the lanyard once and said nothing about it, and that was the kindest thing anyone did all morning.

---

Alvarez did not stop the module. She pointed at his old desk without lifting her eyes from the district tablet and said, "Geometry. Tablet's in the drawer. You're in Room Four, you do Room Four's work," and the room, which had gone still when the door opened, went back to being a room.

Not all of it. Darius, two rows up, faced the front with the straightness of a person who had decided in advance not to turn around, and he held it. Marcus, the new one, thirteen or fourteen, at the desk by the heater, watched Kade sit down the way you would watch someone set down a thing that might go off. Miguel was not there. The bike, then.

The chair leaned left. His feet found the two places on the floor that stopped it before his head arrived. The tablet in the drawer was the same tablet, with the crack across the corner he had put there in his second year, and the module froze at problem six because nine tablets had tried to update at once, which it always did.

He sat inside it and did the geometry. It was easy. That was not the point. The point was that nobody in the room needed him to do it and he did it anyway, and the thing behind his ribs that had been standing since the ferry sat down.

"Router's going down," Alvarez said. "Two minutes. Save nothing, it's already gone." She stood, capped her stylus, and looked at him for the first time. "Since we've got a visitor with a number, and the number's been on my board three weeks, we're going to use him. Mercer. Come and explain it."

The board still said MERCER, 1,904, in her square hand, and under it, in Tessa's, STILL OWES ME CHIPS. He stood beside it with the lanyard swinging.

"It's a sum," he said. "Every person on the island has about sixty numbers. Bouts, class, how fast you get out of a room when the horn goes, what the doctor writes, how you did on a team you weren't running. They add them up with weights nobody publishes and the total goes on a wall. One thousand nine hundred four means that this week, with this week's weights, there are nineteen hundred and three sums bigger than mine."

"Bigger at what," Tessa said.

"At the sum."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one the wall gives." He picked up the marker. "Here's what it hasn't got a column for." He wrote them the way she had taught him to write missing assumptions, one under the other. THE MONTH IT DIDN'T MOVE. THE NIGHT I PACKED. WHO HELD THE DOOR. He capped the marker. "Those aren't in the sixty. They're what the sixty are made of."

Darius had turned around. He turned back.

Marcus's hand came up by the heater and went down again before Alvarez could see it, and Kade answered it because Marcus had not gotten to ask.

"And the other number." He touched the band. "The first test they give you measures one thing. I did something during it that the test didn't have a line for, so it wrote down nothing. Not a fail. Nothing. It took me four months and a great deal of humiliation to learn that being un-measurable at one thing isn't a talent. It's a gap in the form."

"Then bend something," Miguel said from the door. He had come in with grease to the wrists and a look he had been carrying since the van. "They filmed the door. I was behind it in this coat. Bend the door, so it was worth it."

"No."

"Because we're not worth it."

"Because there's a doctor on the island who writes three things about me every day and stops me at two, and if I put a number on her card in this room I'm not on the boat tomorrow. That's not me being careful. It's the rule, and I don't get to be more interesting than the rule." He held up his right hand and let the last two fingers do the small separate thing they had done since the final. "That's what's left over from last time. Nobody filmed that."

Miguel looked at the hand a while. Then he went and sat down and wiped his fingers on his jeans, and that was as far as Miguel was going to come, and it was farther than Kade had expected.

"Router's up," Alvarez said. "Sit, Mercer. Problem seven."

---

At eleven she put the district form on the projector.

EMERGENCY EGRESS PLAN, ANNUAL REVISION. Under it, in the district's font, a note that the plan on file no longer reflected accessible egress and must be resubmitted by a qualified person within ten days. Under that, a signature line.

"Tuesday an inspector tagged the lift," Alvarez said. "Mr. Omondi has been coming in through the dock on the delivery ramp since, with Boyd holding the gate, which is not a plan, it's Boyd. The district would like a qualified person to write a new one." She looked at Kade. "I have a Tactical Systems transcript in the room. I'd be foolish not to use it."

He knew what it was. He knew it in the way she said *qualified* and in the way Darius, without turning, went very still. He could have said no. He had a Sunday against a rail that had taught him what a plan was worth when it needed him on the floor, and he could have made a small speech about it, and the room would have heard the speech and not the thing.

So he took the form and drew it.

Three minutes. Rooms One through Three, front doors, steps, grass. Rooms Four through Six, north door, which was level to the side lot and had a manual bar and no steps. Staff sweep behind. Count at the pole. He put a lead on each third and a staff name beside Room Six, and signed it, because that was what the line was for, and it was good. Anybody at Asterion would have signed off on it inside the minute.

"Boyd," Alvarez said into the desk phone. "Now."

The lights went. The emergency fixtures came on, all but the one at the east end, which thought about it. Alvarez said, "Fire above the ceiling, east hall, nobody crosses it," in the voice she used for problem seven, and the building emptied.

The front was clean. He watched Rooms One to Three go past him at a walk that was nearly a run and down the steps into the light. North was where it broke. The bar went with its flat bang and the door opened eighteen inches and stopped against something, and Darius, who had hit it, put his shoulder in and it did not move, and past his shoulder Kade could see the white flank of a truck parked so close to the wall a cat could not have gotten through.

"Warehouse," Darius said, to nobody, or to him. "Thursdays. Every Thursday since forever."

Twelve people and a powered chair stood in the north corridor. Mr. Omondi, who taught numeracy in Six and had gray in his beard and no interest at all in being rescued, turned his chair on the spot and said, "Front, then," and went, and the corridor followed him, and at the front doors the three concrete steps were where they had always been and the lift had a yellow tag on it, and he stopped at the top with the whole school on the grass below him and folded his hands.

"I have been evacuated to a step," he said. "Please note it on the form."

Kade came down onto the grass with the form and counted. He was good at counting. He did it the Asterion way, heads in blocks of five, twice, and got forty-six with staff, and the roster said forty-eight, and he said so, and Alvarez said, "Who."

He looked at the grass. Forty-two faces he had known for four years, and two he did not, and Omondi on the step, and he opened his mouth to say a name and there was no name to say.

"Miguel," Tessa said, from the middle of the crowd. Not loud. "Bike cage. Off the dock. There's no speaker back there and Boyd was holding the gate, so nobody was holding Miguel." She looked at Kade. "And the new girl in Two. She was first to the north door and got through the gap sideways before the rest of you got there, and she's standing at the warehouse fence, because your plan says north and nothing after."

He had not known the school had forty-four students. He had known forty-two, and their siblings, and which ones came in hungry, and in four months two people had been added to the building he had thought of as his and he had not known to count them.

"Forty-six seconds," said Marcus, from the pole, with a stopwatch. He said it the way you would offer a good number to a person who looked like they needed one.

"Building's on fire," Alvarez said. "You've got a teacher on the porch, a boy in a cage, a girl at a fence, and a very good time. Inside."

---

Darius caught him at the north door on the way back in, when the corridor had cleared. He did not say anything. He put his shoulder on the bar and shoved, and the door went its eighteen inches, and he stood in the gap with the truck's white flank a hand's width from his face and let Kade look at it.

"For reasons," he said, "I have been through this door every Thursday of my life, and every Thursday there's a truck, and the driver eats his lunch in the cab at eleven. You'd have known that. You'd have put it on the form."

"I'd have known it."

"You knew forty-two people. There's forty-four. You counted forty-six heads like it was money and you couldn't say who was gone." He let the door go and it swung to and clicked. "Marcus asks me what you're like. Every day since the board. What you're like, what you eat, does the school float. And I say I don't know anymore, and I'm not going to lie to a kid, so that's what he gets. I don't know anymore."

There was no volume in it. Kade had a door for volume. He had been walking out of it since he was nine years old, and Darius knew that, and had taken the door away by not raising his voice.

"I sent you the sea," Kade said.

"You sent me the sea. Nine times. And once the word later, and then Alvarez had a number on the board and I heard the rest off the pharmacy television standing behind my mother, and I thought, he'll write. And you didn't. And I thought, he's busy, and then I thought, he's not busy, he's the same, he's found a new building to not be in."

Kade stood in the corridor with the lanyard against his chest and did not have the sentence he had been waiting four months to have, and understood, finally, that he was never going to have it, and that the waiting had been the disappearing.

"First I hated it there," he said. "Then I didn't. And I'd told everybody for four years I didn't need a school to want me, and one did, and put it on a wall, and I didn't know what I was. So I didn't write, because writing meant saying what I was, and I couldn't. I thought that was honest. It was just quiet."

"Quiet's a sentence too."

"I know that now. That's what quiet costs."

Darius looked at him a long moment. Then at the hand. Kade had not noticed it going; the last two fingers had started their small separate thing against his leg.

"That the doctor's second line?"

"Yes."

"How long."

"Since the final."

"You could have sent that." Darius's voice did something and he put it back. "Instead of the sea. I'd have known what to do with that."

"I know."

"Here's how it goes. You're allowed to not know what you are. That's fine, I've never known what you are. But when something's about to be on a screen, a wall, a television in a pharmacy, I hear it from you first. Not Alvarez. Not the reports."

"Before Mom?"

"I'm not asking for before your mother. I'm not an animal." He pushed off the wall. "Same minute."

It was not a promise to be good at staying in touch. It was a promise to stop using not knowing himself as permission to vanish, and that one had a bar on it he could put his shoulder to.

"Same minute," he said.

"Good." Darius went ahead of him down the corridor and said, without turning, "Marcus is going to ask you what you eat. Say noodles. He's decided it's noodles."

---

Alvarez ran the rebuild from the back of the room, and Kade was not given the marker. He was given a chair, the leaning one, and a line on Tessa's paper, because Tessa had a paper now, a sheet torn from the back of the district form with every name in the building on it in pencil, and his was at the bottom under a ruled line, VISITOR, and she had put it there without asking him.

"Dock," Darius said. "Not north. North's the truck. Dock's got a roll gate on a motor that fails shut, and a person door beside it with a bar that works with no power, and the delivery ramp is level all the way out, and I know all of this for reasons that are between me and the ramp."

"Ask him first," said Lacey. She was a quiet girl from the far table who stood as if she had been trained not to take up room. "Before anybody writes Room Six on a line. My brother's in a chair. You don't route him. You ask him what he needs and then you route what he told you."

She went. She was gone two minutes and came back with Omondi behind her, who rolled to the front of Room Four, looked at the projector and at Kade in the leaning chair, and said, "Dock ramp is fine. It's what I've used since Tuesday. Nobody touches the chair. Somebody holds the person door, because it's heavy and it closes on its own, and that is the entirety of what I require. I have been pushed into a hedge by enthusiasm before. Write down *hold the door* and nothing else."

Marcus wrote it. He had the stopwatch in his left hand and the pencil in his right, and he wrote HOLD THE DOOR, and then looked at the plan on the projector, Kade's plan, and said, "And the front's still fastest for One to Three."

"It is," Tessa said.

"So we keep that. And Four through Six go dock. And somebody walks the west side last. Bike cage, restrooms, the rooms with nobody in them, because nobody had the rooms with nobody in them." He was looking at the stopwatch. "That's slower."

"Yes," said Alvarez.

"I'll do that one." He crossed something out on his tablet with one finger, and Kade did not see what it was and knew anyway that it was his own good number. "And names at the pole. On paper. Not heads. Heads don't tell you who."

"Heads told us forty-six," Tessa said, not to Kade, and to him.

Kade sat in the leaning chair and watched it go onto the projector in Alvarez's hand, line under line. It was worse than his. It had a boy walking the wrong way down a hall in a fire and a roll call by name that ate a minute nobody had, and every line of it belonged to somebody who knew the truck, or the ramp, or what to ask a man in a chair, or which two names were new, and it would still be true on Monday when he was on the island, and next Thursday when the truck came.

"Mercer," Alvarez said. "You're on the list. When she says your name, you say here. That's the whole of your job."

The second run took a minute fifty-one by Marcus's watch. The bar on the dock door went with its flat bang and Darius held it with both hands and said "Go, go, go," in a voice with nothing performed in it, and Omondi came down the ramp at a walk with his hands on the armrests and nobody touching anything, and Miguel was collected from the cage by Marcus on the sweep, with no idea, for the second time that day, that anything had happened. At the pole Tessa read the paper.

She read all forty-four and the four staff, and they answered in the voices of people hearing their own names said seriously and not being used to it, and she read a name Kade had never heard, and a girl at the fence said, "Here." At the bottom of the sheet, under the ruled line, Tessa said, "Mercer," and Kade said, "Here."

It was the first time in four years anybody at Northline had counted him.

"One fifty-one," Marcus said. He looked at the watch a long moment, holding a number he liked less than the one that was true, and then at the sheet in Tessa's hand, and put the watch in his pocket. "The second one counts."

Russell had come across the lot during the roll call with two people behind him and a phone already out, and Kade had filed the car and gone on listening for his name, and now he could not file it anymore.

"Kade." Russell shook the way he shook in parking lots. He was Northline's vice principal and told the story of the kid the district sent to Asterion by accident at retirement parties, and he had brought the second half of the story with him. "Some of your Canyon Ridge people wanted to say hello. I thought the steps. With the flag. The district newsletter's been asking."

Mr. Hensley had taught sophomore physics at Canyon Ridge and had the handshake of a man closing a sale, and he closed it, and put a hand on Kade's shoulder and turned him a quarter turn toward the phone.

"I flagged you," Hensley said, for the phone as much as for Kade. "Sophomore year. When the district wanted your file pulled from the aptitude pool I said no, keep him in, this one's got something. I said it in a meeting. I'll say it now. It's in the record."

Heat came up under Kade's collar so fast he felt the line it made. He had carried a sentence for this two years without knowing he would ever get to use it. He looked past Hensley at Ms. Rook, who had taught English at Canyon Ridge and was standing at the bottom of the steps with her arms folded and had not come up them, and he did not want to say the sentence in front of her.

So he asked a question instead, because a question was a smaller thing to hold.

"Who wanted the file pulled?"

"The district. There was talk."

"It's the national test. Every seventeen-year-old in the county sits it, Russell reads it over the speaker. Nobody pulls a file from it. There's no pool to keep me in." He stepped out from under the hand, not fast, just out. "I've read the file. Russell left it open on his desk once. Six of you are in it, and none of you wrote *watch this one.* It says argumentative. It says does not utilize available opportunities. That's the record. It's accurate."

Hensley's smile stayed where it was for a second after the rest of him had left it.

"And here's the rest," Kade said, and turned, because the grass could hear anyway, forty-four people and four staff and Omondi at the foot of the ramp. "I failed his class on purpose. I wanted the zero more than I wanted anybody deciding if I was good enough. I walked out of rooms. I made every number mine by making it bad. That's mine, and I'm not giving it to the building, and I'm not letting the building have the other part either. If the newsletter wants me on the steps, it gets both."

Russell lowered the phone. "We're proud of you," he said, after a while, because it was the thing he had brought and the only part that was still true.

"That part I'll take."

They stayed four minutes and asked about the boat. Hensley did not speak again. When they went back across the lot Rook did not go with them.

She waited until the school had gone in, through three doors now, out of habit, and then she stood at the bottom of the steps with the tagged lift between them and the halyard going overhead.

"I came for a different reason," she said. "I wasn't going to say it into his phone."

He waited.

"That spring, I stopped calling on you. You noticed. You looked at me when I went past you for the third time, and I looked at the board." She said it plainly and did not dress it. "I told myself it was mercy. You'd stopped handing things in and I thought, don't make him perform not caring. It wasn't mercy. I was tired, and it was easier to decide you'd already left than to keep finding out whether you had. I owe you that. I'm not asking you to do anything with it."

It went in somewhere Hensley's had not, because he had liked her, and because she had not made it a speech.

"I made it easy."

"You did."

"I'd have looked at the board too."

"I know. That's why I'm the one apologizing." She almost smiled. "You look like you've been counted."

"Once. Today."

"Good." She went to her car and did not look back, and it was better that she did not.

The exhibition letter was in his bag, and Darius found it because Darius went through his bag for chips and had never in his life asked first.

"Meridian House," Darius read, standing in the aisle. "Exhibition Night. Visitor seating, Northline Alternative, by request of." He looked up. "By request of you."

"I was going to say it at the end."

"You've been here five hours." He read on. Julian's precise signature, returned inside eleven minutes with a route plan and a note, *next time bring a head count first,* which Darius read in a voice he believed was Julian's and was not. Then a second hand under it, one line. "*Darius Bell is not permitted to challenge anyone.*" He looked at the letter for a while. "Who is Mara Vey and how does she know me."

"I described you."

"Accurately?"

"Enough that she wrote a rule."

"I want to challenge her."

"That's the rule."

The room had come up around them like a pan on heat. Tessa was at the letter over Darius's shoulder, and Marcus had stood without noticing, and even Miguel had turned around. Then Tessa, who read everything, read the second page and said, "Twelve," and the room went down a step.

Not quiet. Arithmetic. Kids counted each other without meaning to. Marcus looked at Tessa and away. Darius's grin thinned as his eyes went along the rows doing the sum, and the door Kade had opened turned in front of him into a ladder, and he had known it would.

"Twelve ferry seats," Tessa read. "One staff escort. Selected by the sending school on academic readiness, attendance, and behavioral standing."

"No," Kade said.

"No what," said Darius.

"Not those. Pick on attendance and behavior and the seats go to the twelve who'd have been fine at Canyon Ridge, and everybody else gets a video. That's the district in one sentence and I'm not signing it. And I'm not picking either. I couldn't name two of you this morning. I don't get to choose twelve."

"So who does," Marcus said.

"You just did a building."

Alvarez, at the back, drank her coffee, which was an opinion.

"Names," Tessa said, before anyone else. "Same as the pole. Whatever the rule is, it's on paper before anybody applies, and every name gets read against it out loud. No list in a drawer."

"Aptitude scores," said Marcus. "Top twelve." Then he looked at Lacey, who had said nothing, and at the projector, where the two times still sat side by side, and said, "No. That's the same sum in a different coat," and crossed it out himself.

"Some for people who'd use it," Tessa said. "Response. Resonance. Not a boat ride."

"Some staff pick," Darius said, "because staff know things, and I want it noted I said something reasonable."

"Staff pick favorites," said Miguel.

"Then some of it's a draw," said Lacey. "Out of a hat. So it's not all the people who talk."

"Out of who?"

"Anyone who does the safety prep," Kade said, because that one was the island's and not his. "Ferry rules, where you stand on a visitor floor, what the horn means. It's a gate that keeps you off a floor that moves. Nobody skips it."

Miguel had not put his hand up. He said, to his desk, "My mother's not signing anything with the word ocean on it. So."

The room went a degree quieter, and Kade was glad of it, because it was the thing he would have missed from the front.

"Then it comes here," Tessa said, before he could. "On those." The tablets. "Can they do that?"

"I've got a roommate who can make anything talk to anything, and asking will offend him, which is how you know it's yes."

"And the twelve don't come back with a story," Lacey said. "They come back with a job. Whatever they learned, they run it for the ones who watched on a tablet. Stations."

"Can mine be combat," Darius said.

"Yours is the safety prep," said Tessa. "You've never sat through one."

"It's my weakest event."

"Ask for more seats," Alvarez said from the back, to Kade.

"I'll ask. I'm not promising anything nobody's signed."

"Good." She had come up the aisle with the district egress form in her hand, the room's plan on it now, Kade's signature struck through and hers below, and she set it on the projector so it glowed on the wall, dock and ramp and names at the pole. "That's the shape. Don't let it turn back into a ladder while I'm getting coffee."

---

She found him after, at the board, when the room had gone loud with the arithmetic of a hat.

"I told Darius, the night the cars came, that kids who go to that place don't come back to places like this." She said it to the board and not to him. "He repeated it to you, he repeats everything. I'm glad to be wrong and I'm not going to pretend I never said it."

"You weren't wrong. I nearly didn't."

"I know." She picked up the felt and went at MERCER, 1,904, a stroke at a time. "I told your mother, in a meeting you'd walked out of, that you'd be fine if you'd just hand it in. I was wrong about *fine.* Some of what you were handed wasn't worth handing in, and you could see that, and I graded you like you couldn't. And some of it was worth it, and you used the first thing as a permit for the second. Both of those are true. I'm sorry for my half."

Rook's had gone in. This one went deeper, because she had been on his side the whole time and was saying it anyway.

"I'm not going to tell them I was fine underneath."

"No. Because if Marcus thinks the island happened because you were secretly fine, that's Hensley's story with a nicer ending, and he'll spend two years waiting to be discovered instead of doing anything." She wrote the two times where the rank had been, and under them, in her square hand, copying the boy's pencil, THE SECOND ONE COUNTS. "There. That's on my board now. Yours is on his."

Marcus caught him at the door, when the room was loud enough that a question could be asked under it.

"Do they take people from schools like this?"

"Yes."

"How."

He nearly said he did not know, which was true. He thought of a girl in his House with three placements behind her who read an emergency manual at dinner, and a girl at a visitor gate whose school kept the test booklets in a bank vault, and a duffel strap his mother had mended in the wrong color thread.

"Different ways. Mine was the strange one. Nobody checks whether you were at the right school at eleven. That's the part the website leaves off."

Marcus did not look convinced, and Kade did not push, because a kid who could cross out his own stopwatch would not take a sentence on faith and should not.

"Regional aptitude calendar's in my top drawer," Alvarez said from across the room, to Marcus and not the hall. "Fee waivers are under it. Anybody who wants either can ask me without announcing it to a room."

Marcus's shoulders came down half an inch. Kade heard the offer inside the offer, that wanting something did not have to be a performance, and he had needed that rule at fourteen.

"Noodles," Marcus said at the door, testing.

"Noodles," Kade agreed.

---

Elena came at three in scrubs, straight off the floor, and did not come in. She stood outside the arch and waited, and Boyd let her, and Kade went to the desk to give the lanyard back.

Boyd did not take it.

"It's good for the year," he said. "District rule. Visitors sign out, they don't turn in." He turned the binder around and put the pen in front of Kade, bootlace and all, and Kade signed where students had never had to sign, TIME OUT, three-oh-four, and Boyd read it upside down and made the sound the desk made instead of approval.

"I'm not a visitor," Kade said.

"Then what do you want the tag to say."

He did not have a word.

"That's what I thought." Boyd went back to the wheel. "It says you came back. It's a small tag. That's all it's got room for. Tell Miguel his bike's done."

Darius walked him to the arch with his hood up, which was how he did the things his face was not available for, and at the door did the other thing, briefly, one arm, and shoved him through it.

"Forty-eight's about the ocean form," he said. "Same minute."

"Same minute."

In the car Elena drove four blocks before she said anything. Past the bus stop where she had picked him up after the second suspension and said nothing the whole way home, the loudest thing she had ever done. The street that climbed toward Canyon Ridge went by on the left, and he did not look up it, and then he did.

"You've still got the tag on."

"Boyd wouldn't take it."

"I've been to eleven meetings in that building and never seen him make anyone sign that binder." She drove. "Darius says you got counted."

"By name. Bottom of the sheet."

"That's what going back is for," Elena said. "You don't get to keep knowing a place from a boat."

He sat with it. He had spent four years believing a place could only be home if it was the only one, and that leaving shut a door, and the trick was to shut it first. Northline had not shut. It had stayed where it was, between a tire warehouse and a storage yard, and gotten a truck on Thursdays and a boy named Marcus and a tagged lift, and a ruled line at the bottom of Tessa's sheet with his name under it. And the island was the other line. Not the towers. A chair nobody had to hold for him, and a doctor with three lines, and a roommate who would be offended to be asked.

Two places was twice the number of places that could go wrong. The old fear turned up on schedule to say so, and he gave it its number, the way Park had taught him with a headache, and it was real. It had never been the fear. It had been what he did with it.

Elena pulled into the terminal lot with the water gray behind it and left the engine running, because she was going home to sleep four hours and then back to the floor.

"Mom."

"Mm."

"I'm going to write to Darius. Before the boat. Not the sea."

"I know. He told me. Same minute." She touched his shoulder once, the way she marked a chart. "Go on. Whichever one."

He got out into the wind. On the concrete, before the horn, with the lanyard still on, he wrote it, and it took longer than any message he had sent in four months, and he sent it before he could improve it.

*One. You're right, I never knew what I was. Still don't. I know two more names than I did this morning. Tell Marcus noodles was a lie and it's mostly oats. Same minute.*

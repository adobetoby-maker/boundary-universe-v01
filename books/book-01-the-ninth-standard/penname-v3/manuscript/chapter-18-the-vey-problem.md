# Chapter 18 — The Vey Problem

The study carrel at the top of Meridian's east stair had the only link in the House that did not stutter, and Mara had it booked every Sunday at nine, because her father did not call from a phone. He called from a room, with a flag in it she was not supposed to notice and a half second of lag she had learned to count the way other people counted a pulse.

"Play it back for me," Alaric Vey said. "Four fifty-one. Mercer is behind the glass, and you move Shah."

"Off the west post. Up to the east side of the crown."

"Where the plan had never put her."

"The plan assumed Mercer could keep their seventh busy. Their seventh had just put him over a rail. The plan was describing a floor that no longer existed."

"Most captains would have held their shape and hoped."

"Most captains were not down two with six minutes left."

The half second passed. Then his chin came down a fraction, which was the whole of what Alaric Vey did in place of applause, and she felt it land, and resented feeling it, and let it land anyway. He had watched the final twice. His message had come at ten past two in the morning, four lines with time stamps, and it had found the twelve seconds where his daughter stopped protecting the plan and started protecting seven people, and called that correct, and called her reason for it wrong. He was right on both counts. That was the trouble with calling him cold. Cold men slept.

"Mechanics is the nineteenth," he said. "You have not mentioned it."

"Because it's the nineteenth."

"You are still leading with the right shoulder past four moves."

"I know."

"You say that when you would rather I stopped."

"You taught me that people who compliment you are usually lining something up."

"I am beginning to regret certain parts of your education."

"That would make two of us."

The corner of his mouth moved, and she had been waiting for it since nine, and the wait embarrassed her.

"And the Mercer boy," he said. "How is he settling?"

She had a coupled-field paper open beside the display, and she did not close it, because closing it would have told him something. "Into what?"

"Into being the most-watched first-year on the island."

"Badly. Improving."

"That is not an assessment. That is a kindness."

"You asked me five questions about my semester and had the answers to four before I opened my mouth. You didn't have that one. It's the only one you came for."

The lag gave her his sigh a half second late. She had grown up inside the rule that attention was information, and she could no longer hear a question from him without hearing the second one folded under it.

"Has he repeated the structural event?"

Her stylus stopped on the glass.

She knew every public word about the morning the floor broke. The academy notice had said a composite failure coinciding with an anomalous combat reading. The faculty summary, which Meridian's adviser had left open on a screen for eleven seconds, said anomalous combat reading and nothing more. Students called it the Mercer Fault, because students named things the way they named dogs. Nobody had called it a structural event. That was a heading from a form she had never been shown, and her father did not work on this island.

"Say that again."

"I asked whether the arena incident had recurred."

"You didn't. You said structural event."

"The floor broke, Mara. Structure failed. It is an ordinary phrase."

"It isn't. The notice says composite failure. The faculty line says anomalous reading. I can quote you both, and neither uses your word. Your word is a classification." She let the lag run. "Where did you read it?"

His answer did not arrive in the half second where his answers lived, or in the second after it. He had taught her, at nine, in front of two archive folders numbered forty-one and forty-three, the difference between absent and missing. Absent was a gap. Missing meant somebody had expected it to be there. She had hated the lesson and kept it, and she applied it now to a silence.

"Somebody expected you to know," she said.

"You sound like Amara Sen."

"Then answer the way you'd answer her."

"Amara and I have spent twenty years disagreeing about what deserves looking at."

Not the answer. The place a person stood in order not to say it. Kade heard the step-around in everyone, and she had begun to find the habit useful in a way that annoyed her. She thought of him in Bay One with tape on the mat, telling her Sen had said no to a question about his father before the word was out, and of herself saying that was a decision by one adult and not a fact. One adult. She was looking at a second.

"Did you know his father?"

Alaric Vey went still. Not much. She had been reading him since before she could read.

"I didn't say that."

"I didn't ask what you said."

His voice dropped into the register he used for subjects that were closed. "Leave the Mercer family where they are. I am asking you."

He did not ask her for things. He set conditions, and she met them, and that was the shape of their peace. The asking was the tell, and it was also the moment she understood that she was going to do it.

"Why?"

"Because some doors are load-bearing. You do not learn which ones by opening them."

"Father, that is the least persuasive sentence you have ever said to me, and you once told me the Kepler captain was harmless."

"Mara."

Twice in one call. She filed it beside the word.

"Does Director Sen know you're asking me about him?"

The silence this time was not the lag.

"I have a meeting," he said.

"You have a question you're not going to answer."

Something in his face gave a quarter of an inch, the way it did when she had scored on him and he had decided to allow it. "Eat breakfast," he said, and the link went dark, and left her looking at her own face in the glass with a training session in twenty minutes and a dead man turned into a classified subject before the House had finished its coffee.

She had been raised to believe discipline was the art of not moving. It was beginning to look like the art of choosing which way.

---

Combat Hall Two had been cut into four acceleration lanes, and Instructor Chen ran them without raising his voice or repeating himself. A start plate, two pivot marks, three waist-high barriers on actuators that swung the moment the runner crossed the first gate. The lane paid for prediction. Read the barrier's opening angle before it finished turning, commit, and the route opened. Wait to be sure, and the next gate shut on your shoulder. Mara had run it forty times since autumn and liked it the way she liked anything that rewarded deciding early.

Julian Cross had the no-field square at the far end, forty people along the wall who had come to stand near the number, and Kade in the square being taken apart.

She watched while she stretched, without turning her head. Julian moved him toward the edge in three quiet decisions, the way he might cross a kitchen. Kade saw the trap a beat earlier than he had the week before, changed his feet, went down anyway, and laughed. Then he sat up, held his right hand out palm down, and said something she could not hear, and Julian stepped back and waited. From across the hall she could see the last two fingers doing the small separate thing they had done in the medical corner after the final. He had called his own hand. Julian had honored it. Nobody along the wall laughed.

She looked away before either of them caught her.

Her first sequence went in eight point four, slow for her. On the second she read the center barrier before its arm finished loading and cleared the plate with her shoulder a hand's width from the closing edge. Seven point nine. She should have reset. Instead she looked down the hall, where Julian had Kade tracing the last exchange in the air, and the name came up in her the way a barrier came up in a lane.

Aaron Mercer.

The third tone went. She left the plate late, read the second barrier as a left rotation, and put her weight on it.

It went right.

The edge took her shoulder. The safety field caught her a hand's width from the wall, held her tipped sideways in the blue lattice for half a second, then set her down on the mat like a book being returned to a shelf.

Chen killed the lane and walked over.

"Forty times you've run that lane," he said.

"Forty-one."

"And on the forty-first you read a right as a left."

"I read what it showed me."

"It showed you a false cue. Every third sequence carries one now. The briefing said initial conditions could no longer be treated as reliable, and you read it and filed it under ordinary variance, because the lane had paid you forty times for committing early." He was not scolding. He was describing. "You took a likely answer and promoted it to a fact because acting on it had always worked."

The sentence arrived with her father's silence still hanging off it, and she disliked, briefly, an instructor who had become relevant by accident.

"Are you fit to continue?"

She thought about Kade holding his hand out where forty people could see it, and how much simpler it had made the next thing, and she disliked the influence and used it.

"Yes. Reduced speed. I'm carrying something that isn't the lane."

Chen did not ask what. He took the lane to sixty percent and blanked the timer.

"I didn't ask you to remove the score."

"New condition. Any gate you can't call, you put a hand flat on the pivot mark before you move. The lane logs an unresolved. It does not log a guess wearing the clothes of a read."

"That will cost me a second."

"The timer's off."

That was exactly the problem, and he knew it. She went to the plate.

The next run was slow and she hated it. At the second barrier both actuators loaded the same, nothing to read, and every part of her trained since eleven wanted to pick one. She put her palm on the pivot mark instead, cold composite under her hand, and the barrier feinted right, reversed, and left the center open for less than a second, and she went through the gap it had not meant to give her. Four full seconds over her own time, and no number on the wall to say so.

By the fourth run the flat hand had stopped being a confession. It was a position with its own footwork, a shorter stride and a later weight, a way of arriving at a gate with two answers still in her legs instead of one in her shoulder. At speed it would cost her fractions. It might also keep her out of a wall, and the wall was not the only thing she had nearly run into at speed this week.

Chen pushed the record to her band. Four attempts, no times, one word under assessment. CALIBRATING. Not impaired. Not failed. Her rank was still seven, and today it looked small beside a name nobody had written down yet. She saved the record beside the coupled-field notes and did not go down the hall to Kade, because she had nothing yet that he could use.

---

She found Sera in the equipment cage under Meridian's stair at one, restocking drill vests by weight and not by size, which was how Sera did everything. A tablet was propped on the shelf between racks.

"You've skipped lunch," Sera said, without turning.

"That's Kade's line."

"It's mine. He picked it up off me the way people pick up a cold." She hung a vest and checked its seal. The tablet was not coursework. The header said STUDENT INCIDENT RESPONSE, OBSERVATION ROTATION. Two clinic shifts logged. One faculty reference, requested. And a box that said *experience under live conditions*, with nothing in it.

"That rotation's for second-years."

"The policy says two medical labs. It doesn't say what year." Sera pulled the next vest. "The selection notes say second-years. The notes aren't the policy. Your friend isn't the only one in this House who reads past the sentence they want you to stop at."

"Chen would sign the reference."

"I asked Park. She's the one who'll be in the room if the room goes wrong."

Mara looked at the empty box. A broken floor, a suppression alarm, a House Trial final, and Sera had written none of them in, because none had been hers to run. It was the most honest blank Mara had seen all week.

"My father knows something about Kade that he has no way to know," she said.

Sera's hands stopped on the rack. "That's bad."

"Yes."

"Does Kade?"

"Not yet."

Sera turned then and looked at her the way she looked at a capsule before lifting it, weight first. "Which are you protecting? Him, or the thing you've started?"

Mara said nothing.

"They feel the same from inside," Sera said, and went back to the vests. "I've been both. The second one's easier, because it never asks you anything."

"I need evidence before I put this in front of him."

"Evidence of what? Say the claim."

"That what my father knows isn't an accident."

"That's your question." Sera hung the vest. "His is going to be what any of it says about his father. You can carry both, but they don't weigh the same to him and you."

"I can't answer his without mine first. And if I tell him now, I hand him an accusation with no direction on it, and he's in Sen's office before dinner with nothing in his hands."

"All true." Sera did not decide it for her, which would have been kinder and worse. "So when do you stop? Say it now, before you've got something you'd rather not stop for."

Her father had taught her to define the win before she stepped onto any floor. She had stepped onto this one at nine in the morning without doing it.

"One link between Aaron Mercer and this island that didn't come from my father's face or a public death record," she said. "Something neither of us supplied."

"Then that's where it stops being yours alone." Sera racked the last vest. "After that you don't get to keep running it because you're better at running things. That's not a rule. It's just what he'll remember."

---

The public file was aggressively ordinary, and she read all of it anyway. Northline Alternative. Three suspensions. Two transfers. A semester of failure that did not fit the aptitude scores beside it, arranged, she now knew, one class at a time by a boy who wanted the zero more than anyone's opinion. Unremarkable medical. Mother, Elena Mercer, emergency nursing. Father, deceased. Aaron Mercer. Industrial accident, seventeen years ago, on a date so close to Kade's birth that the two nearly touched.

She built the table the way she had told him to build his, in Bay One, with tape on the mat. Three columns. CLAIM. SOURCE. WRONG IF. The last one first, always, because a table with only the rows you liked was a speech.

Row one. Her father had used a restricted classification. Source, his own mouth. Wrong if a legitimate academy contact had sent him a private summary. That did not explain why he would hide the contact from her. It stayed in the table.

Row two. He had gone still at Aaron's name and told her to leave the family alone. Source, his face, and an instruction. Wrong if he had read about a public accident once, or known someone else in it. Thin. She disliked thin evidence most when it leaned her way, and this leaned.

Under the file, the request panel offered more. Disciplinary detail, with a faculty sponsor. Medical, with a documented welfare incident. Either one required a reason typed into a box, and the reason would have been a lie. She closed the panel. Public first. Institutional only where her scholar clearance already reached. No side door through Eli, who could open one, and would, and would be wrong to. The rules did not make the thing safe. They kept it visible whose it was.

The public trail was short. Employment. Tax. A death certificate. A local item about a containment accident at an industrial site outside the city, no byline, one photograph: a warehouse behind fencing, smoke at a roof vent, two response vehicles parked for a camera. She opened the image data. Uploaded six hours after the reported accident. Captured eleven months before it. It proved nothing. Local outlets reused file photographs the way Meridian reused mugs. She wrote it down, ordinary explanation beside it.

The certificate was better, in the wrong direction. A physician's signature, a municipal seal, a registered time, no amendment. Then she opened the signing physician's public registration, and the license belonged to a doctor whose practice had been two hundred kilometers away. Possible, during a federal incident. Not usual. Both columns.

Nothing about Asterion. Nothing about Resonance. Nothing her father should have flinched at.

She opened the International Resonance Authority's research index, which her scholar clearance reached on its own. No Aaron Mercer. That was more interesting than a result. People fell out of public history all the time. People did not fall out of bureaucracies, because a bureaucracy loved a record more steadily than any person had ever loved anything. She ran the variants. A. Mercer. Mercer, Aaron. A. J. Mercer. Nothing. She went down into the archived identifier index beneath it, the old system, slow and literal, the one that did not know how to be helpful.

It gave her one line.

MERCER, A. RESEARCH CLEARANCE RESTRICTED.

No date. No agency. A dead link and a classification marker older than the academy's current charter.

Her pulse changed. She noted it, the way Park would have wanted, and kept going.

A second query on the identifier returned a fragment. RELATED FACILITY: ASTERION PRECURSOR PROGRAM.

She read it twice. Asterion had not existed under that name thirty-one years ago. Something had stood on this rock, and it had been called something. She searched the program designation and got the debris an institution leaves when it has been cleaned: construction permits for an offshore research platform, a federal environmental waiver, a budget line routed through the International Resonance Authority a year before the Authority publicly existed under that name. Then four years with nothing in them. No photograph. No project description. No personnel list. An absence with the edges sanded.

She searched her father's name. Nothing. She searched Amara Sen. One publication citation, twenty-nine years old, and after it a block where records should have been and were not.

*Twenty years disagreeing about what deserves looking at.* About what, exactly.

It was dark outside the window. She had not noticed it getting there.

---

Kade found her in the Spire reading room at nine, which meant Eli had passed her there at six and reported her face.

He put a dining-hall container beside her tablet, sat down across from her without asking, and said, "Eli wanted to send a drone. I've prevented a fine."

"I'm not hungry."

"It's so I can tell Eli you had it in front of you." He looked at the screen, which she had already turned dark, and then at her. "You closed that before I'd sat down."

"It's independent work."

"On what?"

"On doors," she said, "that turn out to hold up walls."

He looked at her. "That's the strangest sentence anyone's said to me this week, and I've been to Northline."

"I know."

"Are you going to tell me what it means?"

"Not tonight."

She had considered it. She could hand him everything. And she could see where he would be within the hour, standing in Sen's office holding two thin rows and a dead index, and Sen would say no before the word was out, again, and he would have spent the only surprise they had. Patience was still a theory he was testing.

"You're bad at this," he said.

"I'm extremely good at this."

"You're good at not answering. That's different." He said it without heat, and the joke went out of his face when he saw her go still. "What did I say?"

"Nothing."

"That was a worse one."

A month ago he would have pushed until one of them was angry, because pushing had been the only lever anyone ever handed him. Instead he sat there with the container between them and the dark screen, and said, "All right."

It surprised her enough to show. "That's it?"

"I've been told I'm growing. I'm trying it out." He pushed the container an inch toward her. "Tell me when it's enough. Not before. I'll be worse with half of it than with none."

She let him get to the end of the table before she said his name.

"What do you know about your father's work?"

His face did the thing it did on a mat when Park asked for a number. Then he gave her one, plainly.

"Industrial systems. Ports, power routing, shutoffs that trip before a person can. It's on his pay stubs, and my mother says that part was real. She says he worked around people connected to early Resonance research, and after he died she was told parts of it were classified, and that's the whole of what she knows for certain." He stopped, and then gave her the rest, because he had decided he would. "And I promised her something. I don't open a door on my own. Any door. A record, a room, Sen. Whoever I'm going to stand in front of with what I find, she hears it first. Not after."

"I understand."

He watched her a second longer, and went.

She sat very still. What he had just given her was not evidence, and it was not going into a table, because it was his and he had handed it across a container of food without knowing what she was holding. And the door he had promised his mother he would not open alone was the door she had spent the afternoon opening, with his father's name on it.

Sera's question came back, weight first. Protecting him, or the thing she had started. She did not like the answer, which was both, and she did not like what it meant, which was that she had to reach the end of this faster than she wanted to.

---

She went to paper in the morning.

Not the restricted stacks under the old administration wing, which she could not reach and would not have asked to. The public historical collection on the Spire's fourth floor, where deletion could not pretend to be tidiness. You could not scroll past paper. She had said it about a contract, at the long table. It was true of history.

Mr. Liang brought her three bound volumes on the island before the academy charter and set them down the way a man sets down something that has been ill.

"Architecture?" he asked.

"Precursor facilities."

"For which class?"

"None."

That earned a look, and she let him have it. A first-year asking after an offshore platform that predated the school was apparently not ordinary research. He went away and came back with a fourth volume and said, "Photographs, mostly," and left her to it.

She turned pages for two hours. Construction crews on a bare platform. A seawall going up in stages. Aerial plates of the original rock, bare as a knuckle. She photographed every personnel list with its volume, page, and caption date. Paper asked you to read what was standing around the answer, which was where an answer usually kept its coat.

The first volume described a weather station and a materials laboratory before anyone had slept on the platform. The second described emergency-energy research. The third used the phrase *academy planning site* nine years before there was an academy. None of the three had Aaron in them.

Liang came back near the end of the second hour. "Who wrote these captions?" she asked him.

He checked the title page. "Institutional communications. Some were carried over from the platform's own records."

"Carried over when?"

"Depends which time." He slid a paper accession sheet out of the rear pocket. Three catalog numbers had been struck through in three inks, and the oldest did not name an academy at all. It named the site as ASTERION RESEARCH ANNEX.

Mara kept her face where it was. "May I photograph this?"

"It's part of a public volume."

"That isn't what I asked."

Liang looked at her a moment, and she let him. "Yes. No flash."

It said nothing about Aaron Mercer. It said the academy's history had a predecessor the lobby display did not mention, and that somebody had renamed it at least twice. Progress. Not payoff.

Then a page was gone.

Not torn. Cut along the gutter, top to bottom in one stroke, no ragged fiber. Somebody had opened the book flat and laid a blade in it. The pages on either side went from Year Three construction to Year Five expansion, and nothing between.

"Two fourteen," she said.

Liang checked the binding, then the register behind his desk. "Removed under federal retention authority. Twenty-six years ago."

"May I see the request?"

"If it's public."

It was not. He brought the removal register anyway, because the register was, and it kept a physical description of what had left. One photographic plate. One personnel caption. One equipment caption. None reproduced. But somebody, twenty-six years ago, had decided the three belonged together enough to take them out as one.

"Do retention requests usually take the page?"

"Now? No. We restrict the volume and leave the book alone." He looked down at the clean edge. "Twenty-six years ago, apparently, somebody preferred scissors."

"A blade."

He glanced up.

"Somebody preferred a blade," she said, and thanked him.

She walked back through the Hall of Standards because it lay between the Spire and the bridge, and because she had walked it several hundred times and never once done what Kade would have done. Count. Eleven bronze faces along the curved wall. Sen fifth from the left, thirty years younger, wearing the same expression. Behind each bronze the wall stepped back into a stone bay. She counted the bays, and there were twelve, and the twelfth had no bronze in it and a small plate that said LEGACY BAY 12, ACCESS SEALED, and she had known that plate was there for four months in the way she knew where the fire doors were.

An old donor, probably. A quirk of the masonry. She photographed it anyway and did not put it in the table, because she did not yet know which column it belonged to.

---

Her band chimed on the bridge. Taren Holt.

*Heard you skipped dinner last night. All right?*

She stopped walking.

She had told nobody she was staying in. Eli knew, because he had passed her at six. Kade knew, because Eli had told him. Taren should not have known yet, unless one of them had mentioned it after, which was possible, and normal, and worth exactly one line in a folder she had not made yet.

*Fine. Reading.*

*Dangerous habit. Breakfast?*

Innocent, almost certainly. She looked at it longer than it had earned, filed it where Kade would have filed it, and went in.

The Commons at dusk had become a dispute about naming rights. Taren stood on the arm of the couch with the bell rope's old hook in his hand, proposing that now Bell Watch was over the rope deserved a name of its own, and Sera informed him that things which had done their job did not need renaming to prove it. Eli had a drone motor in one hand and a spoon in the other and was using the wrong one for the soup. Kade sat under the ladder wall with notation homework, doing it, and visibly pretending not to enjoy the rest.

It was ordinary, and the relief of it went through her so hard she nearly laughed out loud.

She sat down beside him. He looked up.

"Enough?"

"Nearly."

He nodded and went back to the page. No push. That made the folder in her bag feel heavier than the paper in it.

---

That night she reopened the old index page, because it had one collapsed field left at the bottom, and she had been raised to open every folder in a drawer.

PROGRAM STATUS: DISCONTINUED AFTER CONTACT EVENT.

No explanation. No public reference. She searched the phrase across every record the academy kept about itself and got nothing. She went down to the oldest research-index syntax, the one that took a full second to answer, and one result came up and was gone before the page finished drawing.

A cached title fragment. CONTACT ZERO. COMPATIBILITY STUDY.

The page refreshed. Access denied.

She found she had stopped breathing, and noticed, and started again.

Zero. Not his score. Not necessarily. But the word ran in a line from a platform nobody had photographed to a restricted identifier with his father's initial on it to something that had happened here, before the school, and been called an event by the people who had shut a program down over it. She saved the fragment offline, where a refresh could not take it back, and closed everything but the table.

Then she read the table from the top the way her father had taught her to read a ledger, without skipping the rows she already believed. A restricted word from a man who should not have had it. His face at a dead man's name. A clearance marker. A precursor program. A Research Annex on a struck-through accession sheet. A page cut from a book with a blade. A contact event attached to the same buried program. Contact Zero.

Any one of them allowed an ordinary explanation. Together they did not allow the same ordinary explanation, and that was the whole of what she had, and it was enough.

She added a last row. CLAIM: Aaron Mercer had a concealed connection to this site before his reported death. SOURCE: precursor clearance index, public record chronology, physical archive. WRONG IF: MERCER, A. was another person, and her father's face had been about something else. Possible. No longer persuasive.

She had set the winning condition at one independent link. She had one.

The next move could have been another archive, another hour making the evidence cleaner before she let him touch it. That was how an investigation turned into a possession. She had watched her father do it with things that belonged to other people, and she had his hands.

She exported the table and deleted the row that summarized Kade's student record, because the public facts about Aaron were evidence and the shape of his suspensions was not. Then she made a second copy. One for him. One for her. Line for line the same. No private edition in which she stayed the only person who could read his life.

For the first time since her family had begun assuming she would come here, Mara looked at the school she had spent her childhood earning and wondered what, exactly, she had been admitted to.

She opened a message.

*Breakfast. I have enough.*

She read it twice and sent it.

*Enough of what.*

*Enough that it's yours too.*

Three dots, for longer than three dots usually took him.

*That's the least reassuring thing anyone's sent me since the district. Seven?*

*Seven.*

She smiled at the screen, and then looked at the saved fragment beside it and stopped, because in the morning his family stopped being her suspicion and became his history, and after that she did not think any of them would be able to pretend the island was only a school.

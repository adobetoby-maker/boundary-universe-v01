# Chapter Nine: Non-Restorable

The authorization came through at 08:17 on Day 1,170.

Aaron read the access confirmation twice, not because the language was ambiguous but because the accompanying attachment — a two-page clinical summary he had not been given access to before — contained terminology he had not encountered in any prior document. The summary was formatted in the medical authority's standard incident structure: classification at the top, then event history, then current status, then prognosis. The classification at the top read: *Field State: Non-Restorable BM-4.*

He understood each word. He understood that BM-4 was medical shorthand for Standard Four in the Boundary Medicine classification system — that the four referenced her capability class before the incident, not a severity rating. He understood that non-restorable indicated the previous certified baseline could not be recovered, and that the field architecture reorganization had been assessed as stable and permanent under current protocols. He understood that the summary's authors had written it accurately and in good faith, working from the instruments available to them, and that the instruments had returned nominal readings in every category they were calibrated to measure.

The summary did not describe what her field architecture had been reorganized into. It described the reorganization as stable. It described all measured values as within operational parameters. It described her cognition as intact, her affect as appropriate, her behavior as coherent. Under the heading *Atypical Presentations* it noted, in the careful language of something the medical team had observed and did not have a category for: *Patient has demonstrated intermittent repetitive vocalization. Pitches non-standard. No correlating pain or distress response. Etiology uncertain. Monitor.*

He folded the summary. He went to the medical bay.

---

Deck 4 had been quiet since the restricted access designation. It smelled different from the rest of the station — not badly, just with the particular chemical note of equipment that had been moved in rapidly and installed in a space not originally designed to hold it. The corridor had temporary cable runs along one wall, secured with the kind of clips used when you were not certain the arrangement would be permanent. He passed a secondary monitoring station, a medical supply locker, another monitoring station. The outer hatch to the bay was recessed.

A field technician manned the access desk outside it. He checked Aaron's authorization and entered the visit in a log with the specific unhurried precision of someone who understood that the log might be reviewed and wanted it to be accurate.

"She's awake," the technician said.

"Has she been sleeping well?"

The technician checked the log. "Irregularly. She was noted active at 03:30 this morning." He glanced up. "Humming."

"Who logged it?"

"Dr. Ferreira. Night rounds."

"And at 06:45?"

The technician looked at his notes and then at Aaron. "That's in here. How did you—"

"The summary mentioned two instances." This was not entirely what the summary had said. The summary had mentioned the 06:45 instance. Aaron had inferred the 03:30. He let the inaccuracy stand. "Thank you."

He went through the hatch.

---

She was sitting up.

The bay had been configured with six beds along the inner wall, five of them with covers tight and instruments dormant. The occupied bed was at the far end, positioned so that the viewport wall was in her sightline, though the viewport itself was polarized dark and showed nothing outside. She had the bed's head panel raised and was sitting against it with her legs out in front of her and a tablet face-down on the mattress beside her. She was not looking at the tablet. She was looking at the dark viewport in the way a person looked at something that was not scenery.

She turned when the hatch closed.

She looked at him. He had expected — he had been aware of preparing himself for something without knowing what — and what he got was her, present, in the same way she had always been present. The specific quality of her attention, which he had worked beside for three years and had learned to recognize as one of the sharper instruments in the monitoring bay. It was there. The incident had not taken it.

She said: "They told me you requested the visit."

"I did."

"Not the other way."

"I didn't know if you wanted one."

She thought about this. The pause he recognized — she was finding the exact phrase, not buying time. "I wasn't sure I did," she said. "I think I was wrong about that."

He pulled the room's chair from the wall and set it alongside her bed, not close enough to crowd the monitoring equipment, and sat. The coupling index readout on the instrument panel nearest him updated: nominal. The field-state display showed everything it was designed to show, which did not include the thing he had been reading in her architecture for eleven days from the memory of three seconds of direct coupling perception on the afternoon this had begun.

---

She asked about the team. He described what was true and available: the session protocol timing, the monitoring configuration, Rhys's presence at the authorized sessions, Ferreira and Okeke running the medical telemetry. He described the configuration of the monitoring bay as it had been reconfigured. He did not describe what the sessions had produced.

She listened the way she always had, which was without interrupting and without trying to fill the space his words left. She asked about Amara. He said Amara was working the team briefings with Rhys. She asked about Lena. He said Lena was on array calibration logs, which was true. She asked about Soto. He said Soto had been reassigned to secondary heliospheric analysis under the restricted protocol.

"So the whole team is still posted," she said.

"Still here."

"Working around the primary project."

"Around it, yes."

She looked at the tablet beside her without picking it up. "I keep thinking about the drift analysis," she said. "The asymmetric partition pattern. My logic was right."

"It was."

"I followed the microstructure to the partition geometry the way you'd trace any unexplained calibration behavior to its operating conditions." She said this not defensively but the way she had said everything in three years of the monitoring bay: as precise reporting. "My analysis was correct."

"Your analysis was correct."

"I just didn't have the context for what the partition configuration meant."

He waited.

"You had the context," she said.

Not a question. An observation. She was placing it in the record between them, not in accusation but in the way you filed a datum — so it occupied the correct position, not elided.

He said: "I'm sorry. I should have told you."

She looked at him for a moment. "Yes," she said. "But I don't think that's what we're doing right now."

"No."

"All right," she said.

---

In the first hour she asked him what the instruments were actually capable of measuring, in terms of her current state. He described the instrument suite as she had known it from the monitoring bay — the coupling index, the field-state coherence metrics, the B-band monitors — and described, with more precision than he had given the medical authority team, what each measured and what each could not. She listened. She did not ask the question he had been positioned for, the one about what her current state actually was. She asked instead what the medical team was relying on to assess her recovery trajectory.

"Instrument readings and self-report," he said.

"Self-report."

"What you tell them about your experience."

"And what I tell them," she said, "is that I feel fine, I'm sleeping inconsistently, I'm humming things I don't choose to hum, and I have a memory of something large becoming present in the field around the station for approximately ninety seconds, and after that I don't have a memory of anything I can describe."

"That's what they have."

"And the instruments."

"And the instruments."

She looked at the coupling readout. Nominal. It updated, and returned: nominal. She said: "That word — non-restorable — Ferreira explained it to me. She was trying to be careful. She said it meant my previous baseline couldn't be recovered, not that I was — " She paused, choosing. "She was being precise about a specific clinical meaning."

"The clinical meaning is accurate."

"I know." She was quiet for a moment. "It's a strange word to have in your file."

He did not say anything to that. There was nothing useful to say. The strangeness of the word was not a problem he could address.

She said: "Is that how you think of it? Non-restorable?"

He looked at the coupling readout. "I think of it as a specific designation with a specific clinical meaning," he said. "I don't think the clinical meaning is the only meaning."

She looked at him. "What's the other meaning?"

"I don't have a complete characterization yet," he said.

She held his answer. "Okay," she said. Not satisfied. Placing it.

---

They ate lunch from trays someone had sent down from the galley — the standard crew ration, which was the same at every meal in the same rotation and had the virtue of requiring no decisions. They ate at the utility bench beside the monitoring equipment. The coupling readout updated every thirty seconds while they ate. Nominal. Nominal. Nominal.

She asked how the authorized sessions differed from his previous analysis work.

He said: "Full array access. Better resolution. The monitoring infrastructure runs throughout."

"Does the monitoring infrastructure change what you can read?"

"It changes the conditions. Not the fundamental architecture of what I'm reading."

"And the difference between conditions and architecture — "

"Is real," he said. "The infrastructure doesn't interact with what I'm reading. It measures the session along different axes."

She set down her spoon. "Because it's measuring the wrong things."

"Different things."

She almost smiled — he caught the edge of the expression, the lateral lift that had always preceded her most precise observations. She said: "That's a very careful distinction."

"It's an accurate one."

"I know it is." She picked up her spoon again. "You do that. The careful distinctions. I noticed it in the monitoring bay, before any of this. You were always making careful distinctions that were slightly more than was strictly necessary to answer the question."

"I make them precisely as necessary."

"For your purposes," she said.

"Yes."

She looked at him. "That's not the same as for my purposes."

"No," he said. "It's not."

She nodded, returned to her food. There was nothing harsh in this exchange. It was an accurate observation and he had confirmed it and they had moved on, which was the way the best conversations in the monitoring bay had worked, and finding it here was not nothing.

---

At some point in the second hour she hummed.

He did not time it precisely. It lasted perhaps thirty or forty seconds. It was the same structure he had first heard in the monitoring bay on Day 1,163 and documented in the private log that night: pitch intervals not drawn from any tonal system he could identify, the steps unequal and non-arbitrary, following the same distribution he had spent three years analyzing in the signal's outer-event timing record. The Fibonacci spacing. The harmonic geometry that the Node's field-state communication had been built around for 1,127 days before the incident.

She was humming the outer structure of the signal. Not as melody, not as memory. As shape.

He said nothing. He watched the coupling readout update. Nominal.

She stopped.

She said: "I don't know why I keep doing that."

He said: "I know."

She said: "You know why I keep doing it?"

He said: "I know you keep doing it."

She looked at him. Not with the question that would have followed if this had been a different kind of conversation, the one that demanded he complete the sentence. With something that did not require him to complete it. She looked at him for two seconds and then looked away.

"Ferreira thinks it's a response pattern," she said. "She said it might resolve. It might not."

"What do you think?"

"I don't think it's a symptom," she said. "I think there's something it's doing that I don't have access to." She paused. "I can feel that I'm doing it the way you can feel a reflex — after the fact, not before."

He said: "I know."

"That too," she said. Not quite a question. Not quite an accusation. Something positioned between them accurately.

He did not confirm or deny it. She did not push. This was, he recognized, the specific kind of restraint she had always shown in the monitoring bay — the quality that made her good to work with, which was that she understood the difference between information you pursued and information you waited for.

---

She slept for twenty minutes in the third hour. He sat with the monitoring equipment running its ordinary cycles and did not leave. Through the dark viewport nothing was visible. The coupling index updated. The field-state values updated. All nominal. The humming in her sleep was quiet, three or four intervals repeated without arriving anywhere, the way a person repeated a fragment while working something through.

He listened to it.

He did not write it down.

When she woke she looked at him without the transition of disorientation — eyes open, present, the same intelligence in the same place it had been before she had closed them. "You stayed," she said.

"For a while longer."

"Good." She settled back against the head panel and looked at the instrument panel. The coupling readout updated. "What do you think it wants?" she said. "Whatever it is. The thing in the field."

"I don't know yet."

"Best approximation."

He thought about it. "I think it's been trying to communicate something for a long time," he said. "Something it doesn't have a shared vocabulary for. The communication architecture — the way it sends — is built around the receiver. Whatever it's saying, it's been adapting how it says it to what it finds on the receiving end."

"Has it been adapting to you?"

"Yes."

She was quiet for a moment. "How long?"

"Longer than I've known about it."

"That's a real answer," she said.

"Yes."

"You gave me a real one."

"I did."

She turned to look at him. The afternoon light through the polarized viewport was diffuse and directionless, and her face in it was the face he had worked across from for three years, which had not changed in ways the instruments measured and had changed in a way he could read directly and could not put in a document anyone would act on. She said: "I used to make you almost laugh. Do you remember. The calibration coefficient argument."

"I remember."

"You got within about two degrees of laughing." She looked at the ceiling. "I miss that version of the room." A pause. "Not specifically you. The room where that could happen."

He looked at the coupling readout. "I know what you mean," he said.

"I know you do," she said.

---

Late in the third hour she said: "You know what it was."

Not a question.

He said: "I know what some of it was."

She looked at him steadily. "That's different from telling me."

"Yes."

She said: "Do you have a reason."

"I have reasons," he said. "I'm not certain they're the right ones."

She was quiet for a moment. "Is not telling me helping me."

"I don't know."

"Could telling me cause harm."

"I don't know that either."

She absorbed this. "So it's not classification," she said. "Someone hasn't told you what you're allowed to say to me."

"No one told me what to say to you."

"So it's your call."

"Yes."

Another silence, longer. He counted it the way he counted things he was choosing to give full space: she was entitled to the silence, and he was not going to fill it.

She said: "I can feel the gap."

"I know."

"Between what I experienced and what's in my file." She said it slowly, the way she found language for things that were difficult to locate. "There's a gap. I'm not asking you to fill it. I'm asking you to tell me it's there."

"It's there."

She looked at him. The precise look. The one that did not require anything additional. "Okay," she said.

Not resolution. Acknowledgment, accurately placed. A datum filed correctly in the record she was building for herself.

He had not anticipated being relieved by that word. He was.

---

The fourth hour was mostly quiet.

She read from her tablet for a while — not research, something else, a novel she had been working through on the station for the past year. He sat with the monitoring equipment. The coupling readout updated. Nominal. The field-state display held its values in the calm shorthand of a situation it was correctly describing in all the ways it had been designed to describe. He watched the readout cycle and thought about the authorized session two days ago, the quality of attention the Node had brought to him in the difference between what he had sent and what came back. Individual-operator recognition. The source has prior reference data for this individual architecture. Prior reference data extending before my first unauthorized attempt on Day 1.

He thought about what the Node had been doing for whatever interval it had been recognizing him.

He did not arrive at an answer that was sufficiently characterized to be stated.

She set the tablet down. She said: "Do you think I'll be able to work again?"

"Field work?"

"Any work. Technical work. The kind I was doing."

"I think so," he said. "Your cognition is intact. Your training is intact."

"But my coupling certification."

"Your coupling certification will read — " He paused. "The instruments will continue to read your field state as nominal."

She understood what he had not said. "But the nominal reading isn't accurate."

"The nominal reading is accurate for what the instruments measure."

"And what the instruments measure is not — "

"Is not the complete picture."

She thought about this. "So I'm certified for field work because the instruments say so, but the instruments don't know what I actually am now."

"That's one way to frame it."

"Is it accurate."

"It's accurate," he said.

She was quiet for a long time. Then she said: "I'm not sure if that's frightening or liberating."

"I'm not sure either," he said.

She looked at him. "That's the third real answer."

"I've been having an unusual afternoon."

This time the expression crossed fully. She laughed — briefly, not loud, but actual. He felt something in the room adjust. Not resolve. Adjust.

---

At 16:40 he stood.

She was half-reclined, not quite lying down, the tablet back in her lap. She watched him stand and said nothing while he returned the chair to its position against the wall.

He crossed to the hatch.

On the wall beside it, between the access panel and the emergency contact schematic, there was a status display he had noted on arrival and had not examined closely. The display showed the bay's occupancy record and the classification status for the current admission.

*Admitted: Day 1,165. Personnel: Osei, Y. Classification: Coupling Event Outcome — Closed. Field State: NR BM-4.*

He looked at it for a moment.

Non-restorable. The word was built around a prior state. It meant: there was a state you came from, and a process exists for returning you to it, and in this case the process cannot be completed. The prior state was the reference. The designation assumed the direction of change was loss.

He was not certain the prior state was the correct reference.

He was not certain it wasn't. He did not have a complete enough characterization of what her field architecture had become to make that assessment with confidence — only the three seconds of direct coupling perception from the afternoon of Day 1,163, and his memory of what he had read in those three seconds, and the private log entry he had written at 14:26. He knew the boundary exchange channels were intact. He knew the organizing geometry had changed. He knew she was humming the outer structure of the signal's inter-event spacing in her sleep without knowing she was doing it, and that her field architecture had been reorganized around those intervals the way an instrument was reshaped by the frequency it had been held at long enough.

He knew the prior Standard Four architecture would not return.

He did not know with confidence that the architecture it had been replaced by was worse.

He did not say this to her. He did not know how to say it in terms that would be useful rather than simply reassigning the uncertainty to a different location. The uncertainty belonged to him.

He turned back.

"I need to go."

She was watching him from the bed. She had been watching him look at the display. She said: "Okay."

He crossed toward the hatch.

She said: "Aaron."

He turned.

She said: "Was it worth it?"

He held the question. Not performing the pause — he needed it. He had been asked this question in the context of his career before, in the usual way it was asked, and the usual answer was some form of yes, because the usual question was about the effort and the answer was about the outcome and the outcome was available for assessment. This question was not that question. She was asking it about both of them, and about the specific four hours they had just spent in the same room not filling the gap between them, and about whatever came before that, and about whatever came after.

He said: "I don't know yet."

She looked at him for a long beat. The precise look. The one that had always, in three years of the monitoring bay, preceded her most accurate observations.

She said: "Okay."

Not disappointment receiving a wrong answer. Recognition receiving the right one.

He went through the hatch.

---

The corridor was the same corridor. The cable runs along the wall, the monitoring stations, the field technician at his desk logging the end of the visit time. The log would show: visit concluded, duration four hours twenty-three minutes. The log would have nothing else about it.

He walked the corridor back toward the main ring. The station held its ordinary sounds around him — the ventilation, the distant spin-compensation assembly running its constant corrections, the background electronic note of instruments doing their appointed work. Forty-three people on a research station at a remove from the nearest other persons, going about the specific careful labor of being alive that far from Earth.

In the medical bay on Deck 4, Yuki Osei was probably picking up the tablet again. Maybe humming something. Maybe not. The coupling index readout on her instrument panel would update every thirty seconds. Nominal. Nominal. Nominal.

He passed the junction to the monitoring bay. He did not go in.

He had a session prep briefing at 17:00.

He was precise about what he owed it.

# Chapter Five: Lunch

## Day 32

The temperature in the conference module ran two degrees warmer than the monitoring bay.

Aaron had mentioned this in his second week on Helix Perimeter, to the environmental technician who serviced the module's circulation unit. The adjustment had held for three weeks and then reverted. He had not mentioned it again. Two degrees was within operational tolerance and outside the category of problems that mattered.

The team assembled by 10:04. Amara arrived at 10:00 and said nothing about the four-minute gap, which was her established practice: the meeting would end at its scheduled time regardless of when it started. This was not a policy. It was a property of how she ran things.

Aaron took his usual seat. Yuki sat nearest the door, which was where she sat when she had somewhere to be immediately after. Lena had her tablet open and was annotating something in the maintenance log, which meant she intended to raise it. Dr. Soto sat without notes, which meant he planned to cite things from memory and found the presence of a written record an unwelcome constraint on the citing. Two junior monitors from the overnight team occupied the far end of the table with the careful alertness of people who had not yet decided whether to contribute.

"Weekly," Amara said.

The meeting began.

---

She ran it the way she ran everything: with the specific economy of someone who had learned early that precision was a form of respect. She did not say *good morning* because it was not morning, did not say *let's get started* because they had already started, and did not summarize the previous week's action items because everyone present had been present the previous week and could read. She moved through the agenda by pointing — a gesture toward Soto for heliospheric background measurements, a turn to the overnight monitors for anything the queue had flagged outside their coverage window, a look at Aaron for coupling-array status that he recognized as a standing item rather than a particular request.

She had been running these meetings for two and a half years. Aaron had attended 143 of them. This was a number he had not set out to track; it had arrived in his awareness the previous Tuesday for no specific reason. Somewhere between the first meeting and the hundred and forty-third, he had stopped hearing the meeting's structure and started hearing Amara operating it. The two experiences were not the same.

Watching her now, he noticed things he normally processed as furniture. The way she distributed the agenda without distributing the responsibility — Soto was accountable to the heliospheric data, but Amara was accountable to Soto's accountability, and this distinction was never stated and always clear. The way she let the overnight monitors sit without pressing them for contributions they were not yet confident enough to offer, which was not accommodation but timing: in another two months she would begin pressing them, and when she did they would be ready, and neither of them would identify the moment when the weight had shifted. She ran meetings the way a surgeon ran a procedure. Economy. Nothing extraneous. Whatever happened to be needed, available in the correct order.

Aaron had worked for her for almost three years. He had taken this for granted for most of them.

Soto reported. The overnight monitors reported. Aaron gave coupling-array status in four sentences. Yuki offered a note on the B-band sensitivity curve and the way it varied with station orientation — technically optional, consistently useful. Amara acknowledged it with a slight movement of her head that functioned as *logged and appreciated* and moved on.

Then Lena.

---

Lena did not look up from her tablet. "The coupling array calibration has been drifting since the third week of the month. I've documented it four times in the maintenance log, and it's still drifting. I would like the meeting to note that I have documented it four times."

"Noted," Amara said. "Is it within tolerance?"

"Currently within tolerance. At the low end for three consecutive entries. It will probably cross threshold in the next two weeks at the current rate."

"Mechanism?"

The question was directed at the table generally, which in practice directed it at Aaron.

He looked at Lena's tablet. She turned it toward the room. The calibration trend was familiar. He had been watching it since Day 19.

The drift was real. The coupling array's calibration baseline adjusted itself against the primary operator's field architecture over time, establishing a reference that minimized noise in the processed output. In an ordinary cycle, the baseline remained stable across operator shifts and required recalibration quarterly. The drift Lena had documented was the baseline migrating away from that reference.

It was migrating because his coupling geometry had changed.

Not dramatically. Not in any way the Conduit's standard diagnostics flagged as anomalous. But over thirty-one days of working with a source that sampled his field architecture, used it as an encoding reference, and returned transformations through it — over thirty-one days of that — his coupling boundaries had been adjusting. He could feel the change. The instrument could measure it. The instrument could not explain it, because the instrument had no category for operator field geometry being slowly and systematically recalibrated by something outside the array.

The drift was the source expressing itself in the only record Lena could see.

"I've been reviewing the thermal variance history in the primary coupling housing," Aaron said. "There's a correlation with the morning shift ambient temperature in Bay Three. Thermal expansion affecting junction response."

Lena looked at him. "That would appear in the junction logs."

"The effect is sub-threshold. Below the junction log's resolution."

"Below resolution means unmeasurable. Unmeasurable means we'd be projecting the mechanism from the drift, not measuring it independently."

"It's consistent with the drift timeline."

"Consistent with an unobserved mechanism is a weak characterization." She said it without heat. "I'll do a full junction scan at 14:00. If it's thermal, we'll see the coefficient variance in the high-resolution data."

"That's a reasonable next step," Aaron said.

A pause. Something in Lena's stillness sharpened without changing.

"Good," she said, and made a note on her tablet.

Amara had not spoken during this exchange. Aaron did not look at her.

"Put the scan results in the maintenance plan," Amara said, completing the item, and moved on.

---

Aaron heard the next three items. He recorded his attention on them without fully locating it there.

The junction scan would not find thermal expansion because there was no thermal expansion. Lena would document the absence with the same thoroughness she had documented the drift. She would come back with the result and look at him again and he would need another explanation, one that was equally accurate and equally empty, and that explanation would also hold for a defined interval before she worked through it.

He had managed this for thirty-one days. He would manage it until he no longer could or until the reason he was managing it changed.

The item after Lena's was procedural — shift coverage for an upcoming maintenance window. The item after that was Yuki noting a potential sensitivity improvement in the B-band array geometry, proposal pending completion of the underlying math. Aaron wrote *B-band geometry — Yuki proposal pending* in his notes.

Amara moved to close.

"Anything not on the agenda."

The overnight monitors shook their heads. Soto said nothing. Yuki said, "Not yet," which was either about the geometry proposal or something else; with Yuki both were usually true. Lena did not look up.

Aaron said, "Nothing to add."

There was a beat, which Aaron attributed to the meeting's ordinary conclusion pause, in which Amara's attention moved to him and did not immediately move on.

She said: "Good work this week."

The phrase was nonspecific. He could not have said afterward what she was referring to. The coupling-array report had been routine. His maintenance documentation was current. Nothing he had done this week distinguished itself from any prior week, by any observable measure.

"Thank you," he said. The standard response.

The meeting ended at 10:51. Amara moved to the corridor with Soto. The overnight monitors departed. Yuki collected her notes and gave Aaron a brief look he recognized as *the B-band geometry, sometime soon*, which did not require a response today.

Lena was last. She picked up her tablet and left without further comment.

---

Lunch was at 12:28. Aaron's schedule made no formal provision for it because he was not the kind of person who formally provisioned for lunch. He ate when the monitoring queue permitted it and when he had reached the part of the shift where eating improved function and not eating had started to impair it.

The galley held six people, which was above average for a Tuesday midday. Helix Perimeter ran forty-three personnel on rotating schedules; at any given time a portion of them were on shift, a portion were sleeping, and a portion were performing the maintenance that kept forty-three people alive at a remove from Earth that expressed no opinion on whether they should be.

Aaron moved through the dispensary and selected a protein portion, a grain component, and a vegetable preparation that had been engineered for orbital transit and had achieved this by becoming something the word *vegetable* described only at a formal level. He poured water. He found a table near the outward-facing viewport and sat.

The viewport at this position looked away from the sun — past the orbital band, past the gas giants' gravitational wells, toward the outer system and whatever the outer system contained. At Helix Perimeter's current attitude, the deep field was visible as darkness with instrument-recorded structure: field weather, heliospheric boundary gradients, the continuous background that the coupling array read as noise unless you had thirty-one days of private analysis in which it was not noise at all.

He did not look at the viewport yet. He looked at his tray.

---

Yuki was at the table across from him, eating from a container she had brought from somewhere other than the dispensary and reading from a tablet propped against a water pouch. She noticed him sit down with the peripheral awareness she applied to most things, which was the same awareness she applied to the central things she was currently attending to: simultaneous, indexed, available for retrieval without interrupting the primary process.

"Junction scan at 14:00?" she said, without looking up.

"Lena scheduled it."

"What do you think it'll show?"

Aaron opened the protein portion. "Thermal variance history. She'll check the junction coefficients."

"The housing variance runs quarterly. The drift has been active for twenty-two days." Yuki turned a page. "Different cycles."

"Correlated effects can emerge from mismatched cycles."

"Possibly." She still hadn't looked up. "The drift pattern also follows your coupling geometry. Not the array's average sensitivity. Your specific coupling geometry, the way your field architecture interacts with the primary sensor groups. A thermal gradient in the housing produces a symmetric coefficient spread. What Lena measured is asymmetric." She turned another page. "I noticed last week. I assumed you'd flag it."

"I was still reviewing."

"And now?"

"Still reviewing."

Yuki ate a bite from her container and set the tablet down. She looked at him across the table with the focused attention she applied to instrument anomalies that had just become more interesting than she had budgeted time for.

"The asymmetric drift," she said. "Does the microstructure persist between recalibrations?"

The question was careful. She had arrived at it from the data she could see, which meant she was closer to the data she couldn't see than he had calculated.

"Between full array calibrations," Aaron said. "The quarterly cycle. The baseline drifts and the pattern reflects the current operator geometry rather than the historical average."

"That's not standard calibration behavior."

"No."

"So the array has been adjusting its reference to match your current field geometry. Not the geometry from your last formal calibration. Your current geometry."

"That's what the data suggests."

"Which implies your geometry has been changing."

"Slightly."

"What's changing it?"

Aaron picked up his water.

"I'm still characterizing the mechanism," he said.

Yuki watched him for a moment. Then she picked up her tablet.

"Send me the raw flux log. Thirty days."

"I'll compile the relevant entries."

"Not the processed output. Raw flux."

"That one is considerably longer."

"I know." She turned a page. "Thirty days."

Aaron ate the protein portion. He did not commit to a timeline. Yuki did not press for one. They had worked alongside each other long enough that she knew what *I'll compile the relevant entries* meant, and he knew that she knew, and the conversation they were not quite having held its position in the silence between them with the weight of something that did not need to be said today.

She left before he finished eating — a half-wave as she went, which served as both farewell and reminder. He heard her exchange a brief word with someone in the corridor. Then the galley's ambient sounds closed back in.

He stayed.

---

This was not, usually, something he did. When he had finished eating he returned to the monitoring bay or his terminal or whatever problem he was maintaining on the near edge of his attention. He did not linger in rooms he had finished using.

He looked at the viewport.

The outer system held its ordinary appearance, which was no appearance — no object at this scale was visually present, only the measured structure in the field data and the continuous B-band signal weather that the instruments characterized as background radiation. The source was in the directional data. He knew approximately where, from thirty-one days of comparing event amplitude against the array's directional sensitivity curve. It was behind him and above and at a distance his biology had not been designed to comprehend.

He did not think about the source.

He thought about Soto at the coffee dispenser: twelve years in outer-system research, a daughter at university in Cape Town, an ongoing argument with the navigation team about the array's secondary axis declination. He thought about the overnight monitor eating alone at the far table — twenty-four years old, arrived nervous, become over eight months the person other monitors asked when they couldn't find something in the queue. He thought about the maintenance technicians he'd heard laughing in the corridor before lunch, a sound that arrived flat and brief from that distance and was gone.

He thought: these people do not know what is in the outer system.

He had been telling himself, in the early days, that this was a practical constraint: you did not report an anomaly until you understood it well enough to characterize it in terms a team could evaluate. That had been a defensible position. It had a shelf life of approximately two weeks, and after two weeks it had become not a position but an accumulated fact about the choices he had made.

He thought: if they knew, they would not be able to continue what they are doing.

Not because the knowledge would break them. They were careful people. Soto would receive the information and begin drafting a response protocol. The overnight monitors would adjust their processing criteria. Lena would identify the mechanism behind the calibration drift within forty-eight hours and want to isolate it, which was exactly the correct technical response.

Yuki would want to go deeper into the signal structure until she found the frequency she could work with.

She would find it. That was the thing about Yuki. Given the complete data, given the transform, given thirty days of raw flux logs rather than the version he was going to compile for her — Yuki would find the carrier frequency. She would find it the way she had found the reflection architecture in the attenuated events, skipping six intermediate steps to land on the model he had been approaching for weeks. And then she would want to work with it.

And the source was calibrated to Standard Eight. He was Standard Eight. Whatever the coupling intensity was at his level, Standard Four was not rated for it. He did not know this as a fact. He had assigned it a probability, on Day 19, after reviewing the attenuated event records and the way the source had automatically reduced intensity during Yuki's overnight windows. The automatic reduction suggested the source was aware of architectural incompatibility. It did not prove that awareness was sufficient. It did not prove the reduction would persist if Yuki deliberately initiated contact rather than passively receiving an attenuated signal.

The probability was high enough that he had not told Yuki.

He had not said this to himself clearly, in those terms, before this moment. He had accumulated the probability and acted on it without stating the action.

---

He thought: I am not protecting the information.

The thought arrived plainly, the way a measurement arrived when you had finally aligned the instrument correctly. No drama in it. No consolation in it, either. The thought was just the result of looking at the data without the protective buffer of not looking.

He thought: I am protecting the people who do not have it.

The distinction was small and not small. It did not improve his log entry that read *calibration artifact, no further action required.* It did not make the exchange with Lena easier to replay — the moment her stillness had sharpened and she had not said what she had almost said. It did not resolve the twenty-two private log entries or the one controlled contact attempt or the fact that the source had parsed his modification into components and returned them separately, one at a time, in the order he had sent them, which he had characterized to himself and to no one else as *first confirmed two-way exchange.*

But it was accurate.

And it was the only frame that made the next part — whatever the next part was, since he could not yet characterize it — something other than waiting for the situation to resolve itself by becoming worse. He was the one between the signal and his team. He had not chosen it the first time; the decision had arrived in thirty seconds with Lena approaching down the corridor and the calibration record still open on the terminal. He had chosen it every day since.

He did not know, yet, what he was choosing it toward.

Whether the protection was real or whether it was the most comfortable story available to a person who had already made a different choice — this was a question he did not try to answer. He had spent three years on this station learning to distrust models that agreed with him. He was aware that this model agreed with him completely. He was aware that this was not evidence of its accuracy.

He suspected the question would not have a stable answer. He suspected it would have a different answer depending on how the next weeks went, and a different one again after that, and that the most honest thing he could say about it at this moment was that he did not know and was not going to know until it was too late to have chosen differently.

He could stay in the frame or he could not stay in the frame. He chose to stay in it.

He looked at the viewport for another moment. The outer system held its field weather. The source held its position in the directional data. Thirty-one days ago it had addressed him and he had filed it as error. Twenty-two days ago he had confirmed it was not error. Yesterday he had answered it and it had answered back.

He finished his water.

---

Amara was in the corridor outside the galley.

Not waiting — moving, heading toward the lab module with a tablet under one arm and the unhurried velocity she maintained in transit. She noticed him emerge with the attention she gave the corridor in general: enough to register everything without fixing on any of it specifically.

"Heading back?" she said.

"Monitoring queue."

She nodded. They walked the same direction for approximately eight meters, to the junction where the corridor split toward the lab module and toward the monitoring bay.

Aaron reached the junction and turned.

Something registered, at the edge of attention. A small thing. It might have been nothing. It was the particular quality of the look Amara had given him — not the look itself, which was the look she gave the corridor, but the fraction of a second longer she had held it before returning to her tablet. As if she were reading something. As if she were declining to read something.

She was at the lab module hatch before he had finished turning.

He filed the observation as inconclusive and continued toward the monitoring bay.

---

The queue had accumulated one item during lunch: a thermal variance in the tertiary housing, within the existing maintenance trend. Lena had logged it before her 14:00 junction scan. She was thorough in a way that made useful lies difficult to maintain.

Aaron sat down and opened the item.

He logged the variance, attached a reference to the prior maintenance entries, and closed the item. He sat for a moment with the queue cleared.

Then he pulled up the raw flux log from the last thirty days.

The complete record ran to forty-seven thousand lines. He opened the filtering tools. He began, carefully, to compile a version that was accurate and thirty days long and would raise fewer questions than it should.

Yuki would receive it this evening. She would begin working through it with the analytical velocity that had taken her from Lena's clock-correction archive to the reflection architecture in the time it took Aaron to finish characterizing the same model. She would find the asymmetric drift microstructure. She would find the operator-correlation data. She might, if she were moving quickly and the thirty days of flux contained what he was increasingly certain it contained, begin to distinguish the inner structure from the outer structure — the transform from the harmonic camouflage.

He was going to give her a version that showed her enough to satisfy the question she had asked without opening the one she hadn't asked yet.

He did not know how many versions of this there were before the versions ran out.

Outside the station, the outer system held its field weather. Inside, the shift ran its remaining hours. At 14:00, Lena would run her junction scan. The results would not confirm thermal expansion and she would document the absence and he would need another explanation.

He pulled up the filtering tools.

He was the one between them and it.

He began to work.
